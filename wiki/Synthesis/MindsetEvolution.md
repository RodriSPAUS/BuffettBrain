## What this page traces

How Buffett's thinking moves across the corpus, 1977–2024, and — more importantly —
what does *not* move. The technique changes almost beyond recognition; the
underlying discipline is identical in the first letter and the last. This page
separates the two. For the narrower story of how the moat concept itself matured,
see [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]]; this page is the wider mental arc that contains it.

*Boundary (updated): the corpus now runs 1957–2024. The Buffett Partnership letters
[[Sources/1957ltr]]–[[Sources/1970ltr]] are first-hand sources for the entire
partnership era — an earlier version of this page treated that era as general
knowledge; it no longer is. `Raw/1967ltr.md` duplicates 1966 (a source-archive
extraction error, not a missing year; the real 1967 results are in
[[Sources/1968ltr]]). The partnership dissolved in 1969–70, and 1971–1976 remain a
genuine gap — no letters for those years exist in `Raw/` at all.*

## The change: from cheap to wonderful

The largest shift is in *what* Buffett buys. He begins as a quantitative Graham
disciple — buy anything statistically cheap against its assets — and the partnership
letters show precisely what that looked like. The 1961 letter is where the method
ceases to be implicit and becomes a written system: three categories — generally
undervalued "generals", event-driven "work-outs" on a timetable, and "control
situations" [[Sources/1961ltr]] — with a portfolio split between them, the only
borrowing done against work-outs (capped at 25% of net worth), and the year-end
valuation of a controlled asset defined as what a reasonably short sale would bring,
because new and old partners transact at that same price. [[Sources/1961ltr]]
[[Cases/DempsterMill]] is the mature form of the third category: a general stock
bought in 1956 that became majority control in August 1961 and its best year in
1962 was converting inventory to cash at close to 100 cents on the dollar
[[Sources/1962ltr]] — where the slightly earlier [[Cases/SanbornMap]] showed the
market pricing a company's securities portfolio below its own value and handing
over the operating business for nothing [[Sources/1960ltr]].
Buying whole businesses, not just fractions of them, becomes the trend as the
partnership grows — stated as an expectation before it happens:

> "If there is any trend as our assets grow, I would expect it to be toward controls which heretofore have been our smallest category." [[Sources/1964ltr]]

It happens twice more before the partnership dissolves: [[Cases/BerkshireHathaway]]
(bought as a cheap textile stock in 1962, control assumed 1965) and
[[Cases/HochschildKohn]] (the first whole business bought by direct negotiation,
1966). Both are graded honestly rather than retrospectively improved. Berkshire's
own textile operation — the reason the whole position existed — is conceded, by
the partnership's last year, to be a mediocre business kept for its people rather
than its economics:

> "The textile operation presently employs about $16 per share in capital and, while I think it has made some progress relative to the textile industry generally, cannot be judged a satisfactory business." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

Hochschild, Kohn is the sharper case: bought cheap, run well, sold three years
later for close to what was paid — precisely the "cigar butt" pattern Buffett
later names and disowns. He then abandons that method, under
[[People/CharlieMunger]]'s pressure, for something close to the opposite. He dates
the surrender explicitly in 1989, using [[Cases/HochschildKohn]] itself as the
example:

> "So-o-o - three years later I was lucky to sell the business for about what I had paid. After ending our corporate marriage to Hochschild Kohn, I had memories like those of the husband in the country song, 'My Wife Ran Away With My Best Friend and I Still Miss Him a Lot.'" [[Raw/1989ltr.md]]

> "I call this the "cigar butt" approach to investing. A cigar butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may not offer much of a smoke, but the "bargain purchase" will make that puff all profit. Unless you are a liquidator, that kind of approach to buying businesses is foolish." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

This is not a case of the corpus supplying a plausible-sounding example: the
1989 letter is naming, by company, a deal this wiki can trace start to finish
seventeen years earlier — the clearest instance anywhere in the corpus of an
early formulation and its own mature critique sharing a single, named subject.

Twenty-five years later he compresses the whole reversal into one line:

> "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

The enabling idea is the [[Concepts/Moat]], which takes a decade to reach a testable
form (a metaphor in 1986, the three-condition franchise test in 1991), and the
paired lesson from his own failures that good managers cannot rescue bad economics
— [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

## The change: from reported returns to what an owner keeps

Early letters speak the language of return on equity. Buffett replaces it with more
honest measures: [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]] — what a dueño can actually withdraw —
introduced in 1981 alongside the image of inflation as a parasite on capital:

> "inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism." [[Sources/1981ltr]]

See's Candies teaches him [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]] (1983); and by 2007 the
mature recognition of a moat's *limit* — it earns a lot on little capital but gives
you nowhere to put the proceeds:

> "There's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it. Indeed, it's often a mistake to do so: Truly great businesses, earning huge returns on tangible assets, can't for any extended period reinvest a large portion of their earnings internally at high rates of return." [[Sources/2007ltr]]

## The change: size forced a different game

The last quarter-century's evolution is imposed by success. In 1996 he names "The
Inevitables" and already warns that scale drags on returns:

> "Companies such as Coca-Cola and Gillette might well be labeled "The Inevitables."" [[Sources/1996ltr]]

From there the emphasis moves to running an enormous capital base: insurance
[[Concepts/Float]] becomes the engine (GEICO owned outright, [[People/AjitJain]]'s
super-cat book); share repurchases displace acquisitions as the main use of capital
after 2011; and succession is settled in the open — operations to [[People/GregAbel]],
insurance to Ajit in 2018, and the handover stated plainly in the final letter:

> "At 94, it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me as CEO and will be writing the annual letters." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

The instinct to arrange for what happens without him is not a late-career
development — it is present at the partnership's own end. Retiring in 1969,
Buffett names [[People/BillRuane]] as the manager for partners who need one,
in terms that anticipate the Greg Abel handoff by fifty-five years: not a
performance guarantee, but a character judgment, made public, at personal
cost if it goes wrong.

*General knowledge, not developed in the corpus: the Japanese trading-house
positions from 2020 onward, and his standing advice that the ordinary investor use a
low-cost index fund (which the 1996 letter does state).*

## The arc in one sentence

Buffett went from **hunting statistical bargains** (Graham) to **paying a fair price
for inevitable businesses** (Munger) to **allocating a mountain of float and
arranging his own succession** — but in all three eras he does the same thing: buys
below value, lets Mr. Market serve rather than instruct him, and tells his partners
exactly how many strokes he took on each hole. What he buys changed completely; how
he thinks never did.
