## What this page traces

Fidelity in these letters runs in two directions: what Buffett owes shareholders,
and what he requires of the managers he hires. This page follows the first, which
is the older and more explicit of the two, and shows where the second attaches to
it.

The striking feature is how little the standard moves. Unlike
[[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]], where the underlying idea is genuinely revised over
forty years, fidelity is stated early and then repeated, tested and handed on.

## The timeline

| Year | What the letters establish | Source |
|------|---------------------------|--------|
| **1979** | The reporting standard is to be held constant year to year: "the general scope, balance, and level of candor should be similar" | [[Sources/1979ltr]] |
| **1983** | Candour stated as a rule with a test: report "the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed" | [[Sources/1983ltr]] |
| **1983** | Its cost accepted in advance — Berkshire will keep a weak business rather than play "gin rummy managerial behavior" | [[Sources/1983ltr]] |
| **1989** | Candour applied to Buffett's own record: "the cost of this thumb-sucking has been huge" | [[Sources/1989ltr]] |
| **2024** | Fidelity failures in hired managers weighted above financial loss — "a pain that can approach that of a failed marriage" | [[Sources/2024ltr]] |
| **2024** | The standard passed to a successor, with the reason given: "if you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney" | [[Sources/2024ltr]] |

## The rule, as first written

> "We will be candid in our reporting to you, emphasizing the pluses and minuses
> important in appraising business value. Our guideline is to tell you the business
> facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed."
> [[Sources/1983ltr]]

This is stated as one of the Owner-Related Business Principles, alongside the
statement that shareholders are owner-partners — see
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]]. The two are the same commitment seen from
different sides.

## The test it has to pass

A reporting standard is only worth what it costs, and the letters put that cost
on the page. In 1989 the candour is aimed at Buffett's own record:

> "For Berkshire's shareholders, myself included, the cost of this thumb-sucking
> has been huge." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

And in 2024 it is still aimed there, now with the categories of error named:

> "Mistakes – Yes, We Make Them at Berkshire Sometimes I've made mistakes in
> assessing the future economics of a business I've purchased for Berkshire – each
> a case of capital allocation gone wrong." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

## Where it turns outward

The word does double duty. Applied to hired managers, its failure is measured
against something other than money:

> "At other times, I've made mistakes when assessing the abilities or fidelity of
> the managers Berkshire is hiring." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

> "The fidelity disappointments can hurt beyond their financial impact, a pain
> that can approach that of a failed marriage." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

The positive case is [[Cases/ForestRiver]]: a founder who proposed his own salary
at $100,000 because he "wouldn't want to make more than my boss", and who was
still running the business nineteen years later.

## The handover

> "Greg shares the Berkshire creed that a "report" is what a Berkshire CEO
> annually owes to owners. And he also understands that if you start fooling your
> shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as
> well." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

The justification given is self-protective rather than moral, and that is the
most durable thing on this page: candour is described as a defence against
self-deception, which is why it survives a change of author.
