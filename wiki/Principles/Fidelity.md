## The commitment

Fidelity in these letters is not a sentiment. It is a reporting standard, stated
as a rule with a test attached:

> "We will be candid in our reporting to you, emphasizing the pluses and minuses
> important in appraising business value. Our guideline is to tell you the business
> facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed."
> [[Sources/1983ltr]]

> "However, we will also report to you the earnings of each major business we
> control, numbers we consider of great importance." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

The standard is held constant across the years:

> "But the general scope, balance, and level of candor should be similar."
> [[Sources/1979ltr]]

And it is the trait Buffett requires of his successor:

> "Greg shares the Berkshire creed that a "report" is what a Berkshire CEO
> annually owes to owners. And he also understands that if you start fooling your
> shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as
> well." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

That second sentence is the actual argument: candour is a defence against
self-deception before it is a courtesy to owners.

## Its cost is accepted in advance

Fidelity is expensive, and the 1983 letter says so in the same breath — Berkshire
will keep a bad business rather than dress up its results:

> "Nevertheless, gin rummy managerial behavior (discard your least promising
> business at each turn) is not our style. We would rather have our overall results
> penalized a bit than engage in it." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

## On the other side of the relationship

The same word covers what Berkshire looks for in managers, and its failure is
weighted more heavily than financial loss:

> "At other times, I've made mistakes when assessing the abilities or fidelity of
> the managers Berkshire is hiring." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

> "The fidelity disappointments can hurt beyond their financial impact, a pain
> that can approach that of a failed marriage." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Cases/ForestRiver]], where a founder
proposed his own pay at $100,000 because he "wouldn't want to make more than my
boss".
