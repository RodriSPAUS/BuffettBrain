## Purpose

A checklist assembled from the actual, repeated tests Buffett states in the
letters — not a generic quality framework applied to them. Every row below
traces to a specific letter and, where the wording recurs verbatim across
years, the letter where it was first stated.

## 1. The four-part stock-selection test (1977)

The earliest and most durable formulation, restated with only one word changed
fifteen years later (see [[Sources/1992ltr]]):

> "We want the business to be (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable
> long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4)
> available at a very attractive price." [[Sources/1977ltr]]

## 2. The six-point acquisition criteria (1982, repeated unchanged through 1989)

Buffett's standing test for a control purchase, printed with only the minimum
dollar figure updated across decades:

> "(1) large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings), (2)
> demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little
> interest to us, nor are "turn-around" situations), (3) businesses earning
> good returns on equity while employing little or no debt. (4) management in
> place (we can't supply it), (5) simple businesses (if there's lots of
> technology, we won't understand it), (6) an offering price" [[Sources/1986ltr]]

The list first appears in [[Sources/1982ltr]] and is repeated word-for-word in
[[Sources/1989ltr]] seven years later — evidence the criteria are a fixed
filter, not a description adjusted after the fact to fit whatever Berkshire
happened to buy.

## 3. The franchise test (1991)

The clearest observable proof of a durable competitive advantage, and the
anchor for [[Concepts/Moat]]:

> "The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's
> ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to
> earn high rates of return on capital." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

## 4. The capital-appetite test: best business vs. worst business (1992)

A business can pass every other test above and still be a poor one to own, if
its returns require ever-larger reinvestment to sustain:

> "Leaving the question of price aside, the best business to own is one that
> over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at
> very high rates of return. The worst business to own is one that must, or
> will, do the opposite - that is, consistently employ ever-greater amounts of
> capital at very low rates of return." [[Sources/1992ltr]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]

## 5. The five risk factors (1993)

Buffett's substitute for beta — an explicit checklist for what actually
determines whether an investment is likely to preserve purchasing power:

1. "The certainty with which the long-term economic characteristics of the
   business can be evaluated"
2. "The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both as to its
   ability to realize the full potential of the business and to wisely employ
   its cash flows"
3. "The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the
   rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself"
4. "The purchase price of the business"
5. "The levels of taxation and inflation that will be experienced"

[[Sources/1993ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

## 6. The happy-zone discipline (1994)

Passing every criterion above is necessary but not sufficient — the last
filter is size, applied with a baseball analogy borrowed from Ted Williams:

> "My argument is, to be a good hitter, you've got to get a good ball to hit.
> It's the first rule in the book. If I have to bite at stuff that is out of
> my happy zone, I'm not a .344 hitter. I might only be a .250 hitter."
> [[Sources/1994ltr]] [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]]

## How to use this checklist

Apply the sections in order. A business that fails section 1 (not
understandable, no favorable long-term prospects, untrustworthy management, or
priced with no margin of safety) is rejected before the acquisition-specific
criteria in section 2 are even relevant. A business that passes 1-3 but fails 4
— the capital-appetite test — is the trap the letters warn about most often:
a fine business that nonetheless destroys value by requiring constant
reinvestment at mediocre returns. Section 5 restates the whole exercise as risk
assessment rather than opportunity assessment, and section 6 is the final
filter of size and conviction, not business quality.
