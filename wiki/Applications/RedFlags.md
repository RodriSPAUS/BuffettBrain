## Purpose

A list of warning signs Buffett names explicitly in the letters as reasons to
reject a business, a manager, or a proposed deal — each traced to the specific
language he uses, not a generic risk framework applied after the fact.

## 1. Good headline returns hiding a bad marginal one

The single most useful red flag in the letters: a business whose *overall*
return on equity looks fine while the *incremental* capital it reinvests earns
far less.

> "Many corporations that consistently show good returns both on equity and on
> overall incremental capital have, indeed, employed a large portion of their
> retained earnings on an economically unattractive, even disastrous, basis."
> [[Sources/1984ltr]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]

## 2. Capital spending proposed as a rescue

> "And we react with great caution to suggestions that our poor businesses can be
> restored to satisfactory profitability by major capital expenditures."
> [[Sources/1983ltr]]

## 3. The institutional imperative

Buffett's own name for the force that overrides otherwise rational management,
stated as four specific, checkable mechanisms:

> "(1) As if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion, an institution will
> resist any change in its current direction; (2) Just as work expands to fill
> available time, corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up
> available funds; (3) Any business craving of the leader, however foolish, will
> be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies prepared
> by his troops; and (4) The behavior of peer companies, whether they are
> expanding, acquiring, setting executive compensation or whatever, will be
> mindlessly imitated." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

Any one of the four, observed in a target company or a Berkshire subsidiary
alike, is grounds for scrutiny — Buffett states the diagnosis is structural,
not a matter of the individual manager's honesty or intelligence.

## 4. EPS or book-value accretion used as the test for a merger

The wrong question to ask about an acquisition, and the analogy Buffett uses
to expose it:

> "In contemplating business mergers and acquisitions, many managers tend to
> focus on whether the transaction is immediately dilutive or anti-dilutive to
> earnings per share (or, at financial institutions, to per-share book value).
> An emphasis of this sort carries great dangers." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

> "Imagine that a 25-year-old first-year MBA student is considering merging his
> future economic interests with those of a 25-year-old day laborer. The MBA
> student, a non-earner, would find that a "share-for-share" merger of his
> equity interest in himself with that of the day laborer would enhance his
> near-term earnings (in a big way!). But what could be sillier for the
> student than a deal of this kind?" [[Sources/1994ltr]]

The distribution of spoils in a typical deal follows a fixed pattern worth
checking for on any acquisition under review:

> "The sad fact is that most major acquisitions display an egregious
> imbalance: They are a bonanza for the shareholders of the acquiree; they
> increase the income and status of the acquirer's management; and they are a
> honey pot for the investment bankers and other professionals on both sides.
> But, alas, they usually reduce the wealth of the acquirer's shareholders."
> [[Sources/1994ltr]]

## 5. A manager asking staff whether a deal "makes sense"

A specific, checkable process red flag — the decision was outsourced to people
paid to say yes:

> "Often the CEO asks a strategic planning staff, consultants or investment
> bankers whether an acquisition or two might make sense. That's like asking
> your interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

## 6. Accounting charges that go the same direction every year

A charge that always improves reported results (never reduces them) is a sign
the assumption behind it is being pushed rather than estimated honestly.
Munger's own correction discipline is the standing reminder:

> "If you've hit three balls out of bounds to the left, aim a little to the
> right on the next swing." [[Sources/2007ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

Buffett applies the same warning to public and corporate pension assumptions
alike — a "fuse" long enough that the officials who set the assumption will be
gone before the bill arrives.

## 7. Fidelity failures in a hired manager

Distinct from a capital-allocation mistake, and weighted differently:

> "The fidelity disappointments can hurt beyond their financial impact, a pain
> that can approach that of a failed marriage." [[Sources/2024ltr]]
> [[Principles/Fidelity]]

## Usage Instructions

- 🚩 A confirmed match on any single item above warrants rejecting the deal, the
  manager, or the accounting presentation under review, not just a note for
  later.
- 🔍 Every flag above must be verified against the target's own numbers or
  language, the same way each is grounded here against a specific letter — an
  unverified suspicion is not a red flag.
- 🔍 Item 3 (the institutional imperative) is the hardest to observe directly
  since it operates on otherwise honest, competent managers; look for its four
  mechanisms in *decisions*, not in the manager's stated intentions.
