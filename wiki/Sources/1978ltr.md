# 1978 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1978: operating earnings at 19.4% of beginning shareholders' investment,
just short of the 1972 record. The Diversified Retailing merger forces full
consolidation of Blue Chip, and Buffett opens by explaining why the resulting
accounts obscure rather than illuminate. The letter also contains the SAFECO
argument — buying the best company in an industry below book value rather than
paying a premium for a mediocre one — and the first statement of the policy of
concentration.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Consolidated accounts that Berkshire itself does not use

The merger with Diversified Retailing lifted Berkshire's stake in Blue Chip to
about 58%, requiring full consolidation. Buffett's objection is not technical, and
he makes it before reporting any results:

- "This full consolidation of sales, expenses, receivables, inventories, debt, etc.
  produces an aggregation of figures from many diverse businesses - textiles,
  insurance, candy, newspapers, trading stamps - with dramatically different
  economic characteristics."
- "Such a grouping of Balance Sheet and Earnings items - some wholly owned, some
  partly owned - tends to obscure economic reality more than illuminate it. In
  fact, it represents a form of presentation that we never prepare for internal use
  during the year and which is of no value to us in any management activities."

That last clause is the strongest form the argument can take: the numbers required
by convention are ones management does not itself look at. The remedy is segment
reporting — "in this letter we try to present to you a view of our various
operating entities from the same perspective that we view them managerially."

Buffett also flags that the prior year's figures have changed, because accounting
requires the merger to be presented as though it had always existed, and warns the
reader accordingly: "This shifting base makes comparative commentary confusing".
Even the segment table comes with a caveat — the figures "should not be treated as
holy writ, but rather viewed as close approximations".

See [[Principles/Fidelity]] and [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]].

### A good year, reported with the reasons it will not repeat

Operating earnings reached 19.4% of beginning equity, and net worth per share had
roughly doubled over three years, compounding at about 25%. Both numbers are
disowned in the same paragraph:

- "Neither this 25% equity gain from all sources nor the 19.4% equity gain from
  operating earnings in 1978 is sustainable. The insurance cycle has turned downward
  in 1979, and it is almost certain that operating earnings measured by return on
  equity will fall this year."

The distinction he draws between the two ways of measuring — rate versus dollars —
is the one that governs every later letter: return on equity will fall while
"operating earnings measured in dollars are likely to increase on the much larger
shareholders' equity now employed in the business."

On forecasting, the boundary is drawn explicitly: "We make no attempt to predict
how security markets will behave; successfully forecasting short term stock price
movements is something we think neither we nor anyone else can do." What he will
predict is business value over the long run. See [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Textiles as the textbook case

The 1977 letter gave social reasons for staying in textiles. 1978 supplies the
economic anatomy, and it is the cleanest statement of commodity economics before
[[Sources/1982ltr]] generalises it:

- "The textile industry illustrates in textbook style how producers of relatively
  undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate
  returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage. As long as excess
  productive capacity exists, prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather
  than capital employed."

The mechanics beneath it are worth keeping. Old plant carried at a fraction of
replacement cost sounds like an advantage, and is not: "despite this "bargain cost"
of fixed assets, capital turnover is relatively low reflecting required high
investment levels in receivables and inventory compared to sales. Slow capital
turnover, coupled with low profit margins on sales, inevitably produces inadequate
returns on capital."

And the reason effort does not rescue it — the sentence that anticipates the parade
analogy of [[Sources/1985ltr]]: management pursues differentiation, cheaper
manufacturing and better fabrics diligently, but "The problem, of course, is that
our competitors are just as diligently doing the same thing."

Earnings of $1.3 million on $17 million of capital employed. The four reasons for
continuing are restated, and Buffett is explicit about the cost: "we intend to
continue to support our textile business despite more attractive alternative uses
for capital." With a wish attached — "We hope we don't get into too many more
businesses with such tough economic characteristics."

See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### The four-point test, restated for concentration

The 1977 criteria return almost word for word, but the framing has changed from
what to buy to how much:

- "We get excited enough to commit a big percentage of insurance company net worth
  to equities only when we find (1) businesses we can understand, (2) with favorable
  long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) priced
  very attractively. We usually can identify a small number of potential investments
  meeting requirements (1), (2) and (3), but (4) often prevents action."

Price is the binding constraint, not insight. The evidence is a comparison across
years: at the end of 1971 the whole common stock position cost $10.7 million,
because "There were equities of identifiably excellent companies available - but
very few at interesting prices." By the end of 1978 it was $129.1 million at cost
and $216.5 million at market.

Then the policy that follows, stated in three sentences:

- "Our policy is to concentrate holdings. We try to avoid buying a little of this or
  that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are
  convinced as to attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts."

And the preference that most investors would state backwards: "We are not concerned
with whether the market quickly revalues upward securities that we believe are
selling at bargain prices. In fact, we prefer just the opposite since, in most
years, we expect to have funds available to be a net buyer of securities."

See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]] and [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### The three-year proof, with the index that went nowhere

The passage that makes the case empirically rather than by assertion. Between the
end of 1975 and the end of 1978, equity holdings went from $39.3 million at cost
and market to $129.1 million at cost and $216.5 million at market, with $24.7
million of realized pre-tax gains along the way — about $112 million of realized
and unrealized gains in total.

- The comparison that matters: "During this same interval the Dow-Jones Industrial
  Average declined from 852 to 805. It was a marvelous period for the
  value-oriented equity buyer."

Buffett twice sets institutional behaviour against this, in footnotes that are
sharper than the main text: in 1971 pension managers put a record 122% of net
available funds into equities "at full prices they couldn't buy enough of them",
then 21% in 1974 after the bottom fell out — and in 1978, "a group that logically
should maintain the longest of investment perspectives, put only 9% of net available
funds into equities - breaking the record low figure set in 1974 and tied in 1977."

The structural claim underneath: "We continue to find for our insurance portfolios
small portions of really outstanding businesses that are available, through the
auction pricing mechanism of security markets, at prices dramatically cheaper than
the valuations inferior businesses command on negotiated sales."

And he states the disjunction honestly rather than assuming his side of it: "It
seems quite clear to us that either corporations are making very significant
mistakes in purchasing entire businesses at prices prevailing in negotiated
transactions and takeover bids, or that we eventually are going to make
considerable sums of money buying small portions of such businesses at the greatly
discounted valuations prevailing in the stock market."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### SAFECO — buying the best operator for less than book

The letter's set piece, and the fullest early argument for passive ownership of an
excellent business.

- "SAFECO probably is the best run large property and casualty insurance company in
  the United States. Their underwriting abilities are simply superb, their loss
  reserving is conservative, and their investment policies make great sense."
- Ranked against Berkshire's own: "SAFECO is a much better insurance operation than
  our own (although we believe certain segments of ours are much better than
  average), is better than one we could develop and, similarly, is far better than
  any in which we might negotiate purchase of a controlling interest."
- And the price: "we paid less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best company in
  the business, when far more than 100 cents on the dollar is being paid for
  mediocre companies in corporate transactions. And there is no way to start a new
  operation - with necessarily uncertain prospects - at less than 100 cents on the
  dollar."

The answer to the obvious objection — that a minority holder cannot direct
management — is the sharpest thing in the letter: "But why should we wish to do
this? The record would indicate that they do a better job of managing their
operations than we could do ourselves. While there may be less excitement and
prestige in sitting back and letting others do the work, we think that is all one
loses by accepting a passive participation in excellent management. Because, quite
clearly, if one controlled a company run as well as SAFECO, the proper policy also
would be to sit back and let management do its job."

That extends the non-control argument made about Capital Cities in
[[Sources/1977ltr]] into a general position.

### Retained earnings you never see, and when they should not be retained

SAFECO earnings attributable to Berkshire's shares were $6.1 million in 1978, of
which only the dividends — about 18% — appeared in operating earnings. Buffett's
claim about the rest is the look-through argument in embryo:

- "We believe the balance, although not reportable, to be just as real in terms of
  eventual benefit to us as the amount distributed. In fact, SAFECO's retained
  earnings (or those of other well-run companies if they have opportunities to
  employ additional capital advantageously) may well eventually have a value to
  shareholders greater than 100 cents on the dollar."
- Consistency is the argument: "We are not at all unhappy when our wholly-owned
  businesses retain all of their earnings if they can utilize internally those funds
  at attractive rates. Why should we feel differently about retention of earnings by
  companies in which we hold small equity interests, but where the record indicates
  even better prospects for profitable employment of capital?"

And the condition is stated in the same breath, which is what stops it becoming a
blanket endorsement of retention: "This proposition cuts the other way, of course,
in industries with low capital requirements, or if management has a record of
plowing capital into projects of low profitability; then earnings should be paid out
or used to repurchase shares - often by far the most attractive option for capital
utilization."

That is the retention test of [[Sources/1984ltr]] and the buyback argument of
[[Sources/1984ltr]], both present six years early. See [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]]
and [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### Reserving self-delusion, and why it is everyone's problem

On reinsurance, Buffett explains a failure mode that becomes the subject of a full
section in [[Sources/1984ltr]]:

- "It is very easy to fool yourself regarding underwriting results in reinsurance
  (particularly in casualty lines involving long delays in settlement), and we
  believe this situation prevails with many of our competitors. Unfortunately,
  self-delusion in company reserving almost always leads to inadequate industry rate
  levels. If major factors in the market don't know their true costs, the
  competitive "fall-out" hits all - even those with adequate cost knowledge."

The forecast for the industry is arithmetic rather than opinion: in auto lines,
January 1979 rates were 3% above a year earlier while repair and medical costs were
up over 9% — against year-end 1976, when rates had risen 22% and costs 8%. "Margins
will remain steady only if rates rise as fast as costs. This assuredly will not be
the case in 1979, and conditions probably will worsen in 1980."

And the warning against reading one's own optimism as an edge: "our underwriting
performance relative to the industry will improve somewhat in 1979, but every other
insurance management probably views its relative prospects with similar optimism -
someone is going to be disappointed."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Expansion, discouraged by the person proposing it

Buffett wants to expand insurance and immediately tells shareholders not to be
pleased about it:

- "But your reaction to this intent should not be unrestrained joy. Some of our
  expansion efforts - largely initiated by your Chairman have been lackluster,
  others have been expensive failures."
- The conclusion drawn from a decade: "It is not easy to buy a good insurance
  business, but our experience has been that it is easier to buy one than create
  one."

The best part of the business remains the segment bought in 1967 and still run by
Phil Liesche.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The equity portfolio — $133.8 million at cost, $220.9 million at market

Eight positions above $8 million of market value:

| Company | Cost | Market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Washington Post (934,300 sh) | $10.6m | $43.4m |
| GEICO convertible preferred (1,986,953 sh) | $19.4m | $28.3m |
| SAFECO (953,750 sh) | $23.9m | $26.5m |
| Interpublic Group (592,650 sh) | $4.5m | $19.0m |
| Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical (1,066,934 sh) | $18.1m | $18.7m |
| Knight-Ridder (453,800 sh) | $7.5m | $10.3m |
| GEICO common (1,294,308 sh) | $4.1m | $9.1m |
| American Broadcasting Companies (246,450 sh) | $6.1m | $8.6m |

Kaiser Aluminum was more than doubled from 1977 and SAFECO is new. The Washington
Post, unchanged in share count since 1977, had gone from $33.4m to $43.4m of market
value against the same $10.6m cost.

### SAFECO — 953,750 shares at $23.9 million

Bought at substantially under book value. Berkshire's share of 1978 earnings was
$6.1 million, of which only about 18% reached operating earnings as dividends. It
is still in the portfolio in [[Sources/1981ltr]].

### Insurance underwriting — $11 million of profit on ~$90 million of earned premium

From the National Indemnity segment run by Phil Liesche, with Roland Miller in
Underwriting and Bill Lyons in Claims — "a truly extraordinary achievement even
against the background of excellent industry conditions." Buffett also credits Jack
Ringwalt, the founder, "whose operating philosophy remains etched upon the company."

Mixed elsewhere: Cypress under Milt Thornton outstanding in its first year as a
subsidiary; Frank DeNardo brought in to fix National Indemnity's California
Worker's Compensation business, with volume cut to about 25% of its level eighteen
months earlier; the homestate operation disappointing despite favourable industry
conditions, with Kansas Fire and Casualty under Floyd Taylor the exception.

### Illinois National Bank — 2.1% on average assets

About three times the level of major banks, and Buffett argues with less asset risk
than most of them carry. Since 1968 consumer time deposits had quadrupled, net
income tripled and trust department income more than doubled.

Divestiture was required by 31 December 1980, most likely by spinning it off to
Berkshire shareholders in the second half of 1980.

### Associated Retail Stores — acquired outright via the Diversified merger

About 75 popular-priced women's apparel stores, launched in Chicago on 7 March 1931
with one store, $3,200 and two partners. Ben Rosner, who has run it since
Diversified bought it in 1967, produces returns on capital employed "often in the
20% after-tax area" from a business that has not grown and faces adverse
demographic and retailing trends.

Buffett closes on the ages of his managers — Rosner 75, Abegg 81, Vincenti 73 — and
what they have in common: "It is a real pleasure to work with managers who enjoy
coming to work each morning and, once there, instinctively and unerringly think like
owners."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].
