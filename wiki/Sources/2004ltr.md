# 2004 Annual Letter

A year Buffett grades as his own miss: book value per share gained 10.5% against
the S&P 500's 10.9%, the first shortfall since 1999, and he says so without
qualifying it — his operating managers pulled more than their share of the
load, but by his own account he did not do his job of deploying capital very
well, striking out on the multi-billion dollar acquisitions he had hoped for and
leaving Berkshire sitting on $43 billion of cash equivalents. Around that
admission sits a long insurance section told as history rather than just
results — National Indemnity's disciplined-underwriter table back to 1980, and
GEICO's full arc from Leo Goodwin's 1936 founding through the 1976 near-failure
to Tony Nicely's turnaround — plus a governance fight in which Buffett himself
was accused of not being independent enough to sit on Coca-Cola's audit
committee.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A year graded as a personal miss

Net worth rose $8.3 billion, lifting book value per share 10.5% against the
index's 10.9% — a shortfall of four-tenths of a point, the kind of comparison
Buffett insists still matters:

> "However the yearly comparisons work out, Berkshire's long-term performance
> versus the S&P remains all-important. Our shareholders can buy the S&P through
> an index fund at very low cost. Unless we achieve gains in per-share intrinsic
> value in the future that outdo the S&P, Charlie and I will be adding nothing to
> what you can accomplish on your own." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The shortfall is attributed to himself, not to the businesses:

> "My hope was to make several multi-billion dollar acquisitions that would add
> new and significant streams of earnings to the many we already have. But I
> struck out." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The consequence was a cash pile Buffett is explicit about disliking:

> "I didn't do that job very well last year. My hope was to make several
> multi-billion dollar acquisitions that would add new and significant streams of
> earnings to the many we already have. But I struck out. Additionally, I found
> very few attractive securities to buy. Berkshire therefore ended the year with
> $43 billion of cash equivalents, not a happy position." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

Over the 40 years of present management, book value had compounded from $19 to
$55,824 — 21.9% annually — and Buffett is careful to note that intrinsic value
has grown somewhat *faster* than that, since 1964's Berkshire was a struggling
textile business worth less than book and 2004's is worth far more. See
[[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

He also flags the year as statistically unusual on its own terms: of the 35
years since the 1960s ended, only one before 2004 produced an S&P total return
within six points of the long-run 11.2% average — "last year's 'normal' return
is anything but."

### Investors minimizing their own returns

The letter carries no formal How to Minimize Investment Returns essay in
2004 — that runs the following year, see [[Sources/2005ltr]] — but its seed is
already present in the opening pages: three causes of mediocre investor
experience are named — high costs from
excessive trading or investment management, portfolio decisions based on tips
and fads rather than business evaluation, and start-and-stop timing that enters
late and exits after declines.

> "Investors should remember that excitement and expenses are their enemies. And
> if they insist on trying to time their participation in equities, they should
> try to be fearful when others are greedy and greedy only when others are
> fearful." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

### The zinc write-off and mono-linked chains

MidAmerican wrote off a zinc-recovery project begun in 1998, aimed at extracting
zinc from the brine produced by Berkshire's California geothermal operations.
Commercial viability seemed imminent for months, then one problem after another
appeared until, in September, "we threw in the towel." Buffett draws a general
rule from the failure — the value of a single point of possible breakdown:

> "Our failure here illustrates the importance of a guideline – stay with simple
> propositions – that we usually apply in investments as well as operations. If
> only one variable is key to a decision, and the variable has a 90% chance of
> going your way, the chance for a successful outcome is obviously 90%. But if ten
> independent variables need to break favorably for a successful result, and each
> has a 90% probability of success, the likelihood of having a winner is only
> 35%." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

> "Since a chain is no stronger than its weakest link, it makes sense to look for
> – if you'll excuse an oxymoron – mono-linked chains." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The write-off cost MidAmerican $579 million in 2004, against a $46 million loss
from the same project the year before. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Insurance: National Indemnity as the disciplined underwriter

Buffett uses the section to explain, from the ground up, how Berkshire's
insurance operations escape the commodity economics of the industry — most
insureds, unlike buyers of Gillette blades or Coca-Cola, have no brand
preference ("we wait in vain for 'I'd like a National Indemnity policy,
please'"), so price competition is normally fierce. NICO's answer since 1967 has
been to price for profit rather than volume and let volume go where it will:

> "Can you imagine any public company embracing a business model that would lead
> to the decline in revenue that we experienced from 1986 through 1999? That
> colossal slide, it should be emphasized, did not occur because business was
> unobtainable. Many billions of premium dollars were readily available to NICO
> had we only been willing to cut prices. But we instead consistently priced to
> make a profit, not to match our most optimistic competitor. We never left
> customers – but they left us." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The letter names the "institutional imperative" and the fear factor working
against this discipline at other insurers — no CEO wants to report shrinking
volume, and employees facing layoffs rationalise underpriced business to keep
the organisation intact. NICO's answer to the fear factor is a standing promise:

> "To combat employees' natural tendency to save their own skins, we have always
> promised NICO's workforce that no one will be fired because of declining
> volume, however severe the contraction. (This is not Donald Trump's sort of
> place.)" — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The "Portrait of a Disciplined Underwriter" table runs written premium,
headcount and underwriting profit margin from 1980 to 2004: premium collapsed
from $366.2 million in 1986 to a low of 54.5 in 1999 (in $ millions) as prices fell, then
recovered to $605.6 million by 2004 as the market hardened, with underwriting
profit margins ranging from a 18.7% loss (1983) to a 30.7% profit (1986). Tom
Murphy's $3-million-hire parable — that adding one $20,000 employee is really a
lifetime commitment once raises, benefits and job security are counted — is
told as the discipline that keeps NICO lean between cycles. Don Wurster, NICO's
CEO since 1989, has amassed $950 million of float there that Buffett expects
proves negative-cost over time. See [[Concepts/Float]] and
[[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### GEICO: the history behind the low-cost claim

The letter tells GEICO's origin as the industry's structural counter-example.
Auto insurance began as a cartel of Northeastern "bureau rate" companies
competing for agents rather than customers; State Farm (1922) and Allstate
(1931) undercut them with captive agency forces; then Leo Goodwin went further:

> "But a man named Leo Goodwin had an idea for an even more efficient auto
> insurer and, with a skimpy $200,000, started GEICO in 1936. Goodwin's plan was
> to eliminate the agent entirely and to deal instead directly with the auto
> owner." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

GEICO reached a 4% market share by 1975, superbly managed under Lorimer
Davidson. Then it nearly failed:

> "But after my friend and hero Lorimer Davidson retired as CEO in 1970, his
> successors soon made a huge mistake by under-reserving for losses. This
> produced faulty cost information, which in turn produced inadequate pricing. By
> 1976, GEICO was on the brink of failure." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

Jack Byrne's rescue required price increases severe enough to cost the company
its policyholders — market share fell to 1.8% by 1980 — followed by an unwise
diversification detour that stalled growth near 1.9% by 1993. Tony Nicely took
charge that year, and the letter states the result plainly:

> "And what a difference that's made: In 2005 GEICO will probably secure a 6%
> market share." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

2004 delivered $1 billion of customer savings versus what policyholders would
otherwise have paid, a $191 million associate profit-sharing bonus averaging
24.3% of salary, and a re-entry into New Jersey — a state GEICO had avoided since
Byrne's 1976 withdrawal — after commissioner Holly Bakke's reform made the
market workable again; GEICO reached 140,000 New Jersey policyholders, about 4%
share, within months. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### Float: $46.1 billion, better than costless

> "The $20 million of float that came with our 1967 purchase has now increased –
> both by way of internal growth and acquisitions – to $46.1 billion."
> — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

| Insurance Operation | Underwriting Profit ($m) | Yearend Float ($m) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General Re | 3 | 23,120 |
| B-H Reinsurance | 417 | 15,278 |
| GEICO | 970 | 5,960 |
| Other Primary | 161 | 1,736 |
| **Total** | **1,551** | **46,094** |

Buffett states the mechanism the same way he does every year — float is better
than free when underwriting is profitable, which has been true in roughly half
of Berkshire's 38 insurance years — but adds a specific caution for 2005: mega-cat
pricing was softening even as reinsurance capacity stayed available, so "in 2005
pricing will be less attractive than it has been." See [[Concepts/Float]] and
[[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### MidAmerican Energy: structure, and an Omaha grudge repaid

The 80.5% (fully diluted) interest is capped at 9.9% of the vote by the Public
Utility Holding Company Act, so MidAmerican is carried on Berkshire's books as a
one-line entry rather than consolidated — a structural quirk Buffett expects
PUHCA's eventual repeal to end. At yearend $1.478 billion of MidAmerican's junior
debt was payable to Berkshire, carrying 11% interest that lets the company fund
acquisitions without diluting Walter Scott, Dave Sokol and Greg Abel.

The Northern Natural pipeline, sold back to MidAmerican after passing through
Enron and Dynegy (told in full in [[Sources/2002ltr]]), produced an unexpected
coda in 2004: a $72.2 million profit from the sale of an Enron receivable
bundled into the original purchase.

> "Walter, Dave and I, as natives of Omaha, view this unanticipated gain as war
> reparations – partial compensation for the loss our city suffered in 1986 when
> Ken Lay moved Northern to Houston, after promising to leave the company here."
> — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

MidAmerican's HomeServices brokerage participated in $59.8 billion of
transactions, up $11.2 billion from 2003, through 17 brokerage firms and more
than 18,000 brokers under Ron Peltier. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Finance and Finance Products: Clayton's financing engine

Clayton Homes, bought in 2003, is financed by borrowing from Berkshire at a
one-point markup on Berkshire's own cost — $2 billion outstanding at the start
of 2004, $7.35 billion by January 2005 after a large seasoned-portfolio purchase
from an exiting bank. The manufactured-home industry itself remained in
"intensive care," selling under 135,000 new homes in 2004, about 40% of the
volume of the loosely-financed 1995-99 boom years — a boom Buffett describes as
built on "irresponsible financing and naïve funders" — [[Sources/2004ltr]].

Gen Re Securities continued its wind-down, down to 2,890 outstanding contracts
from a peak of 23,218, at a cost written as a warning rather than a footnote:

> "Like Hell, derivative trading is easy to enter but difficult to leave. (Other
> similarities come to mind as well.)" — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

> "It's small wonder that phantom profits are often recorded. Investors should
> understand that in all types of financial institutions, rapid growth sometimes
> masks major underlying problems (and occasionally fraud). The real test of the
> earning power of a derivatives operation is what it achieves after operating
> for an extended period in a no-growth mode. You only learn who has been
> swimming naked when the tide goes out." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The American Express-Coca-Cola-Gillette-Wells Fargo "Big Four" had absorbed
$3.83 billion since May 1988, held on a dollar-weighted basis about 12½ years by
yearend 2004; Berkshire's share of their 2004 earnings was $1.2 billion, about
31.3% of cost, with cash distributions of $434 million (11.3% of cost). Buffett
grades the group's investment result as "satisfactory, though far from
spectacular" and concedes a specific error of omission:

> "Clearly, Berkshire's results would have been far better if I had caught this
> swing of the pendulum. That may seem easy to do when one looks through an
> always-clean, rear-view mirror. Unfortunately, however, it's the windshield
> through which investors must peer, and that glass is invariably fogged."
> — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

> "Nevertheless, I can properly be criticized for merely clucking about
> nose-bleed valuations during the Bubble rather than acting on my views. Though
> I said at the time that certain of the stocks we held were priced ahead of
> themselves, I underestimated just how severe the overvaluation was. I talked
> when I should have walked." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

Lou Simpson's GEICO-managed equity portfolio (about $2½ billion) outperformed the
S&P by an average of 6.8 points annually from 1980 to 2004, and Buffett is
explicit that he does not direct or even always know Lou's transactions in
advance — he learns of them roughly ten days after each month ends, "sometimes,
it should be added, I silently disagree with his decisions. But he's usually
right." A separate discipline is restated for the record:

> "The media continue to report that 'Buffett buys' this or that stock.
> Statements like these are almost always based on filings Berkshire makes with
> the SEC and are therefore wrong. As I've said before, the stories should say
> 'Berkshire buys.'" — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

A €254 million Level 3 bond position, bought in 2001 at 51.7% of par and sold in
December 2004 at 85% of par, produced a $163 million gain split almost evenly
between the market's revised view of Level 3's credit and the Euro's
appreciation against the dollar during the holding period.

### Foreign currencies and the trade deficit

Berkshire held about $21.4 billion of foreign exchange contracts across 12
currencies at yearend — a position Buffett had never taken before March 2002,
now sustained by his view that U.S. trade policy will pressure the dollar for
years. He is careful to separate the currency bet from any doubt about the
American economy itself:

> "Be clear on one point: In no way does our thinking about currencies rest on
> doubts about America. We live in an extraordinarily rich country, the product
> of a system that values market economics, the rule of law and equality of
> opportunity." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

The deficit is quantified — $618 billion in 2004, up from an "alarming" $263
billion in 1999 that prompted a Congressional study still gathering dust — and
the transfer-of-wealth mechanism is spelled out: other countries and their
citizens now own a net of about $3 trillion of the U.S., a figure Buffett
projects to roughly $11 trillion within a decade if the deficits persist,
implying an annual "tribute" equal to about 3% of GDP simply to service what
foreigners then hold. He names the destination with a deliberately provocative
label:

> "A country that is now aspiring to an 'Ownership Society' will not find
> happiness in – and I'll use hyperbole here for emphasis – a 'Sharecropper's
> Society.'" — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

He quotes Keynes on the reputational asymmetry of an unconventional bet —
"Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally
than to succeed unconventionally" — and states the practical version: leaving
all of Berkshire's assets in dollars would let a currency mistake go
unnoticed, while the foreign-exchange position makes any error "very public."
The macro caveat is explicit: "macro-economics is a tough game in which few
people, Charlie and I included, have demonstrated skill. We may well turn out to
be wrong in our currency judgments."

### Governance: the Coca-Cola independence fight

Several institutional shareholders sought to remove Buffett from Coca-Cola's
board, or at least from its audit committee, questioning his independence
because McLane and Dairy Queen buy Coca-Cola products. Buffett's rebuttal turns
on Berkshire's $8 billion Coca-Cola stake dwarfing any profit on those routine
purchases, and reaches for a biblical measure of where loyalty actually sits:

> "I can't resist mentioning that Jesus understood the calibration of
> independence far more clearly than do the protesting institutions. In Matthew
> 6:21 He observed: 'For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.'
> Even to an institutional investor, $8 billion should qualify as 'treasure' that
> dwarfs any profits Berkshire might earn on its routine transactions with
> Coke." — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

He generalises the point into a standing test of which directors are actually
compromised — not those with large disclosed stakes, but those who depend on
board fees for a meaningful share of income:

> "In our view, based on our considerable boardroom experience, the least
> independent directors are likely to be those who receive an important fraction
> of their annual income from the fees they receive for board service (and who
> hope as well to be recommended for election to other boards and thereby to
> boost their income further). Yet these are the very board members most often
> classed as 'independent.'" — [[Sources/2004ltr]]

A worked example follows: directors each collecting about $100,000 annually in
fees scuttled a multi-billion-dollar acquisition proposal before shareholders
ever saw it, then voted themselves a fee increase at the same meeting. Two
post-bubble reforms are credited at Berkshire itself — regular director sessions
without the CEO present, and a whistleblower line direct to Buffett and the
audit committee — with Buffett faulting himself for not adopting either sooner.
See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Options expensing, again

Buffett encloses a Washington Post op-ed on a bill passed 312-111 by the House
that would have gutted option expensing, blocked in the Senate by Richard
Shelby, with SEC chairman Bill Donaldson standing firm against the same
check-writing CEOs who had muscled Congress in 1993. He clarifies what is not at
stake — no one is proposing to restrict the use of options, and Buffett expects
his own successor to be paid partly in options, provided they carry an
appropriate strike price, an escalation reflecting retained earnings, and a
ban on quickly selling shares received. Mandatory expensing was scheduled for
June 15, 2005. See [[Applications/RedFlags]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**No large acquisitions closed.** Buffett states plainly that he "struck out" on
the multi-billion-dollar purchases he hoped for, leaving $43 billion in cash
equivalents at yearend — a deliberate non-decision rather than an oversight, since
the alternative was deploying capital at prices he did not consider attractive.

**Portfolio**

- Held the Big Four (American Express, Coca-Cola, Gillette, Wells Fargo)
  unchanged in substance; no major new equity positions established.
- Sold €254 million of Level 3 bonds (bought 2001 at 51.7% of par) at 85% of
  par, for a $163 million gain split between credit-quality recovery and Euro
  appreciation.
- Maintained $21.4 billion of foreign-exchange contracts across 12 currencies,
  a position first taken in 2002 and held on the trade-deficit thesis.
- PetroChina "H" shares held at $488 million cost, $1,249 million market value —
  the position Buffett would sell out of entirely within the following year on
  valuation grounds.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew $1.9 billion to $46.1 billion at a cost of less than nothing —
  $1.551 billion of underwriting profit.
- MidAmerican wrote off its zinc-recovery project ($579 million pre-tax) after
  seven years of development.
- $1.478 billion of MidAmerican junior debt held at 11% interest, funding
  acquisitions without diluting the utility partners.

**Finance**

- Clayton Homes' Berkshire-financed receivables portfolio grew from $2 billion
  to $7.35 billion, mostly via a single seasoned-portfolio purchase from an
  exiting bank lender.
- Gen Re Securities wound down to 2,890 contracts from a peak of 23,218.
