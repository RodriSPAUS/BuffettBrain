# 2022 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

Berkshire's first fully in-person annual meeting in three years, back in Omaha with Buffett, Munger, Abel and Jain on stage for two full Q&A sessions plus the formal shareholder meeting, all carried live by CNBC with its own anchors and guest interviews filling the intermissions. The quarter itself supplies most of the news: Berkshire went from a five-quarter run as a net seller of stocks to spending roughly $40-41 billion in about three weeks in late February and March, acquiring Alleghany Corporation for $11.6 billion, building a roughly 14% stake in Occidental Petroleum, adding Hewlett-Packard and more Chevron, and taking a merger-arbitrage position in Activision Blizzard. Buffett is unusually blunt throughout about the market's "casino" character over the prior two years, and both men are pointed about Bitcoin, passive-index voting power, and a shareholder proposal to strip Buffett of his chairmanship — which the formal meeting later votes down along with three other shareholder proposals.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Cash as Oxygen, and What Actually Counts as Money
Opening with Q1 operating earnings of about $7 billion, Buffett explains Berkshire's insistence on holding actual Treasury bills rather than commercial paper or money-market funds, recalling the 2008 freeze and the close call in March 2020:

> "It's just -- it's like oxygen. It's there all the time. But if it disappears for a few minutes, that's all over." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

He illustrates what "money" legally means with two $20 bills: a current Federal Reserve note and one issued decades earlier by the Illinois National Bank & Trust of Rockford, a bank Berkshire itself bought in 1969 and which had once been allowed to print its own currency. Only the Federal Reserve note still qualifies:

> "But it says this note is legal tender for all debts public and private." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

### The Alleghany Story: An $11.6 Billion Deal from One Email
Buffett recounts buying Alleghany Corporation, a company he says he had tracked for 60 years, after its new CEO Joe Brandon sent him an unsolicited copy of his first annual report as a courtesy on February 25 — one day before Buffett's own letter told shareholders Berkshire saw little to buy. A brief note back proposing a meeting in New York led directly to an offer; Buffett is explicit that no bankers or formal process preceded the decision, and that the deal would not have happened had the email never been sent.

### Occidental Petroleum and "The Casino": Buying 14% in Two Weeks
Buffett uses Berkshire's rapid accumulation of roughly 14% of Occidental Petroleum — achievable, he says, because index funds and a few large institutional holders effectively locked up 40% of the stock and left the rest churning among short-term traders — as his central illustration of how speculative the market had become over the prior two years:

> "We have computers, algorithms trading against other computers." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

He contrasts this "casino" behavior with real investment (a farm or apartment building bought and held for decades) and reads from Keynes's 1936 chapter on markets, tying the theme to his own first exposure to raw speculation: watching gamblers at the Flamingo casino in Las Vegas on his honeymoon in 1952.

### The Activision Arbitrage: Buffett Breaks His Own News
Buffett deliberately uses the meeting to correct the record on Activision Blizzard: roughly 15 million shares had been bought months earlier by one of Berkshire's other portfolio managers for unrelated reasons, but after Microsoft announced a $95-per-share acquisition in January, Buffett personally decided to increase the position — a classic merger-arbitrage bet dependent on regulatory approval rather than the stock's fundamentals — bringing Berkshire's stake to about 9.5%. He stresses the decision and any resulting losses if the deal collapses are his alone, not the analyst who made the original purchase.

### Nuclear and Cyber Risk: "We Have No Solution for That"
Asked about nuclear risk to Berkshire's insurance operations, Buffett compares the daily probability of catastrophic nuclear conflict to a coin flip repeated indefinitely, and Munger is blunt about the limits of preparation:

> "I know what I'm going to do if there's a nuclear war, I'm going to crawl under the table and kiss my ass goodbye." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

Ajit Jain says nuclear exposure is the one major risk category he cannot reasonably estimate, unlike earthquake, hurricane or cyber risk, partly because regulators and courts tend to override policy exclusions after the fact. Greg Abel describes Berkshire Hathaway Energy alone fielding billions of cyberattacks daily, and praises unusually close, functioning cooperation between the company and U.S. government agencies on cyber defense.

### GEICO, Progressive, State Farm, and the Charlie Munger Bridge
Ajit Jain again attributes GEICO's margin gap with Progressive mainly to a late start on telematics-based pricing, though he expects it to close within a year or two. Greg Abel defends BNSF's operating-ratio gap with Union Pacific as the cost of prioritizing customer service over precision-scheduled-railroading metrics, and Buffett notes a bridge on BNSF's network is actually named the Charles T. Munger Bridge after Munger's early questioning of the railroad's capital spending. Separately, Buffett holds up State Farm — a mutual insurer with no public shareholders or stock-based incentives, roughly $140 billion in net worth versus Progressive's roughly $20 billion — as a business that "shouldn't exist under capitalism" by conventional theory, yet remains the largest U.S. auto insurer eight decades after Leo Goodwin started GEICO to compete with it.

### The Power of Repurchases: American Express and Apple
Buffett illustrates the mechanics of compounding through buybacks with two examples: Berkshire's roughly 11.2% stake in American Express, bought by 1998, has grown to about 20% purely through Amex's own share repurchases, without Berkshire spending another dollar; and Apple's continued buybacks mean each 1/10 of 1% rise in Berkshire's ownership adds about $100 million to attributable earnings from a company now earning roughly $100 billion a year.

### The "Aha Moment": Buffett's Own Investing Education
Prompted by a question on market timing, Buffett insists neither he nor Munger has ever bought or sold based on a view of where markets or the economy were headed, citing his own poorly timed 2008 purchases and his complete miss of the March 2020 bottom. He then describes discovering value investing as a teenager in Lincoln, Nebraska, comparing the shift to an optical illusion that suddenly resolves from one image into another — years of amateur technical analysis and chart-reading undone by a single chapter of Benjamin Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*.

### Corporate Activism, Independent Directors, and Political Silence
Asked how CEOs should navigate political and social issues, Buffett says he deliberately avoids taking public positions as Berkshire's chairman, reasoning that he would alienate more people permanently than he would please, at the expense of employees and shareholders who never agreed to bear that cost; he references Munger's 1989 letter resigning Wesco's savings-and-loan trade association over industry practices Munger found intolerable as the rare exception worth the cost. On board independence, Buffett describes (without naming him) a director whose entire income came from board fees across five prestigious companies, calling into question what "independent" really means when someone is that financially dependent on the role; his own re-election vote once fell from around 98% to 84% after some institutions challenged his independence over Berkshire subsidiaries' incidental Coca-Cola purchases. Munger is characteristically direct:

> "He's independent the way a slave is independent." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

### The Chairman/CEO Split Proposal and Passive Index Fund Power
The National Legal and Policy Center's shareholder proposal to require an independent board chair — effectively separating Buffett's chairman and CEO roles — draws sharp dismissal from Munger:

> "And to me it's the most ridiculous criticism I ever heard." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

Separately, both men warn about the concentration of voting power in a handful of index-fund managers, with Munger calling it "not good for the country" if it continues growing, and Buffett predicting Congress would eventually intervene if public opinion turned against a small number of firms effectively controlling corporate America's governance.

### Bitcoin, and a Note on Tribalism
Buffett elaborates his standing view that Bitcoin is not a productive asset — unlike farmland or apartments, which generate crops or rent, Bitcoin "doesn't multiply, it doesn't produce anything" — while Munger goes further:

> "Oh well, in my life, I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison with somebody else, and Bitcoin does all 3." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

Buffett connects the attachment some investors have to crypto with what he calls a broader rise in societal "tribalism," comparing it to the intense partisan loyalty around Franklin Roosevelt in his own 1930s childhood.

### Inflation: $2.2 Trillion in Circulation
Buffett cites Federal Reserve balance-sheet data showing currency in circulation rising from about $800 billion roughly 15 years earlier to about $2.2 trillion — roughly $7,000 for every person in the country — and uses a thought experiment (mailing $1 million to every household) to argue that large-scale stimulus necessarily shows up eventually as higher prices, not just higher paper wealth. He calls Fed Chair Jerome Powell's pandemic-era actions correct even though inflation was a foreseeable consequence, and repeats his long-held view that personal skill, not any financial asset, is the best individual inflation hedge.

### The Salomon Rescue and the Price of a Fairness Opinion
Recommending the book *Trillion Dollar Triage* from the shareholder bookstore, Buffett revisits his 1991 emergency chairmanship of Salomon Brothers, when a bond-trading scandal nearly forced the firm into a bankruptcy Buffett believed would have spread through Wall Street like the 2008 Lehman collapse; the Federal Reserve, Treasury and SEC reversed an earlier decision within hours to keep Salomon funded, and Buffett personally flew to California to persuade CalPERS, a major lender that had pulled back, to keep doing business with the firm. Separately, on Delaware corporate law, he describes the only two times in Berkshire's history a "fairness opinion" was legally required (the 1978 Diversified Retailing/Berkshire merger and the later Blue Chip Stamps merger) and how, at Munger's suggestion, he shopped the opinion to ten prestigious investment banks starting at a deliberately insulting $60,000 fee — E.F. Hutton and PaineWebber both accepted — to illustrate how little of substance such opinions actually add. CNBC's own broadcast around the sessions included unrelated interstitial interviews (with actor Bill Murray on his NFT project, and with several Berkshire subsidiary CEOs on inflation and supply chains) that are incidental to the meeting's substance and not summarized further here.

### China and Insurance Float, from Jack Ringwalt to Ajit Jain
Munger reaffirms his willingness to accept added political risk investing in China in exchange for better businesses at lower prices, despite acknowledging rising regulatory tension there. On float, Buffett retells how National Indemnity came to Berkshire in 1967 after founder Jack Ringwalt, frustrated with insurance regulators, agreed to sell for $50 a share in a single conversation with no audit — "pure luck," Buffett says, that set up decades of float growth later transformed by Ajit Jain's arrival in 1986.

### The Formal Meeting: Directors Elected, Four Shareholder Proposals Fail
The shareholder meeting convened with 614,692 Class A and 1,287,632,719 Class B shares outstanding. All 15 director nominees, including Buffett, Munger, Abel and Jain, were elected with at least 449,190 votes each. Four shareholder proposals opposed by the board all failed: an independent-chair requirement from the National Legal and Policy Center (72,298 for / 421,181 against), a climate-risk disclosure proposal from Federated Hermes, CalPERS and CDPQ (127,214 for / 370,415 against), a greenhouse-gas-emissions reporting proposal from As You Sow/Whistle Stop Capital (127,065 for / 370,630 against), and a diversity-and-inclusion reporting proposal, also from As You Sow (123,614 for / 373,925 against).

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Q1 2022 net securities purchases**: roughly $40-41 billion, concentrated in about three weeks in late February and March, ending five straight quarters as a net seller of stocks through 2021
- **Alleghany Corporation**: acquired for $11.6 billion
- **Occidental Petroleum**: built to roughly 14% ownership within about two weeks
- **Chevron**: increased to Berkshire's 4th-largest holding, up more than 35% year to date
- **Hewlett-Packard**: new equity stake disclosed
- **Activision Blizzard**: roughly 9.5% stake built as a merger-arbitrage position on Microsoft's announced $95-per-share acquisition
- **Share repurchases**: about $3.1-3.2 billion in Q1 2022; none in April
- **Berkshire Hathaway Energy**: over $3 billion in new Iowa transmission investment approved
- **Formal meeting**: all 15 director nominees elected; independent-chair, climate-risk, GHG-emissions and diversity-reporting shareholder proposals all failed
