# 2025 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

Buffett's 60th annual meeting, and the one in which he ends the day by recommending to Berkshire's board that Greg Abel become CEO at year-end — an announcement he sprang on the audience, and on Abel himself, with no advance warning beyond his two children on the board. Everything before that moment is otherwise a fairly typical wide-ranging session: tariffs and trade policy, a record cash pile approaching $350 billion, the Japanese trading-house investments, GEICO's turnaround under Todd Combs, AI's implications for insurance underwriting, private equity's push into the insurance business, a new 1% excise tax on stock buybacks, Berkshire Hathaway Energy's wildfire liabilities and falling valuation, and the long-run fiscal and currency risks Buffett keeps returning to. As in 2021 and 2022, CNBC's own hosts and guest interviews fill the breaks, but this transcript ends immediately after the succession announcement — no formal shareholder-meeting business (director elections, proxy votes) is recorded here.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Headline: Buffett Recommends Greg Abel as CEO at Year-End
With a five-minute warning from the production crew, Buffett pivots from Q&A to announce, unprompted by any question, that he will ask Berkshire's eleven-member board — only two of whom (his children Howard and Susan) had advance notice — to make Greg Abel CEO effective at year-end:

> "But I think it's -- the time has arrived where Greg, should become the Chief Executive Officer of the company at year-end." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He says he expects the board to act unanimously, that he intends to keep coming into the office and be available for major decisions or crises, that Howard Buffett is still slated to become non-executive chairman only after Buffett's death, and that he will not sell a single share of Berkshire in the meantime:

> "But I will not -- I have no intention, zero, of selling one share of Berkshire Hathaway, it will get given away." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He frames holding every share as itself an economic judgment — that Berkshire's prospects are better under Abel's management than his own. The hall's reaction was a lengthy standing ovation, which Buffett dryly noted "can be interpreted in two ways."

### Tariffs as "Economic War"
Asked to reconcile his 2003 proposal for import certificates (a mechanism to balance trade) with his current criticism of tariffs, Buffett distinguishes the two but is blunt about where he now stands:

> "But the main thing to do is not -- trade should not be a weapon." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He argues the U.S. has already "won" as the dominant economy built from a tiny 1790 population base, and calls it a mistake for a country that prosperous to treat trade adversarially toward a world of nine nuclear powers, several unstable. He repeats the point later in the session, calling the administration's protectionist posture a "big mistake" and arguing a more prosperous rest of the world makes Americans safer, not poorer.

### A Record Cash Pile, and the Discipline of Waiting
With cash and short-term investments around 27% of assets (versus a 13% historical average) and a near-miss on a $10 billion acquisition Buffett declines to name, he defends Berkshire's willingness to sit mostly in Treasury bills for long stretches, rejecting any framing of it as a smoothing gift to Abel's incoming tenure. He compares opportunities to occasional windfalls rather than a steady stream, and repeats his long-standing metaphor that patience is not passive:

> "And I would say that turning every page is one important ingredient to bring to the investment field." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

### Japan's Five Trading Companies: A 50-Year Position
Buffett and Abel reaffirm no intention of ever selling the roughly $20 billion stake built in Japan's five major trading companies, recently allowed to exceed the 10% ownership ceiling Berkshire originally promised each company. Abel describes regular in-person meetings and a deliberately "super long term" relationship; Berkshire has begun issuing yen-denominated debt to match the investment's currency exposure — the first deliberate currency-matching policy of its kind at Berkshire, distinct from a single large dollar-short currency bet the firm ran profitably decades earlier.

### Berkshire Hathaway Energy: Falling Valuation and Wildfire Liability
Buffett walks through why Berkshire's two purchases of BHE minority stakes implied sharply different valuations — $87 billion when buying 1% from Greg Abel in late 2022, versus $48.8 billion buying the Walter Scott family's remaining 8% in 2024 — attributing the roughly 44% drop to a broader deterioration in how some states treat regulated utilities, not merely PacifiCorp's own wildfire exposure. Abel details a policy shift adopted after the 2020 Oregon wildfires: rather than prioritizing keeping power on, BHE's utilities now proactively de-energize lines when fire risk is high, even though it raises other risks (hospitals, emergency services) that have to be separately managed. Both men are explicit that Berkshire will not use shareholder money to absorb open-ended wildfire liability as a "insurer of last resort" if the economics don't work.

### GEICO's Turnaround Under Todd Combs
Ajit Jain credits GEICO's recovery to closing a telematics gap with Progressive that had persisted for roughly 15-20 years, matching rate to risk more precisely, and a cost-cutting program that reduced headcount from about 50,000 to 20,000, saving roughly $2 billion a year; the result was seven consecutive quarters with a combined ratio in the 80s and $39 billion of float. Buffett adds historical color: Berkshire paid $50 million for half of GEICO in 1976, a stake that alone earned roughly $1 billion in the first quarter of 2025.

### AI and Insurance: "I Would Take Ajit Any Time"
Asked how AI might reshape insurance risk assessment, Ajit Jain says Berkshire is deliberately not chasing the technology as a first mover, preferring to wait until the opportunity is clearer, though individual insurance units are experimenting. Buffett is emphatic about where he'd place his bet regardless:

> "So if you gave me a choice that -- gave me a choice of having $100 billion available to participate in an insurance -- property casualty insurance business for the next 10 years and a choice of getting the top AI product out of whoever is developing it or having Ajit making the decision, I would take Ajit any time." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

### Private Equity's Push Into Insurance
Jain says large private-equity firms (naming Blackstone, Apollo and KKR by category, not directly) have taken over much of the life-insurance float business with more leveraged, credit-heavy investment strategies Berkshire won't match:

> "And therefore, we put up the white flag and said, we can't compete in this segment right now." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He distinguishes this from the property-casualty segment, where Berkshire still competes, and warns that regulators may eventually push back on the risk these firms are taking with policyholder assets.

### The Buyback Tax, and "The Cathedral and the Casino"
Buffett notes Berkshire made no share repurchases in the reported period, partly because of a 1% federal excise tax on buybacks that makes Berkshire's own repurchases more expensive than an outside investor's purchase of the same shares — illustrating with Apple's roughly $100 billion in annual buybacks, which now cost Tim Cook's shareholders about $1 billion a year in tax. Widening into a broader reflection on capital-light "Magnificent Seven"-style businesses and the money-management industry's fee structures, he offers an extended metaphor:

> "Capitalism in the United States has succeeded like nothing you've ever seen, but what it is, is it's a combination of this magnificent cathedral which has produced an economy like nothing the world has ever seen." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He warns that the "casino" side — asset management fees, speculative trading — risks crowding out the "cathedral" of genuine productive investment if the balance isn't maintained.

### Currency, the Fiscal Deficit, and DOGE
Buffett calls the U.S. running roughly a 7% fiscal deficit against what he estimates as a sustainable 3% an unsustainable trajectory, invoking economist Herbert Stein:

> "But you know that if something can't go on forever, it will end, the quote of Herbert Stein, famous economist." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

Asked directly whether DOGE (the Department of Government Efficiency) will prove net positive, he calls unchecked bureaucracy "amazingly prevalent and contagious", including inside Berkshire itself, but stops short of a verdict on DOGE specifically, framing the deeper problem as no democracy's ability to durably cut spending once promised.

### Health Care: Revisiting Haven's Failure
Asked again about the defunct Berkshire-JPMorgan-Amazon health venture, Buffett restates that U.S. health spending near 20% of GDP was too politically entrenched for three outside companies to change, regardless of resources:

> "And we've couldn't -- we -- as I said to Jamie and Jeff, I said, well, the tapeworm won." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

### Turn Every Page: Career Advice and the Rockwood Chocolate Story
Fielding several questions from young shareholders about career advice, Buffett repeats his standard counsel — work for people you admire, find what interests you the way he found investing through his father's office at age eight — and credits luck alongside effort:

> "But the luckiest day in my life is the day I was born." [[Sources/Conference_call_2025]]

He retells, at length, how a 1954 tax-code change on LIFO cocoa inventory drew a 24-year-old Buffett to a nearly empty 1954 shareholder meeting in Brooklyn, where Jay Pritzker was the only other attendee and personally tutored him on the tax code — the deal that eventually became the foundation of Marmon, the diversified industrial holding company Berkshire now owns.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Succession**: Buffett will recommend Greg Abel become CEO at year-end 2025; Buffett stays on, intends no chairman role change announced yet, and will not sell any Berkshire shares
- **Cash position**: roughly $350 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries (about 27% of assets, versus a 13% historical average); no share repurchases made this period
- **A near-miss $10 billion acquisition** referenced but not disclosed
- **Japan**: roughly $20 billion invested across five trading companies, ownership ceiling above 10% now being relaxed by mutual agreement; Berkshire has begun issuing yen-denominated debt to match the position
- **Berkshire Hathaway Energy**: 1% stake bought from Greg Abel in late 2022 at an implied $87 billion valuation; remaining 8% bought from the Walter Scott family in 2024 at an implied $48.8 billion valuation
- **GEICO**: headcount cut from roughly 50,000 to 20,000 (about $2 billion/year in savings); combined ratio in the 80s for seven straight quarters; $39 billion of float
- **Buyback excise tax**: 1% federal tax on repurchases now costs Apple's shareholders roughly $1 billion a year on its ~$100 billion buyback program, cited as a factor curbing Berkshire's own repurchase appetite
