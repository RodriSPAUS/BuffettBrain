# 1965 Annual Letter Summary

`Raw/1965ltr.md` holds three documents: the **January 18, 1965 annual letter**
(full-year 1964 results, "Our Goal," and the method's expansion from three
categories to four), the **July 9, 1965 mid-year letter** (a controlling
interest acquired but not yet named), and the **November 1, 1965 letter to
partners for 1966**, which contains the single most consequential sentence in
the partnership's history: the first time Berkshire Hathaway is named as a
BPL holding.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### 1964 — a good year that Buffett grades as mediocre by his own standard

Partnership +27.8%, limited partners +22.3%, against the Dow's +18.7%.
Compounded 1957–1964: Dow 131.3%, partnership 608.7%, limited partners 402.9%
(annual rates 11.1% / 27.7% / 22.3%). By his own yardstick this was a weak
year:

> "Both the advantage of 9.1 percentage points on a partnership basis and 3.6 points by the limited partners were the poorest since 1959, which was a year of roughly comparable gains for the Dow." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

### Why professional management underperforms — five causes, named

Responding to the recurring question of why "very intelligent management"
fails to beat an unmanaged index, Buffett gives a five-part diagnosis:

> "I think it is much more the product of: (1) group decisions - my perhaps jaundiced view is that it is close to impossible for outstanding investment management to come from a group of any size with all parties really participating in decisions; (2) a desire to conform to the policies and (to an extent) the portfolios of other large well-regarded organizations; (3) an institutional framework whereby average is 'safe' and the personal rewards for independent action are in no way commensurate with the general risk attached to such action; (4) an adherence to certain diversification practices which are irrational; and finally and importantly, (5) inertia." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

### The Joys of Compounding — Peter Minuit and the Manhattan Indians

$24 for Manhattan in 1626, compounded at 6.5%, becomes $42 billion by 1964 —
"the Indians have only had to achieve a 6 1/2% return" to have the last laugh
on Minuit, and an extra half point takes the figure to $205 billion.

### Our Goal — the target given as a number, not a slogan

For the first time the ten-point ambition is turned into an explicit
compound-return forecast rather than a comparative one:

> "I am hopeful that our longer term experience will unfold along the following basis: (1) An overall gain from the Dow (including dividends, of course) averaging in the area of 7% per annum, exhibiting customarily wide amplitudes in achieving this average -- say, on the order or minus 40% to plus 50% at the extremes with the majority of years in the minus 10% to plus 20% range; (2) An average advantage of ten percentage points per annum for BPL before allocation to the general partner - again with large amplitudes in the margin from perhaps 10 percentage points worse than the Dow in a bad year to 25 percentage points better when everything clicks; and (3) The product of these two assumptions gives an average of 17% to BPL or about 14% to limited partners." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Synthesis/MindsetEvolution]]

He immediately flags the honesty risk in publishing a number — "This figure
would vary enormously from year to year" — and calls the whole exercise
"conjecture, perhaps heavily influenced by self-interest, ego, etc."

### The method grows a fourth category

"Generals" splits in two. The old category — bargains priced below what a
private owner would pay — is renamed "Generals-Private Owner Basis." A new
sibling covers securities cheap only *relative to comparable quality* —
what the letter calls Generals-Relatively Undervalued — where "value to a
private owner" is not a meaningful concept because of size. A new hedging
technique is introduced for it:

> "We have recently begun to implement a technique, which gives promise of very substantially reducing the risk from an overall change in valuation standards; e.g." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

Work-outs and controls are unchanged in definition. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### Swap funds — a test tube for taxes without investment sense

A sharp aside on a then-new vehicle that let investors swap concentrated
stock for a diversified basket without triggering tax — until redemption:

> "The dominant sales argument has been the deferment (deferment, when pronounced by an enthusiastic salesman, sometimes comes very close phonetically to elimination) of capital gains taxes while trading a single security for a diversified portfolio." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

Two of the three largest swap funds, Federal Street and Westminster,
compounded at 2.6% and 1.1% respectively over 1962–64 against the Dow's 9.8%
— a management record with the tax-deferral privilege the marketing point,
not results. Buffett's own tax philosophy is restated plainly:

> "If our objective is to produce the maximum after-tax compound rate, we simply have to own the most attractive securities obtainable at current prices, and, with 3,000 rather rapidly shifting variables, this must mean change (hopefully 'tax-generating' change)." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

### A warning about temperament, stated once and plainly

> "If a 20% or 30% drop in the market value of your equity holdings (such as BPL) is going to produce emotional or financial distress, you should simply avoid common stock type investments." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Concepts/MrMarket]]

### Mid-year — a control interest acquired, still unnamed

The July letter reports +10.4% for the first half (a 9.6-point advantage over
the Dow's +0.8%) and one large event kept deliberately vague:

> "During the first half, a series of purchases resulted in the acquisition of a controlling interest in one of the situations described in the 'General Private Owner' section of the last annual letter." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

> "The annual letter, issued in January, 1966, will carry a full story on this current control situation." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

### November — Berkshire Hathaway, named

The commitment letter for 1966 reveals what the mid-year letter withheld:

> "The partnership owns a controlling interest in Berkshire Hathaway Inc., a publicly-traded security." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

The valuation rule for the position is fixed immediately, in the same
fiduciary spirit already established at [[Cases/DempsterMill]] — a rule, not
a hope:

> "We will value our position in Berkshire Hathaway at yearend at a price halfway between net current asset value and book value." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

This is the single most consequential sentence in the corpus so far: the
holding that becomes, eventually, the entity this whole wiki is about.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Berkshire Hathaway Inc. — named as a controlling interest.** First disclosed in the November 1, 1965 letter; valuation fixed at halfway between net current asset value and book value. The "control interest acquired" mentioned unnamed in the July letter.
- **Results (full year 1964):** partnership +27.8%, limited partners +22.3%, against the Dow's +18.7%. Compounded 1957–64: 608.7% / 402.9% vs 131.3%.
- **Results (H1 1965):** partnership +10.4% against the Dow's +0.8%.
- **Method:** "Generals" split into Private-Owner and Relatively-Undervalued sub-categories; a new hedging technique introduced for the latter.
- **Structure:** net assets $26,074,000 on 1/1/1965 (from $105,100 at the May 1956 start); Susie and Warren's interest $3,406,700.
