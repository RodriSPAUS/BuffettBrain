# 2016 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share book value grew 10.7% in 2016 (net worth +$27.5 billion), trailing the S&P 500's 12.0% total return, extending the 52-year compounded annual gain to 19%. Beyond the standard business report, the letter carries two notable essays: a plain-language explainer on share repurchases (when they help versus hurt continuing shareholders) and "The Bet" — the full account of Buffett's ten-year, $500,000 wager (via Long Bets) that a low-cost S&P 500 index fund would beat a basket of fund-of-hedge-funds after fees, with nine years of results already in and the index fund far ahead.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Berkshire's Strategic History Since 1999
Buffett recaps how Berkshire shifted from a securities-driven company to an owner of large operating businesses, naming three pivotal moves: the 1996 buyout of the GEICO half Berkshire didn't already own (cash), the 1998 purchase of General Reinsurance using "a boatload" of Berkshire stock — which he calls a mistake that diluted shareholders by 21.8% — and the corrective 2000 cash purchase of 76% (later 90%) of MidAmerican Energy. He is blunt about his preference since:

> "Today, I would rather prep for a colonoscopy" than issue Berkshire shares. [[Sources/2016ltr]]

An after-tax earnings table for 1999–2016 (operating earnings versus capital gains) is reproduced in full, showing operating earnings growing from $0.67 billion in 1999 to $17.57 billion in 2016.

### America's Economic Miracle
Buffett repeats a recurring theme, crediting America's $90 trillion of accumulated wealth to its market system, rule of law, and immigration, and quoting Gertrude Stein on how ownership of assets survives defaults:

> "As Gertrude Stein put it, 'Money is always there, but the pockets change.'" [[Sources/2016ltr]]

> "I'll repeat what I've both said in the past and expect to say in future years: Babies born in America today are the luckiest crop in history." [[Sources/2016ltr]]

He cites the New York Fed's Meg McConnell on the unpredictability of financial panics — systemic risk tends to find investors rather than being spotted in advance — and reiterates that widespread market fear is an investor's friend while personal fear is the enemy.

### Share Repurchases: A Plain-Language Framework
Buffett lays out when buybacks help versus hurt continuing shareholders: repurchases below intrinsic value benefit those who remain (using a three-partner, $3,000-business analogy where buying out one partner at $900 versus $1,100 either helps or hurts the other two by $50 each), and are value-destroying above intrinsic value regardless of a company's other merits. He identifies two situations where repurchases should be skipped even at attractive prices — when internal cash needs for the business take priority, or when a better acquisition opportunity competes for the same funds. Berkshire's own policy: Buffett is authorized to repurchase shares at 120% of book value or below, a level the Board judges to be a significant discount to intrinsic value.

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses
BNSF and BHE accounted for 33% of after-tax operating earnings and invested $8.9 billion combined in plant and equipment. BHE's Iowa rate (7.1¢/KWH) remained the region's lowest, with a promise of no base-rate increase before 2029; wind supplied 55% of Iowa retail electricity in 2016, projected to reach 89% by 2020. [[Cases/BNSF]] and [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]] earnings tables for 2014–2016 are reported in full, including HomeServices, which grew to 38 realty companies and over 29,000 agents across 28 states.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: The Stock-Based Compensation Fight
This segment posted $120.059 billion revenue and $8.462 billion pre-tax earnings for 2016 (up from $7.115 billion in 2014), earning 24% after-tax on about $24 billion of average net tangible assets. Buffett escalates his critique of "adjusted earnings," specifically targeting managements that exclude both restructuring costs and stock-based compensation from reported results, with a satirical "accounting laboratory" passage arguing that Berkshire's own cash executive pay is functionally identical to the stock compensation other companies wave away:

> "To say 'stock-based compensation' is not an expense is even more cavalier. CEOs who go down that road are, in effect, saying to shareholders, 'If you pay me a bundle in options or restricted stock, don't worry about its effect on earnings.'" [[Sources/2016ltr]]

He separately notes that BNSF's GAAP depreciation charge understates the railroad's true replacement cost, meaning reported railroad-industry earnings overstate true economic earnings.

### Finance and Financial Products
Clayton Homes became America's largest home builder in 2016, delivering 42,075 units (5% of all new American homes) and beginning to diversify into site-built homes. Marmon's railcar fleet utilization fell to 91% from 97% amid a slowdown. Segment pre-tax earnings recap (in millions): Berkadia $91, Clayton $744, CORT $60, Marmon Containers and Cranes $126, Marmon Railcars $654, XTRA $179, net financial income $276, for a total of $2,130 versus $2,086 in 2015.

### Investments: New Airline and Apple Stakes
The Kraft Heinz stake (325,442,152 shares, cost $9.8 billion, carried at $15.3 billion GAAP, market value $28.4 billion) is excluded from the ranked holdings table because it is equity-method accounted. The top-15 table at year-end 2016 (cost/market, in millions) included new positions in Apple ($6,747/$7,093), Delta ($2,299/$2,702), Southwest Airlines ($1,757/$2,153) and United Continental ($1,477/$1,940) alongside American Express ($1,287/$11,231), Coca-Cola ($1,299/$16,584), IBM ($13,815/$13,484), Phillips 66 ($5,841/$6,445) and Wells Fargo ($12,730/$27,555, 10.0%), for total common stocks of $65,828M cost / $122,032M market. Excluded from the table: $5 billion of Bank of America preferred stock (paying $300 million annually) plus a warrant for 700 million common shares at $5 billion through September 2021, worth an unrealized $10.5 billion gain at year-end.

### "The Bet": Ten Years, Index Fund vs. Funds of Hedge Funds
Buffett recounts his 2007 public wager that an unmanaged low-cost S&P 500 index fund would beat, after all fees, a basket of at least five funds-of-hedge-funds over ten years, taken up only by Ted Seides of Protégé Partners. Through nine of the ten years, the five funds-of-funds' cumulative gains to date ranged from 2.9% to 62.8% (the S&P index fund itself gained 85.4% cumulative), and the five funds averaged just 2.2% compounded annually — meaning $1 million invested in those funds would have gained only $220,000, versus $854,000 gained by the index fund. Buffett estimates roughly 60% of all gains earned by the five funds-of-funds over the period were consumed by manager fees at the fund and fund-of-funds level, and credits Jack Bogle by name as the person who has done the most for American investors. He closes with an old Omaha stockyards line from his brother-in-law Homer Rogers on why sales skill beats expertise:

> "Warren, it's not how you sell 'em, it's how you tell 'em." [[Sources/2016ltr]]

Channeling Gordon Gekko on manager compensation, Buffett writes simply: "Fees never sleep." [[Sources/2016ltr]]

### The Annual Meeting
Held Saturday, May 6, 2017, again webcast worldwide via Yahoo (the prior year's inaugural webcast drew 1.1 million live viewers and 11.5 million replays, cutting in-person attendance about 10% to roughly 37,000), with simultaneous Mandarin translation added for the first time.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Book value per share +10.7%; 52-year compounded annual gain of 19%
- **Acquisitions closed**: Precision Castparts and Duracell, both contributing a full year's earnings starting 2017
- **Insurance**: 14th consecutive year of underwriting profit; float grew to $91.577 billion (and past $100 billion shortly after year-end on one large policy)
- **New equity positions**: Apple, Delta, Southwest Airlines, and United Continental added to the top-holdings table
- **Share repurchase policy**: Authorized up to 120% of book value, a level the Board views as a clear discount to intrinsic value
- **"The Bet"**: Nine-year interim result overwhelmingly favors the S&P 500 index fund over funds-of-hedge-funds, with about 60% of the hedge funds' gains consumed by fees
