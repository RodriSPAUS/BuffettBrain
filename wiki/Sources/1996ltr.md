# 1996 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire achieved a remarkable gain in net worth of $6.2 billion (36.1%) in 1996, driven by strong performance across insurance operations, successful acquisitions of Kansas Bankers Surety and FlightSafety International, and outstanding results at GEICO following full acquisition.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Exceptional Performance and Growth
Berkshire's net worth grew by 36.1% ($6.2 billion) in 1996, while per-share book value increased by 31.8%. Over 32 years since present management took over, per-share book value has grown from $19 to $19,011, at a rate of 23.8% compounded annually. Buffett emphasized that while book value is a rough indicator, intrinsic value remains the true measure of economic progress.

> "Our gain in net worth during 1996 was $6.2 billion, or 36.1%. Per-share book value, however, grew by less, 31.8%, because the number of Berkshire shares increased: We issued stock in acquiring FlightSafety International and also sold new Class B shares." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "Over the last 32 years (that is, since present management took over) per-share book value has grown from $19 to $19,011, or at a rate of 23.8% compounded annually." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Accounting Technicalities and GEICO Acquisition Impact
A significant accounting adjustment occurred due to the full acquisition of GEICO. The 51% stake Berkshire previously held was written down when the remaining 49% was acquired, resulting in a $478.4 million reduction in book value. However, this was offset by issuing shares at premiums to book value in May (Class B shares) and December (for FlightSafety acquisition).

> "The restatement was required because GEICO became a wholly-owned subsidiary of Berkshire on January 2, 1996, whereas it was previously classified as an investment." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "From an economic viewpoint - taking into account major tax efficiencies and other benefits we gained - the value of the 51% of GEICO we owned at year-end 1995 increased significantly when we acquired the remaining 49% of the company two days later." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Acquisitions of High-Quality Businesses
Two significant acquisitions were made in 1996: Kansas Bankers Surety (KBS), an insurance company with an extraordinary underwriting record specializing in banker coverage ($75 million), and FlightSafety International, the world's leader in pilot training for $1.5 billion. Both acquisitions possessed "excellent business economics and an outstanding manager" fitting Berkshire's criteria perfectly.

> "We made two acquisitions in 1996 that fit our criteria perfectly." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "FlightSafety, the world's leader in computer-based training of pilots, is an extraordinary business." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Insurance Excellence and Float Generation
Berkshire's insurance business performed tremendously in 1996. The company generated substantial float at a low cost, with negative cost of funds in recent years meaning Berkshire was "paid for holding money." GEICO showed exceptional growth with voluntary auto policies growing 10%, surpassing previous records.

> "Berkshire's insurance business performed tremendously in 1996." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "In recent years, our cost of funds has been negative - meaning we were actually paid for holding money." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Market Price vs. Intrinsic Value Philosophy
Buffett addressed the relationship between market price and intrinsic value, noting that while Berkshire's stock underperformed the business in 1996 (with intrinsic value increasing significantly while market price changed little), the price/value relationship was now more appropriate than a year prior.

> "Though Berkshire's stock underperformed its business in 1996, intrinsic value increased significantly while market price changed little." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Managerial Philosophy and Hiring Practices
Buffett humorously addressed Berkshire's hiring practices, stating that the real explanation for hiring older executives was self-interest: "It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks."

> "An observer might conclude from our hiring practices that Charlie and I were traumatized early in life by an EEOC bulletin on age discrimination. The real explanation, however, is self-interest: It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Catastrophe Risk Management
Berkshire's position in catastrophe reinsurance was detailed, showing confidence in risk assessment. The company's after-tax "worst-case" loss from a true mega-catastrophe was estimated at no more than $600 million, which is less than 3% of book value and 1.5% of market value.

> "Large as these coverages are, Berkshire's after-tax 'worst-case' loss from a true mega-catastrophe is probably no more than $600 million, which is less than 3% of our book value and 1.5% of our market value." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Investment Approach and Concentration
Buffett emphasized the importance of concentration over diversification for informed investors, citing Ben Graham's teaching that significant investment results come from a few securities rather than many.

> "Most investors, of course, have no real knowledge of business dynamics. So they should own a market-index fund and get on with their lives." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "For the investor who has little time, interest or training to select individual securities, the right course is to own a market-index fund." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Net Worth Gain**: $6.2 billion, or 36.1%
- **Per-Share Book Value Growth**: 31.8% (from $19 to $19,011 over 32 years at 23.8% compounded annually)
- **Acquisition of FlightSafety International**: $1.5 billion for the world's leader in pilot training
- **Acquisition of Kansas Bankers Surety**: $75 million for insurance company specializing in banker coverage
- **GEICO Full Acquisition**: Completed full ownership of GEICO which became a wholly-owned subsidiary on January 2, 1996
- **Class B Shares**: Issued new Class B shares in May 1996 at a premium to book value
- **Investment Portfolio Performance**: Significant holdings in American Express (10.5%), Coca-Cola (8.1%), Disney (3.6%), Freddie Mac (8.4%), Gillette (8.6%), McDonald's (4.3%), Washington Post (15.8%), and Wells Fargo (8.0%)
