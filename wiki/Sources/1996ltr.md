# 1996 Annual Letter Summary

Net worth grew $6.2 billion (36.1%) in 1996; per-share book value grew 31.8%, held back by shares issued for the new Class B stock and the FlightSafety acquisition. The letter introduces the Class A / Class B share structure (created specifically to block "clone" unit trusts), completes the GEICO acquisition with a step-up accounting writedown explained in full, closes the USAir saga with a rare admission that a mistake worked out, and contains "The Inevitables" — the letter's lasting contribution on why a handful of businesses can be judged nearly certain over an investment lifetime.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The GEICO restatement, and Class A / Class B accounting

GEICO became a wholly-owned subsidiary on January 2, 1996; "step acquisition" accounting rules required Berkshire to write down the value of the 51% it already owned by $478.4 million once the remaining 49% was purchased, even though Buffett states the economic value of the original 51% had *increased* at the moment of full acquisition. [[Sources/1996ltr]] The Class B share, created in May 1996, carries 1/30th the economic interest and 1/200th the vote of a Class A share and is not eligible for the shareholder-designated contributions program; Berkshire states all per-share figures as "Class A equivalents."

Investments per share grew 29.0% and pre-tax operating earnings per share (excluding investment income) grew 63.2% in 1996 — headline numbers Buffett is careful to frame as unrepeatable given Berkshire's size: "Berkshire's equity capital is now large - in fact, fewer than ten businesses in America have capital larger - and an abundance of funds tends to dampen returns." [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] He restates the promise underlying all of it:

> "We do promise you, however, that virtually all of the gains Berkshire makes will end up with shareholders. We are here to make money with you, not off you." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Market price versus intrinsic value: overperformance corrects itself

Having warned a year earlier that Berkshire's stock price had run ahead of its intrinsic value, Buffett reports the opposite in 1996: intrinsic value rose sharply (helped by GEICO) while the market price barely moved, so "in 1996 Berkshire's stock underperformed the business." [[Sources/1996ltr]] He frames the fairness goal in partnership terms — when price and value diverge, some shareholders profit at others' expense, and management's job is to minimize that, not to chase a higher stock price for its own sake. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### Acquisitions found by accident: Kansas Bankers Surety and FlightSafety

Kansas Bankers Surety ($75 million) was found through a chance conversation at a birthday party — Roy Dinsdale mentioned the company in passing, and a follow-up letter closed the deal within a day of Buffett naming a price. [[Sources/1996ltr]] FlightSafety International ($1.5 billion, the world's leading pilot-training company) began when longtime shareholder Richard Sercer suggested the fit to Salomon's Bob Denham; Buffett met 79-year-old founder Al Ueltschi and closed within a month:

> "I had long been familiar with FlightSafety's business, and in about 60 seconds I knew that Al was exactly our kind of manager." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

Buffett notes the irony that Munger, who long mocked Buffett's use of Berkshire's jet ("The Indefensible"), "has recently experienced a 'counter-revelation'" now that FlightSafety trains corporate pilots for a living. [[Sources/1996ltr]] Both deals fit the age-blind hiring pattern Buffett names explicitly:

> "An observer might conclude from our hiring practices that Charlie and I were traumatized early in life by an EEOC bulletin on age discrimination. The real explanation, however, is self-interest: It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks." [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

### Insurance Operations: float past $6.7 billion, and a "certainty, not a possibility"

The cost-of-float table extends to 1996: average float of $6,702.0 million (up from $3,607.2 million, boosted by GEICO), again at negative cost, having compounded at 22.3% annually since 1967. [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/Float]] On the super-cat book, now including a roughly $1 billion Florida-hurricane exposure written with Allstate and an even larger California Earthquake Authority commitment, Buffett is blunter than in any prior letter:

> "A truly terrible year in the super-cat business is not a possibility - it's a certainty. The only question is when it will come." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

He quotes Robert Woodruff, who built Coca-Cola and never sold a share, on when to sell a wonderful business: "I don't know. I've never sold any." [[Sources/1996ltr]] and states the worst-case number directly:

> "Berkshire's after-tax "worst-case" loss from a true mega-catastrophe is probably no more than $600 million, which is less than 3% of our book value and 1.5% of our market value." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

National Indemnity's traditional book (Don Wurster) ran a 74.2 combined ratio; homestate (Rod Eldred) 87.1 despite state-expansion costs; workers' compensation (Brad Kinstler) expanded into six new states while staying profitable; Central States Indemnity (John Kizer) set new premium-volume records.

### GEICO: a moat widened by focused compensation

GEICO's voluntary auto policy count grew 10% in 1996 — its best year in the prior two decades had been 8%. Buffett credits GEICO's low-cost, direct-marketing structure (over one million annual policyholder referrals generating more than half of new business) and Tony Nicely's leadership:

> "Low costs permit low prices, and low prices attract and retain good policyholders." [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/Moat]]

Post-acquisition, Berkshire redesigned GEICO's compensation to tie bonuses for dozens of top executives to exactly two variables — voluntary policy growth and underwriting profitability on seasoned business — explicitly rejecting "lottery ticket" stock-option arrangements whose payoff is outside the recipient's control. [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] Lou Simpson's equity portfolio beat the S&P 500 by 6.2 points in 1996; his pay is tied to four-year investment performance, kept deliberately separate from underwriting results.

### Taxes: "I gave at the office"

Berkshire paid $860 million in federal income taxes for 1996 — Buffett notes that if 2,000 other taxpayers had matched that sum, the federal government would have run a balanced budget without collecting a dollar from anyone else in the country.

> "Berkshire shareholders can truly say, "I gave at the office."" [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "Charlie and I believe that large tax payments by Berkshire are entirely fitting. The contribution we thus make to society's well-being is at most only proportional to its contribution to ours." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Sources of Reported Earnings, and look-through earnings past $1.5 billion

The 1996 segment table (Berkshire's after-tax share, in millions) shows Insurance Underwriting $142.8, Insurance Net Investment Income $593.1, Buffalo News $29.5, Fechheimer $9.3, Finance Businesses $14.9, Home Furnishings $24.8, Jewelry $16.1, Kirby $39.9, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing $32.2, See's Candies $30.8, Shoe Group $41.0, World Book $9.5, plus Sales of Securities of $1,605.5, for Total Earnings — All Entities of $2,488.6 million versus $725.2 million in 1995. Buffalo News, Shoe Group and World Book — all flagged for declining earnings in 1995 — recovered in 1996; Helzberg's was the year's one disappointment, its expense base built for same-store growth that didn't materialize.

Look-through earnings reached $1,522 million, with American Express (10.5% owned, $132M undistributed), Coca-Cola (8.1%, $180M), Disney (3.6%, $50M, new to the table), Freddie Mac (8.4%, $77M), Gillette (8.6%, $73M), McDonald's (4.3%, $38M, new), Washington Post (15.8%, $27M), and Wells Fargo (8.0%, $84M).

### Common Stock Investments: "The Inevitables"

The over-$500-million common stock table at 12/31/96 (cost/market, in millions): American Express $1,392.7/$2,794.3, Coca-Cola $1,298.9/$10,525.0, Disney $577.0/$1,716.8, Freddie Mac $333.4/$1,772.8, Gillette $600.0/$3,732.0, McDonald's $1,265.3/$1,368.4 (new), Washington Post $10.6/$579.0, Wells Fargo $497.8/$1,966.9, plus $3,295.4 million of "Others," for total common stocks of $7,910.2 million cost / $27,750.6 million market.

Buffett names Coca-Cola and Gillette "The Inevitables" — businesses whose global dominance a decade or two out is close to certain, even though he can name only a handful of such companies after a lifetime of looking. He supports the label with an 1896 Coca-Cola report he tracked down, quoting company president Asa Candler:

> "We have not lagged in our efforts to go into all the world teaching that Coca-Cola is the article, par excellence, for the health and good feeling of all people." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

> "No article of like character has ever so firmly entrenched itself in public favor." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

He pairs the label with an explicit warning that even an Inevitable can be mismanaged into stagnation — noting Coke once grew shrimp and Gillette once explored for oil — and states the discipline that follows from the whole approach:

> "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes." [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]]

For most investors, though, the letter's advice is simpler: "the best way to own common stocks is through an index fund that charges minimal fees." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### USAir: the mistake that (barely) worked out

Buffett opens the section with Richard Branson's line on becoming a millionaire — "Start as a billionaire and then buy an airline" — and confesses his 1989 analysis of USAir was "both superficial and wrong": he was captivated by the company's profit history and the apparent safety of a senior security, and missed that deregulated revenue would collide with a regulated-era cost structure. [[Sources/1996ltr]] USAir lost $2.4 billion in aggregate from 1990-94, suspended its preferred dividend in 1994, and Berkshire wrote the $358 million position down 75% to $89.5 million — after which Buffett twice tried and failed to sell the stake, at 50% and later 84% of face value. [[Sources/1996ltr]]

The one thing Buffett says he got right: a penalty-dividend clause compounding unpaid arrearages at 5 points over prime, which gave USAir a strong incentive to catch up once it returned to profitability in late 1996, paying Berkshire $47.9 million. By the 1996 letter, USAir's securities implied the preferred was worth close to its $358 million par value, on top of $240.5 million of dividends collected over the years.

> "In another context, a friend once asked me: "If you're so rich, why aren't you smart?" After reviewing my sorry performance with USAir, you may conclude he had a point." [[Sources/1996ltr]]

### Financings: the B share, engineered to repel "flippers"

Berkshire's May 1996 sale of 517,500 Class B shares (net proceeds $565 million) was structured explicitly to make Berkshire-clone unit trusts "unmerchandisable" — the same threat named in the 1995 letter. The offering carried only a 1.5% commission (deliberately unattractive to brokers) and was left open-ended in size to avoid the artificial scarcity that draws short-term flippers; trading volume immediately after the offering came in far below normal for a new issue, and about 40,000 new shareholders were added. [[Sources/1996ltr]] Late in the year, Salomon Brothers — thanked by name for consistently prioritizing Berkshire's goals over its own fee income — placed $500 million of five-year notes exchangeable into Salomon shares, at an effective interest cost of about 1.1% if exchanged before maturity.

### Miscellaneous: Berkshire goes online

Berkshire announced it would begin posting quarterly and annual reports at berkshirehathaway.com, always on a Saturday to give shareholders time to digest the numbers before markets opened, with an eye toward eventually dropping mailed quarterlies to cut costs.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### GEICO — full ownership, restated

The step-acquisition accounting writedown ($478.4 million) is a technical artifact, not an economic loss; Buffett states the 51% Berkshire already owned became more, not less, valuable the moment full ownership closed.

### Kansas Bankers Surety and FlightSafety International

$75 million and roughly $1.5 billion respectively, both negotiated within weeks of first contact, both retaining their founder-managers (Don Towle, Al Ueltschi) running day-to-day operations.

### The portfolio, again mostly unchanged

McDonald's and Disney are the only new entries among the largest holdings; American Express, Coca-Cola, Freddie Mac, Gillette, Washington Post and Wells Fargo positions carried over unchanged from 1995.

### USAir preferred, close to recovered

Marked near its $358 million par value by yearend, after a 75% writedown in 1994 and two failed attempts by Buffett to sell out at a loss.
