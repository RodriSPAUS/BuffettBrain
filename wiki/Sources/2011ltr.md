# 2011 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 4.6%, a modest year Buffett frames honestly against
the S&P's stronger showing. The letter's structural centerpiece is an
extended defense of share repurchases and a related essay on why a
long-term owner of a repurchasing company — using IBM as the worked
example — should root for that company's stock to stay cheap. A second major
essay — titled "The Basic Choices for Investors and the One We Strongly
Prefer" — sorts all investments into three categories and works a striking
thought experiment
comparing the entire world's gold stock to all U.S. cropland plus sixteen
Exxon Mobils. The investment-succession bench is now two deep with Todd
Combs and Ted Weschler, and CEO succession is described as settled in all but
name. Buffett also confesses a "big mistake" in Energy Future Holdings bonds
and admits his prior-year housing-recovery call was "dead wrong."

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Succession: two investment managers, and a CEO handoff called seamless

> "As 2011 started, Todd Combs joined us as an investment manager, and
> shortly after yearend Ted Weschler came aboard." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

Both would initially manage a few billion dollars each, with Buffett stating
plainly that either has "the brains, judgment and character to manage our
entire portfolio" once he and Charlie are no longer running Berkshire. On the
CEO question, the board's process is described as complete rather than
pending:

> "When a transfer of responsibility is required, it will be seamless, and
> Berkshire's prospects will remain bright." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

More than 98% of Buffett's net worth remains in Berkshire stock, all
earmarked for philanthropy — a concentration he acknowledges "defies
conventional wisdom" but accepts given his confidence in the businesses and
managers left behind. See [[People/WarrenBuffett]].

### Lubrizol, and the "fabulous five"

Lubrizol, a specialty-chemicals producer, was acquired September 16; under
CEO James Hambrick since 2004, its pre-tax profits had grown from $147
million to $1.085 billion, with three bolt-on acquisitions already agreed for
$493 million. Together with BNSF, Iscar, Marmon and MidAmerican Energy,
Lubrizol rounds out what Buffett calls Berkshire's "fabulous five" — each of
which set a record in 2011, combining for more than $9 billion pre-tax:

> "Contrast that to seven years ago, when we owned only one of the five,
> MidAmerican, whose pre-tax earnings were $393 million." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

Capital spending across all of Berkshire's operating companies hit $8.2
billion — "smashing our previous record by more than $2 billion" — with about
95% of it domestic.

### Two confessed mistakes: Energy Future Holdings, and a wrong housing call

> "A few years back, I spent about $2 billion buying several bond issues of
> Energy Future Holdings, an electric utility operation serving portions of
> Texas. That was a mistake – a big mistake." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

Tied to natural-gas prices that collapsed after purchase, the position was
written down $1 billion in 2010 and a further $390 million in 2011, carried
at $878 million against Buffett's own verdict: "this was a major unforced
error by your chairman." Separately, the housing call from
[[Sources/2010ltr]] is retracted directly:

> "Last year, I told you that 'a housing recovery will probably begin within
> a year or so.' I was dead wrong." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

Five housing-linked businesses (Clayton plus Acme Brick, Shaw, Johns Manville
and MiTek) earned $513 million pre-tax combined in 2011, similar to 2010 but
down from $1.8 billion in 2006, with employment at 43,315 against 58,769 in
2006. Buffett's underlying argument is demographic rather than cyclical:
household formation has outpaced housing starts every day since the bubble
burst, so the imbalance must eventually reverse — "eventually hormones take
over."

### Intrinsic value, share repurchases, and IBM's arithmetic

Buffett restates book value as the working proxy for intrinsic value and
notes the 42 five-year rolling comparisons from [[Sources/2010ltr]] held
again through 2007-11, though he flags that the streak "will almost certainly
snap" if the S&P puts together its own five-year winning run. Per-share
investments rose 4% to $98,366; non-insurance pre-tax earnings per share rose
18% to $6,990.

Berkshire itself became a repurchaser for the first time in this era, buying
$67 million of stock at up to 110% of book value before the price moved
beyond that limit. Buffett states the two-part test for a legitimate
repurchase:

> "First, a company has ample funds to take care of the operational and
> liquidity needs of its business; second, its stock is selling at a material
> discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively
> calculated." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

> "The first law of capital allocation – whether the money is slated for
> acquisitions or share repurchases – is that what is smart at one price is
> dumb at another." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

IBM becomes the letter's central worked example of a paradox: a long-term
shareholder in a company that is aggressively repurchasing stock should hope
the stock price stays *low*, not high, since a lower price lets the same
dollar amount retire more shares. Buffett runs the numbers — a $200 average
price over five years leaves Berkshire owning about 7% of IBM after $50
billion of buybacks; a $300 average price leaves it owning only 6.5% — and
states the conclusion plainly:

> "I won't keep you in suspense. We should wish for IBM's stock price to
> languish throughout the five years." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

He traces his own reversal on this point to reading Chapter Eight of Ben
Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*: "Immediately the scales fell from my
eyes, and low prices became my friend." And he sets a standing bet for
Berkshire employees: "if repurchases ever reduce the IBM shares outstanding
to 63.9 million, I will abandon my famed frugality and give Berkshire
employees a paid holiday." See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

Nine consecutive years of underwriting profit had produced a cumulative $17

carried as a full liability on the balance sheet as if due tomorrow, when in

Ajit Jain's operation reached $34 billion of float from its 1985 standing

Jersey medical-malpractice writer, was folded into Tim Kenesey's Medical

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses: a Franklin line updated

BNSF and MidAmerican remain a combined reporting sector. BNSF's interest
coverage held at 9.5x in a soft 2011 economy, moving about 37% of the U.S.
rail industry's ton-miles — roughly 15% of all inter-city freight ton-miles
by any mode — across 23,000 miles of track, 13,000 bridges, 80 tunnels, 6,900
locomotives and 78,600 freight cars, while absorbing widespread flooding that
summer. Buffett adapts a Ben Franklin line to the regulatory relationship:

> "Keep thy shop, and thy shop will keep thee." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

> "Take care of your customer, and the regulator – your customer's
> representative – will take care of you." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

MidAmerican's wind capacity was set to reach 3,316 megawatts by end of 2012
($6 billion invested or committed), plus new solar projects in California and
Arizona costing about $3 billion; Northern Natural's pipeline ranking rose
from dead-last (43rd of 43) shortly after its 2002 purchase to second, behind
Kern River, and MidAmerican's U.S. utilities ranked second of 60 groups in
the most recent customer-satisfaction survey. BNSF and MidAmerican together
carry $20 billion of goodwill on the balance sheet that Buffett judges well
below their combined intrinsic value. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: Rule 11, and the housing/non-housing split

Buffett restates why Berkshire keeps underperforming subsidiaries rather than
selling them — "Rule 11" of the economic principles reprinted each year —
provided they aren't a structural cash drain or beset by labor strife:

> "We have made – and continue to make – a commitment to the sellers of
> businesses we buy that we will retain those businesses through thick and
> thin." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

Stripping out the four housing-linked companies from this sector (Clayton is
reported separately under Finance), the remaining non-housing operations
earned $1,831 million (2009), $3,912 million (2010) and $4,678 million
(2011) — a clean read on the broader recovery. Record-setters: CTB (Vic
Mancinelli, $124 million pre-tax against a $139 million 2002 purchase price,
$180 million already distributed to Berkshire); TTI (Paul Andrews, $2.1
billion sales, up 12.4%, earnings up 127% since the 2007 purchase); Iscar's
Tungaloy unit, which set a sales record despite tsunami damage the prior
spring; and McLane (Grady Rosier), $370 million pre-tax, $2.4 billion
cumulative since the 2003 purchase. NetJets, under new CEO Jordan Hansell
(who took over in April), delivered $227 million pre-tax — a full recovery
from "my number one worry" a few years earlier — while beginning a China
expansion. Marmon (Frank Ptak) grew earnings in ten of its eleven business
sectors and formed a second joint venture with India's Kundalia family.

**See's** posted record pre-tax earnings of $83 million, bringing its
cumulative total since the 1972 purchase to $1.65 billion against a $25
million purchase price and a yearend carrying value net of cash *below
zero*. Buffett names the formula directly:

> "'Buy commodities, sell brands' has long been a formula for business
> success." — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

He cites Coca-Cola since 1886 and Wrigley since 1891 alongside See's. See
[[Cases/SeeCandies]].

**Nebraska Furniture Mart** set an earnings record — more than ten times its
1983 level, when Berkshire bought in — and acquired a 433-acre Dallas tract
for what will likely become the country's highest-volume home-furnishings
store. Buffett tells Rose "Mrs. B" Blumkin's story directly: she started NFM
in 1937 with $500, sold Berkshire her stake at 89, worked until 103, and died
the year after retiring — "a sequence I point out to any other Berkshire
manager who even thinks of retiring." Her son Louie and grandsons Ron and Irv
carried the business forward; a fourth generation had by 2011 joined as well.

A GAAP quirk is flagged as a footnote: a scheduled 2011 purchase of an
additional 16% of Marmon under the original Pritzker formula required
Berkshire to retroactively write off $614 million against 2010 — a paper
loss Buffett calls disconnected from economic reality. See
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Finance and Financial Products: leasing recovers, Clayton stays in "the emergency room"

XTRA and CORT's combined pre-tax earnings rose from $13 million (2009) to
$53 million (2010) to $155 million (2011), tracking the broader recovery.
Clayton's world, by contrast, showed no recovery: manufactured-housing sales
nationally were 49,789 (2009), 50,046 (2010) and 51,606 (2011) units, against
146,744 at the 2005 peak. Buffett's account of the housing bubble names every
participant complicit — "government, lenders, borrowers, the media, rating
agencies, you name it" — built on the shared belief that home prices could
only rise, and adds an overlooked detail:

> "Large numbers of people who have 'lost' their house through foreclosure
> have actually realized a profit because they carried out refinancings
> earlier that gave them cash in excess of their cost. In these cases, the
> evicted homeowner was the winner, and the victim was the lender."
> — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

### Derivatives: winding down, and a new rule that ends new positions

A change in collateral-posting rules for new contracts led Buffett to state
plainly that Berkshire will not initiate major new derivatives positions
going forward, since any requirement for sudden, large collateral postings
conflicts with the redundant-liquidity discipline from
[[Sources/2010ltr]] and [[Sources/2011ltr]]'s Life-and-Debt lineage. The
high-yield credit-default book was nearly closed out: $2.6 billion of
cumulative losses paid against $3.4 billion of premiums received, an
underwriting profit "almost certain" once the remaining exposure runs off.
The equity-put book carried a $8.5 billion balance-sheet liability at
yearend against a $6.2 billion settlement value had all contracts come due
that day — plus $4.2 billion of float already held more than five years,
expected to run fifteen or more.

### The Basic Choices for Investors and the One We Strongly Prefer

Buffett's fullest taxonomy of investment types, built on a definition of
investing as "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the
reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power" later, and a
redefinition of risk as purchasing-power loss rather than price volatility.

**Currency-based assets** (bonds, cash, money-market funds) are labeled the
most dangerous category despite appearing safest: since 1965 the dollar has
lost 86% of its value, meaning a tax-free institution needed 4.3% annual
interest just to break even in real terms, while a taxable investor rolling
Treasury bills at an average 5.7% nominal return over the same 47 years
earned essentially nothing after both income tax and the "invisible inflation
tax":

> "'In God We Trust' may be imprinted on our currency, but the hand that
> activates our government's printing press has been all too human."
> — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

**Unproductive assets**, chiefly gold, are the second category — valuable
only because buyers expect other buyers to want them more later. Buffett's
thought experiment: at $1,750/oz, the world's roughly 170,000 metric tons of
gold (a cube about 68 feet per side) was worth $9.6 trillion — enough to buy
every acre of U.S. cropland (400 million acres) plus sixteen Exxon Mobils,
with $1 trillion left over:

> "Can you imagine an investor with $9.6 trillion selecting pile A over pile
> B?" — [[Sources/2011ltr]]

A century on, he argues, the cropland will still be producing crops and
Exxon still paying dividends, while "the 170,000 tons of gold will be
unchanged in size and still incapable of producing anything. You can fondle
the cube, but it will not respond."

**Productive assets** — businesses, farms, real estate — are Buffett's
stated preference, judged by their ability to deliver output whose value
holds up under inflation without heavy new capital investment. He notes both
extremes of crowd sentiment as warning signs, not signals: "We heard 'cash is
king' in late 2008, just when cash should have been deployed rather than
held. Similarly, we heard 'cash is trash' in the early 1980s just when
fixed-dollar investments were at their most attractive level in memory."
Coca-Cola, IBM and See's are named as businesses passing the productive-asset
test; regulated utilities are named as an example that partly fails it,
since inflation forces them to invest more capital just to earn more. See
[[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Lubrizol** — specialty chemicals producer, closed September 16; three
  bolt-on acquisitions worth $493 million agreed the same year.
- **Princeton Insurance** — New Jersey medical-malpractice writer, folded
  into Medical Protective, bringing more than $600 million of float.
- **Bank of America** — $5 billion of 6% preferred stock plus warrants on
  700 million common shares at $7.14, exercisable to September 2, 2021.
- **IBM** — 63.9 million shares for $10.9 billion, Berkshire's largest new
  equity position of the year.
- Added $1 billion to the existing Wells Fargo position.

**Sold / written down**

- Energy Future Holdings bonds written down a further $390 million (on top
  of $1 billion in 2010), carried at $878 million yearend.
- Swiss Re, Goldman Sachs and General Electric redeemed $12.8 billion of
  fixed-income securities that had produced about $1.2 billion of annual
  pre-tax earnings.

**Corporate**

- Repurchased $67 million of Berkshire stock at up to 110% of book value
  before the price moved past the limit.
- Todd Combs (full year) and Ted Weschler (from early 2012) both managing
  multi-billion-dollar portfolios independently.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew to $70.571 billion at a $248 million underwriting profit, the
  ninth consecutive profitable year.
- MidAmerican committed to $6 billion of wind investment and roughly $3
  billion of new solar projects.
