# 2018 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

The first meeting after Ajit Jain and Greg Abel were named Vice Chairmen, opening with Buffett's account of buying 3 shares of Cities Service Preferred as an 11-year-old on March 11, 1942 — a $10,000 investment in the S&P 500 that day would be worth $51 million by 2018, versus about $400,000 for the same sum in gold. From there the meeting ranges across the newly announced Berkshire-Amazon-JPMorgan healthcare venture (where Buffett first uses the word "tapeworm" for U.S. health costs, a metaphor he would return to for years), a new mark-to-market accounting rule Buffett calls "enormously deceptive" if read as operating results, the Wells Fargo scandal graded against Berkshire's own past crises at American Express and GEICO, cryptocurrency dismissed by both men in blunt terms, and a formal meeting where two shareholder proposals on methane disclosure and sustainability reporting both failed.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The 1942 Cities Service Story: $10,000 Becomes $51 Million
Buffett walks through buying 3 shares of Cities Service Preferred on March 11, 1942 during the darkest days of the Pacific war, selling too early for a $5 profit, and then poses the alternative: a hypothetical $10,000 invested in the S&P 500 that same day and never touched again.

> "You'd have $51 million, and you wouldn't have had to do anything." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

The same $10,000 in gold, held and never productive, would be worth about $400,000 — "for every dollar you have made in American business, you would have less than $0.01 by...of gain by buying" gold instead. The lesson is framed as the meeting's overriding theme: judge every question that follows by how American business will do over an investing lifetime, not by that day's headlines.

### Semi-Retirement, and Where Ted, Todd, Ajit and Greg Fit
Asked whether delegating manager oversight to Ajit and Greg, and investing to Ted Weschler and Todd Combs, makes Buffett "semiretired," he says he has been semiretired for decades, but breaks down the numbers: Ted and Todd together manage $25 billion, versus roughly $300 billion still under Buffett's own direct responsibility (equities, bonds and cash). Munger's assessment of Buffett's actual daily activity is characteristically dry: "Warren is very good at doing nothing." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

### Precision Castparts and the Mark Donegan Endorsement
Two years after the acquisition, Buffett defends Precision Castparts' long lead times and reliability-driven aerospace contracts, crediting CEO Mark Donegan by name as a manager "I wouldn't have bought it without him in charge, he also has been very helpful to us in other areas, and he loves to do it." Munger's endorsement is characteristically brief:

> "Well, I think we buy another one just like it tomorrow if we had the chance." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

### Trade with China: "A Win-Win Situation"
Asked about the U.S.-China trade tensions, Buffett argues both countries benefit enormously from trade, citing U.S. exports rising from 5% of GDP in 1970 to over 11% while imports reached about 14.5% — a gap he says shouldn't be allowed to widen too far, but is manageable. Munger attributes China's faster growth simply to starting from a lower base plus a high savings rate, and both express confidence that "both nations will be smart enough to realize that the last thing they should do is having the wealth of the other" — the alternative being, in Buffett's words, that "2 intelligent countries will do something extremely foolish." Later, asked directly about tariffs, Buffett and Munger both downplay the risk of a sustained trade war, with Munger noting conditions in the American steel industry had become "almost unbelievably adverse" — a rare point of agreement with tariff proponents.

### Wells Fargo: "A Cardinal Sin," Graded Against Berkshire's Own History
Asked whether Wells Fargo's fake-account scandal makes it a "chronically leaking boat" Berkshire should abandon, Buffett distinguishes the original bad incentive system from the deeper failure of not fixing it once discovered:

> "But if ignoring the fact that they had a faulty incentive system, which was incenting people to do things that were kind of crazy like opening nonexisting accounts, et cetera, and that is a cardinal sin at Berkshire." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

He draws direct parallels to two of his own best investments recovering from institutional scandals: American Express's 1963 salad-oil warehousing fraud (bought into afterward, at what became "the best investment I ever made in my partnership years") and GEICO's 1976 near-bankruptcy from under-reserving, both companies emerging stronger. Munger, notably, calls Wells Fargo possibly "more likely to behave the best in the future" of any big bank precisely because of how acutely embarrassed it was.

### Cyber Risk: "We Don't Know What We're Doing"
Asked about a potential $400 billion mega-catastrophe from cyber risk, on top of the figure already discussed in that year's letter, Buffett is candid about the limits of actuarial knowledge in an unprecedented risk category:

> "And that's why that's one of the reasons I'd say that a $400 billion event has a -- I think, has roughly a 2% probability per year event of happening." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

He says Berkshire deliberately avoids being the #1, #2 or #3 writer of cyber exposure precisely because nobody — Berkshire included — understands the aggregation risk or how courts will interpret ambiguous policy language.

### The New Mark-to-Market Accounting Rule: "Enormously Deceptive"
A new accounting rule taking effect in 2018 requires unrealized gains and losses on equity holdings to run through the income statement daily. Responding to an accounting expert's challenge that this simply reflects reality, Buffett insists operating earnings, not net income, are what matters:

> "I think it would be -- well, I can say it would be enormously deceptive." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

Munger is characteristically blunter about the questioner: "the questioner doesn't understand his own profession." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]] This is the same rule change discussed at length in the 2021 conference call.

### The Haven Venture, and the First "Tapeworm"
Announcing the still-unnamed CEO search for the Berkshire-Amazon-JPMorgan healthcare partnership (later named Haven), Buffett introduces the metaphor that recurs in every conference call for years afterward:

> "It is a tapeworm in terms of American business and its competitiveness." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

He cites U.S. health spending near 18% of GDP (up from 5% in 1960, versus per-capita costs of $170 in 1960 to over $10,000 by 2018) as the target, while being explicit the goal is not profit but lower costs for the three companies' own employees. Munger compares the effort to John D. Rockefeller's early philanthropic push to improve American medicine.

### Cryptocurrency: "It Will Come to a Bad Ending"
Asked why crypto is a bubble, Buffett applies his productive-versus-nonproductive-asset framework directly:

> "And you can get that and it will feed on itself for a while and sometimes for a long while and sometimes, to extraordinary numbers but in the end -- but they come to bad endings, and crypto currencies will come to bad endings." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

Munger is unrestrained:

> "And so to me, it's just dementia and I think the people who are rational traders, they go into trading crypto currencies, it's just disgusting." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

> "It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide I can't be left out." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

### Culture, Decentralization, and "Bureaucracy Is a Cancer"
Discussing zero-based budgeting and 3G's cost-cutting at Kraft Heinz, Buffett notes Berkshire has never had a consolidated monthly budget and runs headquarters with about 30 people, half of them internal auditors. Munger states the underlying philosophy plainly:

> "I think a bureaucracy is sort of like a cancer, and it functions sort of like a cancer." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

Both men tie this directly to why Berkshire attracts sellers: the promise of being left alone, not just capital.

### Munger's Costco, and the Limits of a Formula
Asked to reduce intrinsic-value investing to a formula, Munger points to Costco — a stock he personally held and championed while it traded around 12-13x earnings — as an example that resisted quantification: "I liked the cheap real estate. I liked the competitive position. I liked the way the personnel system work. I liked everything about it." He is explicit there is no reducible method: "if you want a formula, you should go back to graduate school. They'll give you lots of formulas that won't work." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]] Buffett notes this is the longest stretch in memory without Munger volunteering that he prefers Costco to Berkshire itself.

### Missing Google and Amazon
Asked why Berkshire never bought Google or Amazon despite recognizing their durable advantages, Buffett is unusually direct about the miss:

> "I made the wrong decision on Google and Amazon." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

He credits Jeff Bezos with something "close to a miracle" — building Amazon Web Services and transforming retail simultaneously without enormous capital — and says he underestimated Bezos's ability from their first meeting. He contrasts this with Apple, where the decision rested on consumer-ecosystem durability rather than technical understanding of the product.

### Capital-Light Preference, and Why Berkshire Bought a Railroad Anyway
An 8-year-old shareholder asks why Berkshire moved from capital-efficient businesses (Coca-Cola, See's, GEICO, American Express) into capital-hungry ones like BNSF. Buffett's answer is a capacity constraint, not a change in philosophy:

> "The answer is that we have -- we'd love -- we always prefer the businesses that earned terrific returns on capital... we can't get more money deployed in capital-light businesses at prices that make sense to us, and so we have gone into more capital-intensive businesses that are good businesses." [[Sources/Conference_call_2018]]

Munger frames Berkshire Hathaway Energy's and BNSF's regulated returns as "quite satisfactory" precisely because of their scale, wishing Berkshire had two more businesses just like them.

### Newspapers: A Societal Loss, Not an Economic One
Reviewing five years of circulation data he included in that year's letter, Buffett says the decline of daily newspapers (about 1,300 remaining, down from 1,700) has not moderated and that only the Wall Street Journal, New York Times and (probably) Washington Post have found a viable digital model. He calls the economic impact on Berkshire "almost negligible" but the societal impact "enormous," and neither he nor Munger has found a fix.

### The Formal Meeting: Directors Elected, Two Proposals Fail
The meeting convened with 748,347 Class A and 1,344,969,701 Class B shares outstanding. All 14 director nominees, including Buffett, Munger, Abel and Jain, were elected with at least 605,906 votes each. Two shareholder proposals from Freeda Cathcart on behalf of Baldwin Brothers both failed after the board's recommendation against: a methane-emissions disclosure report (48,040 for / 558,640 against), during which Greg Abel presented data showing Berkshire Hathaway Energy's pipeline throughput loss at 0.046% against a 1% industry target; and a sustainability-reporting encouragement policy (67,282 for / 544,256 against).

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Q1 2018 operating earnings**: a record for any quarter, aided by the federal corporate tax rate cut from 35% to 21%
- **New mark-to-market accounting rule**: unrealized equity gains/losses now run through the income statement daily, which Buffett calls "enormously deceptive" if read as operating performance
- **Cyber catastrophe estimate**: roughly a 2% annual probability of a $400 billion mega-catastrophe event, with Berkshire deliberately avoiding being a top-3 writer of cyber exposure
- **AIG retroactive reinsurance deal**: $10 billion premium received for assuming losses on AIG's pre-2016 business, the largest such deal in history at the time
- **Phillips 66**: shares sold back to stay just under the 10% regulatory-disclosure ownership threshold, consistent with the same policy applied to Wells Fargo and the airlines
- **Google and Amazon**: explicitly named as missed investments ("I made the wrong decision")
- **Formal meeting**: all 14 director nominees elected; methane-disclosure and sustainability-reporting shareholder proposals both failed
