# 1984 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1984: net worth up $152.6 million, 13.6% — a number Buffett opens by
calling mediocre. The letter contains four set pieces that outlast the year: the
case for buybacks and the contempt for greenmail, a full accounting of Berkshire's
own loss-reserving errors, the treatment of a bond as a business (WPPSS), and the
dividend-policy essay separating restricted from unrestricted earnings.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A good-looking number, called mediocre in the first paragraph

- "Our gain in net worth during 1984 was $152.6 million, or $133 per share. This
  sounds pretty good but actually it's mediocre. Economic gains must be evaluated
  by comparison with the capital that produces them."

Twenty years of compounding at 22.1% had taken book value from $19.46 to
$1,108.77; the year itself managed 13.6%. Buffett marks the change of register
himself: "Using my academic voice, I have told you in the past of the drag that a
mushrooming capital base exerts upon rates of return. Unfortunately, my academic
voice is now giving way to a reportorial voice. Our historical 22% rate is just
that - history."

The arithmetic that follows becomes the recurring constraint of every later
letter: earning even 15% annually over the next decade would require profits
aggregating about $3.9 billion. "Accomplishing this will require a few big ideas -
small ones just won't do." And the plan, stated without pretence: "Charlie Munger,
my partner in general management, and I do not have any such ideas at present, but
our experience has been that they pop up occasionally."

On the market: "It's been over ten years since it has been as difficult as now to
find equity investments that meet both our qualitative standards and our
quantitative standards of value versus price. We try to avoid compromise of these
standards, although we find doing nothing the most difficult task of all."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### Buybacks — the arithmetic reason and the reason nobody states

GEICO and General Foods both repurchased stock while Berkshire sold back
proportionately, keeping its percentage unchanged. Buffett uses those transactions
to make the general case, and it has two halves.

The obvious half is arithmetic: "major repurchases at prices well below per-share
intrinsic business value immediately increase, in a highly significant way, that
value. When companies purchase their own stock, they often find it easy to get $2
of present value for $1. Corporate acquisition programs almost never do as well
and, in a discouragingly large number of cases, fail to get anything close to $1
of value for each $1 expended."

The subtle half is a signal about the manager, and it is the more interesting
claim: "By making repurchases when a company's market value is well below its
business value, management clearly demonstrates that it is given to actions that
enhance the wealth of shareholders, rather than to actions that expand
management's domain but that do nothing for (or even harm) shareholders." The
market then rationally revalues the assets in that manager's hands — "Investors
should pay more for a business that is lodged in the hands of a manager with
demonstrated pro-shareholder leanings than for one in the hands of a
self-interested manager marching to a different drummer."

Read in reverse, it becomes a diagnostic: "A manager who consistently turns his
back on repurchases, when these clearly are in the interests of owners, reveals
more than he knows of his motivations." Public-relations language does not fix it:
"His heart is not listening to his mouth - and, after a while, neither will the
market."

Berkshire's endorsement is explicitly bounded by price, and the exclusion is
written with unusual heat. Greenmail is called odious and repugnant, a transaction
in which "two parties achieve their personal ends by exploitation of an innocent
and unconsulted third party" — the extortionist, "the corporate insiders who
quickly seek peace at any price - as long as the price is paid by someone else",
and the shareholders whose money funds the payoff.

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### The accounting dispute, reported against Berkshire's own presentation

The GEICO and General Foods redemptions had been booked as dividend income, with
the Omaha office and the Chicago reviewing partner of Peat Marwick concurring.
Late in 1984 the New York office disagreed and required them to be shown as stock
sales.

Buffett does three things worth noting: he restates prior years rather than
fighting for a qualified opinion, he says plainly that he still disagrees "from
both the viewpoint of economic substance and proper accounting", and he separates
presentation from reality — "None of this, however, has any effect on intrinsic
business value: our ownership interests in GEICO and General Foods, our cash, our
taxes, and the market value and tax basis of our holdings all remain the same."

Tax treatment was unaffected: the $21 million from GEICO and $21,843,601 from
General Foods were dividends for IRS purposes, taxed at the 6.9% inter-corporate
rate.

See [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Nebraska Furniture Mart — the cost advantage, quantified against a competitor

The 1983 introduction is upgraded: "After another year of observing their
remarkable talents and character, I can honestly say that I never have seen a
managerial group that either functions or behaves better than the Blumkin family."

Sales rose $14.3 million to $115 million, all from the one Omaha store — the
year's *increase* exceeding the annual volume of many successful stores. Buffett
then does something he rarely does and names a public comparison, which makes the
moat measurable:

- Levitz Furniture, the largest independent specialty retailer of home
  furnishings, described its prices as generally lower than conventional furniture
  stores in its trading area. "Levitz, in that year, operated at a gross margin of
  44.4% (that is, on average, customers paid it $100 for merchandise that had cost
  it $55.60 to buy). The gross margin at NFM is not much more than half of that.
  NFM's low mark-ups are possible because of its exceptional efficiency: operating
  expenses (payroll, occupancy, advertising, etc.) are about 16.5% of sales versus
  35.6% at Levitz."
- Where the advantage goes: "By unparalleled efficiency and astute volume
  purchasing, NFM is able to earn excellent returns on capital while saving its
  customers at least $30 million annually from what, on average, it would cost them
  to buy the same merchandise at stores maintaining typical mark-ups."

That is the whole mechanism of a durable low-cost position in two sentences — the
savings are handed to the customer, which is what keeps the position from being
competed away, and it funds expansion beyond Omaha's natural growth. "The business
achieves this success because it deserves this success."

The four family habits Buffett lists amount to a character checklist, and two of
them are the circle of competence in plain language: "(2) define with extraordinary
realism their area of special competence and act decisively on all matters within
it; (3) ignore even the most enticing propositions failing outside of that area of
special competence".

And the transaction itself, the most-quoted passage in the letter:

- "NFM had never had an audit and we did not request one; we did not take an
  inventory nor verify the receivables; we did not check property titles. We gave
  Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even
  exchange."

Mrs. B, at 91, was at the store seven days a week and "probably makes more
decisions in a day than most CEOs do in a year (better ones, too)." NYU gave her an
honorary doctorate in commercial science, having never spent a day in a classroom.

See [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]], [[Concepts/Moat]] and
[[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### The economics of a dominant newspaper, stated against Berkshire's interest

The Buffalo News section contains the most unsentimental thing Buffett wrote about
a business he owned and admired:

- "The economics of a dominant newspaper are excellent, among the very best in the
  business world. Owners, naturally, would like to believe that their wonderful
  profitability is achieved only because they unfailingly turn out a wonderful
  product. That comfortable theory wilts before an uncomfortable fact. While
  first-class newspapers make excellent profits, the profits of third-rate papers
  are as good or better - as long as either class of paper is dominant within its
  community."
- "Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how
  good or how bad the paper will be. Good or bad, it will prosper. That is not true
  of most businesses: inferior quality generally produces inferior economics."

Quality mattered in *achieving* dominance, and after that the discipline has to be
self-imposed: "Since high standards are not imposed by the marketplace, management
must impose its own." Berkshire's quantitative version of that standard was the
news hole, held at 50.9% in 1984 against a typical 35–40%, with newsroom costs
rising 9.1% against an overall cost increase of 4.9%.

The competitive economics are spelled out too: circulation concentrated where
advertisers want it, since "expenses of a newspaper are determined largely by gross
circulation while advertising revenues (usually 70% - 80% of total revenues) are
responsive only to useful circulation". The News ranked first in weekday
penetration among the 100 largest US papers and second on Sunday.

Costs were well managed — total hours worked down about 2.8% excluding the
newsroom, overall costs up only 4.9% — but Buffett warns margins will contract in
1985 on a union catch-up increase he calls "entirely appropriate", given the
unions' cooperation through the unprofitable 1977–1982 fight with the
Courier-Express.

See [[Cases/BuffaloNews]] and [[Concepts/Moat]].

### See's — flat volume, and the things that will not be traded away

Sales of $135.9 million, after-tax operating profit of $13.4 million, 214 shops.
Per-pound realization rose only 1.4% to $5.49 and per-pound costs other than raw
materials rose only 2.2% — the cost problem flagged in [[Sources/1983ltr]] was
being fixed. Same-store volume still declined 1.1%.

The seasonality is extreme and worsening: "In the four weeks prior to Christmas, we
do 40% of the year's volume and earn about 75% of the year's profits." The
industry's answer to that peak is preservatives and freezing. Berkshire's is not:
"We reject such techniques, opting, in effect, for production headaches rather than
product modification."

Buffett is careful to say the record is not a rising tide: many well-known
boxed-chocolate competitors lost money or were marginally profitable over the same
period. "The success of See's reflects the combination of an exceptional product
and an exceptional manager, Chuck Huggins."

See [[Cases/SeeCandies]].

### Insurance — a humbling 134, and strength as the strategy

- "Our own combined ratio in 1984 was a humbling 134." Against an industry 117.7,
  and the third consecutive year far poorer than the industry.

Against that, the balance sheet: "We are almost without question the strongest
property/casualty insurance operation in the country, with a capital position far
superior to that of well-known companies of much greater size." Buffett states the
policy that makes it a strategy rather than an accident — "our corporate policy is
to retain that superiority" — and gives the reason in the form of what an insurance
policy actually is:

- "The buyer of insurance receives only a promise in exchange for his cash. The
  value of that promise should be appraised against the possibility of adversity,
  not prosperity."

Structured settlements and loss-reserve assumption grew accordingly, with funds
held against assumed liabilities rising from $16.2 million to $30.6 million, and
capital added to Columbia Insurance Company. The day predicted since
[[Sources/1981ltr]] may have arrived: "For some years I have told you that there
could be a day coming when our premier financial strength would make a real
difference in the competitive position of our insurance operation. That day may
have arrived."

See [[Concepts/Float]].

### Errors in loss reserving — the most self-incriminating section in the corpus

Buffett devotes a full section to showing that his own reported underwriting
figures had been wrong, in the same direction, every year. The table restates each
year's reported result against what it looked like one year later:

| Year | as reported to you | corrected after one year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1980 | $6,738,000 | $14,887,000 |
| 1981 | $1,478,000 | $(1,118,000) |
| 1982 | $(21,462,000) | $(25,066,000) |
| 1983 | $(33,192,000) | $(50,974,000) |
| 1984 | $(45,413,000) | ? |

- The admission: "my errors in reporting to you have been substantial and recently
  have always presented a better underwriting picture than was truly the case."
- And why it matters, in three ranked reasons the first of which is about trust:
  "(1) I like for you to be able to count on what I say; (2) our insurance managers
  and I undoubtedly acted with less urgency than we would have had we understood the
  full extent of our losses; and (3) we paid income taxes calculated on overstated
  earnings and thereby gave the government money that we didn't need to."
- Casualty and reinsurance lines are genuinely harder to reserve than property —
  "Sometimes the extent of these losses, or even their existence, is not known for
  decades" — but Buffett refuses the excuse: "But I still find our errors
  embarrassing."
- The standing commitment: "our record of past error should make us humble, and you
  suspicious. I will continue to report to you the errors, plus or minus, that
  surface each year."

He then separates his own innocent error from the industry's convenient error, and
explains why nobody catches it: "external auditors cannot effectively police the
financial statements of property/casualty insurers. If liabilities of an insurer,
correctly stated, would exceed assets, it falls to the insurer to volunteer this
morbid information. In other words, the corpse is supposed to file the death
certificate."

The structural reason insolvent insurers keep operating is the best short
explanation of float's dark side in the corpus: "In most businesses, of course,
insolvent companies run out of cash. Insurance is different: you can be broke but
flush." Cash arrives at inception and losses are paid much later, so the walking
dead write business at any price to keep it flowing — and Berkshire pays for it
twice, through sell-at-any-price competition and later through state guarantee fund
assessments.

See [[Principles/Fidelity]], [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### WPPSS — a bond valued as a business

Berkshire bought $139 million of Washington Public Power Supply System Projects 1,
2 and 3 bonds between October 1983 and June 1984, in the shadow of the $2.2 billion
default on Projects 4 and 5.

The method is the letter's contribution, not the position. Buffett states the
general principle first — "we buy marketable stocks for our insurance companies
based upon the criteria we would apply in the purchase of an entire business" —
then extends it to debt. The WPPSS holding is treated as a business that
"contractually earns $22.7 million after tax", available in cash: "Only a
relatively few businesses earn the 16.3% after tax on unleveraged capital that our
WPPSS investment does and those businesses, when available for purchase, sell at
large premiums to that capital."

The minuses get equal space: a slight risk the business is worth nothing within a
year or two, a risk of interrupted interest, and a hard ceiling — the most it can
be worth is the $205 million face value, only 48% above cost.

The section then turns into an argument about institutional behaviour that later
letters return to repeatedly:

- "Most managers have very little incentive to make the
  intelligent-but-with-some-chance-of-looking-like-an-idiot decision. Their personal
  gain/loss ratio is all too obvious: if an unconventional decision works out well,
  they get a pat on the back and, if it works out poorly, they get a pink slip."
- "as a group, lemmings may have a rotten image, but no individual lemming has ever
  received bad press."
- Why Berkshire is exempt: "With 47% of Berkshire's stock, Charlie and I don't worry
  about being fired, and we receive our rewards as owners, not managers. Thus we
  behave with Berkshire's money as we would with our own."

Concentration is defended on the same footing, and explicitly not recommended to
others: "This concentration makes sense only because our insurance business is
conducted from a position of exceptional financial strength. For almost all other
insurers, a comparable degree of concentration (or anything close to it) would be
totally inappropriate." Billy Rose supplies the case against over-diversification:
"If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well."

The disclosure policy is set out here too, with the cost quantified: Berkshire
bought less than 3% of the outstanding bonds despite trying to buy everything
available, and a few well-heeled imitators "easily could have cost us $5 million."
Hence no comment on securities activity to anyone.

And the standing view on bonds: "we dislike the purchase of most long-term bonds
under most circumstances and have bought very few in recent years. That's because
bonds are as sound as a dollar - and we view the long-term outlook for dollars as
dismal."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]], [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] and
[[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Dividend policy — restricted and unrestricted earnings

The closing essay is the most rigorous statement of the retention question in the
early letters, and it starts by noting that the question is normally dodged:
"Dividend policy is often reported to shareholders, but seldom explained."

The distinction that organises it: "The first point to understand is that all
earnings are not created equal." Restricted earnings are those a capital-hungry
business must plough back merely to hold its position — its unit volume, its
competitive position, its financial strength. "No matter how conservative its
payout ratio, a company that consistently distributes restricted earnings is
destined for oblivion unless equity capital is otherwise infused." Consolidated
Edison is the illustration: a dollar retained turned into 25 cents of market value,
and the dollars kept being retained.

For unrestricted earnings there is exactly one valid test:

- "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect
  - backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful
  analysis of the future - that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at
  least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."
- Restated for the owner: "you should wish your earnings to be reinvested if they
  can be expected to earn high returns, and you should wish them paid to you if low
  returns are the likely outcome of reinvestment."

Buffett then identifies the double standard that lets managers avoid the test. CEOs
apply it rigorously to subsidiaries and not to themselves: a division earning 5% is
told to send its cash to one earning 15%, while the parent's own 5% record is
covered by "some historical or industry-wide payout pattern."

And he supplies the measurement trap that hides the failure — the reason a
consolidated return on incremental capital can look fine while capital allocation
is disastrous:

- "The situation is analogous to a Pro-Am golf event: even if all of the amateurs
  are hopeless duffers, the team's best-ball score will be respectable because of
  the dominating skills of the professional."
- "Their marvelous core businesses, however, whose earnings grow year after year,
  camouflage repeated failures in capital allocation elsewhere". On the post-mortems
  that follow: "The managers at fault periodically report on the lessons they have
  learned from the latest disappointment. They then usually seek out future lessons.
  (Failure seems to go to their heads.)"
- The accountability rule: "Managers of high-return businesses who consistently
  employ much of the cash thrown off by those businesses in other ventures with low
  returns should be held to account for those allocation decisions, regardless of how
  profitable the overall enterprise is."
- And the line the whole essay builds to: "If earnings have been unwisely retained,
  it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained."

Applied to Berkshire: the test has been met — "Historically, Berkshire has earned
well over market rates on retained earnings, thereby creating over one dollar of
market value for every dollar retained" — and Buffett commits to distributing
unrestricted earnings if that ceases, judged on a five-year rolling average.

The self-assessment underneath is worth keeping. The three cornerstone businesses
he and Munger chose have "(1) survived but earned almost nothing, (2) shriveled in
size while incurring large losses, and (3) shrunk in sales volume to about 5% its
size at the time of our entry." The conclusion is not that they picked well: "Only
by committing available funds to much better businesses were we able to overcome
these origins."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The equity portfolio — $585.0 million at cost, $1,268.9 million at market

Ten named positions. GEICO 6,850,000 shares, cost $45.7 million, market $397.3
million. General Foods 4,047,191 shares at $226.1 million. Exxon 3,895,710 shares
at $175.3 million — a new and fourth-largest holding. The Washington Post 1,868,600
shares, still carried against a $10.6 million cost, now $150.0 million. Also Time,
Handy & Harman, Affiliated Publications, Interpublic, Northwest Industries, and
740,400 shares of American Broadcasting Companies.

### GEICO — 36% at year end, and a year the stock stood still

Direct property/casualty volume of $885 million gave Berkshire a $320 million
share, well over double its own premium volume. The market value of the holding
barely moved while intrinsic value rose substantially — and since GEICO had been
27% of Berkshire's net worth at the start of the year, that flat market value
depressed the reported gain.

Buffett treats this as the correct outcome rather than a disappointment: "we would
far rather have the business value of GEICO increase by X during the year, while
market value decreases, than have the intrinsic value increase by only 1/2 X with
market value soaring. In GEICO's case, as in all of our investments, we look to
business performance, not market performance. If we are correct in expectations
regarding the business, the market eventually will follow along."

On what GEICO actually owns: "In its core business - low-cost auto and homeowners
insurance - GEICO has a major, sustainable competitive advantage. That is a rare
asset in business generally, and it's almost non-existent in the field of financial
services." With the boundary drawn immediately — superior profitability had eluded
GEICO in everything outside that core.

And on Lou Simpson: "Lou has the rare combination of temperamental and intellectual
characteristics that produce outstanding long-term investment performance.
Operating with below-average risk, he has generated returns that have been by far
the best in the insurance industry." See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### WPPSS bonds — $139 million cost, $205 million face

Projects 1, 2 and 3, bought October 1983 to June 1984. Contractually earning $22.7
million after tax, a 16.3% unleveraged after-tax return, against a capped upside of
48%. Less than 3% of the bonds outstanding, despite trying to buy all that were
available.

### General Foods and GEICO proportionate redemptions

$21,843,601 received from General Foods with ownership held at exactly 8.75%; $21
million from GEICO in 1983 on the same basis. Both taxed as inter-corporate
dividends at 6.9%; both restated as stock sales for accounting purposes at the New
York auditor's insistence.

### Capital Cities/ABC — agreed 18 March 1985, after the letter was set

Three million shares at $172.50, contingent on Capital Cities completing its
acquisition of ABC. Reported as a subsequent event: "Our admiration for the
management of Capital Cities, led by Tom Murphy and Dan Burke, has been expressed
several times in previous annual reports. Quite simply, they are tops in both
ability and integrity."

The transaction is covered in full in [[Sources/1985ltr]]. See [[People/TomMurphy]].

### Shareholder-designated contributions

A record 97.2% of eligible shares participated; $3,179,000 to 1,519 charities.
Buffett adds an advisory shareholder vote on the programme, and notes he could find
no precedent for management asking owners their view on an owner-related policy:
"Managers who put their trust in capitalism seem in no hurry to put their trust in
capitalists."
