# 1985 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1985, a year with a 48.2% gain in net worth, the closure of the textile
business Berkshire was named after, and four major transactions: Capital
Cities/ABC, Scott & Fetzer, the Fireman's Fund quota-share, and the sale of
General Foods.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Size is the binding constraint, and Buffett says so plainly

The headline number comes with an immediate warning that it will not recur.

- "Our gain in net worth during the year was $613.6 million, or 48.2%. It is
  fitting that the visit of Halley's Comet coincided with this percentage gain:
  neither will be seen again in my lifetime."
- The general law: "an iron law of business is that growth eventually dampens
  exceptional economics. just look at the records of high-return companies once
  they have amassed even $1 billion of equity capital."
- Made concrete: "Last year I told you that we needed profits of $3.9 billion over
  the ten years then coming up to earn 15% annually. The comparable figure for the
  ten years now ahead is $5.7 billion"
- A second reason, called transitory: "Today we cannot find significantly-
  undervalued equities to purchase for our insurance company portfolios."

This is the earliest full statement of the constraint that governs every later
letter. See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### Market price should track business value, and the discount had gone

- "Historically, Berkshire shares have sold modestly below intrinsic business
  value... But recently the discount has disappeared, and occasionally a modest
  premium has prevailed."
- Buffett tells existing owners this is good news and prospective ones that it is
  not: "That was good news for any owner holding while that move took place, but
  it is bad news for the new or prospective owner."
- The stated preference, unusual for a CEO: "My own preference, as perhaps you'd
  guess, is for a market price that consistently approximates business value."
- Why it matters: "long periods of substantial undervaluation and/or
  overvaluation will cause the gains of the business to be inequitably distributed
  among various owners"

An instance of [[Principles/Fidelity]] with a real cost — talking down your own
share price is not standard practice.

### The textile shutdown, told as a capital-allocation autopsy

The most instructive section of the letter, and the one Buffett writes against
himself.

- The trap: "Each proposal to do so looked like an immediate winner. Measured by
  standard return-on-investment tests, in fact, these proposals usually promised
  greater economic benefits than would have resulted from comparable expenditures
  in our highly-profitable candy and newspaper businesses."
- Why the tests lied: "Viewed individually, each company's capital investment
  decision appeared cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the
  decisions neutralized each other and were irrational (just as happens when each
  person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on
  tiptoes). After each round of investment, all the players had more money in the
  game and returns remained anemic."
- The industry description: "The domestic textile industry operates in a commodity
  business, competing in a world market in which substantial excess capacity
  exists."
- Personal accountability: "But in the end nothing worked and I should be faulted
  for not quitting sooner." And: "I ignored Comte's advice - "the intellect should
  be the servant of the heart, but not its slave" - and believed what I preferred
  to believe."
- Management was not the problem: "Ken and his recent successor, Garry Morrison,
  have been excellent managers, every bit the equal of managers at our more
  profitable businesses."

The Burlington Industries comparison generalises it: twenty-one years, $3 billion
of capital expenditure, and a share price that in real terms bought a third of
what it had. "a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its
industry is a remarkable textile company - but not a remarkable business."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/Moat]].

### Incentive compensation: the savings-account argument

Buffett's most systematic attack on fixed-price stock options, built on an
analogy anyone can check.

- The mechanism: a $100,000 savings account at 8% with a quarter of the interest
  paid out grows to $179,084 in ten years and its "earnings" rise 70% — with no
  skill involved at all.
- Applied: "Many stock options in the corporate world have worked in exactly that
  fashion: they have gained in value simply because management retained earnings,
  not because it did well with the capital in its hands."
- The double standard: "nowhere in the business world are ten-year fixed-price
  options on all or a portion of a business granted to outsiders. Ten months, in
  fact, would be regarded as extreme."
- Options are not ownership: "No owner has ever escaped the burden of capital
  costs, whereas a holder of a fixed-price option bears no capital costs at all."
- Berkshire's alternative: "we use an incentive@compensation system that rewards
  key managers for meeting targets in their own bailiwicks. If See's does well,
  that does not produce incentive compensation at the News - nor vice versa."
- And it is uncapped: "We do not put a cap on bonuses, and the potential for
  rewards is not hierarchical."

Notably, Buffett records the counter-argument rather than burying it: "some
managers whom I admire enormously - and whose operating records are far better
than mine - disagree with me regarding fixed-price options."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Insurance: financial strength becomes a competitive weapon

- The turn: "In past reports, I have told you that Berkshire's strong capital
  position - the best in the industry - should one day allow us to claim a
  distinct competitive advantage in the insurance market. With the tightening of
  the market, that day arrived. Our premium volume more than tripled last year"
- Why customers came: "a policy is only an IOU - and who, in 1985, could not
  collect on many of their IOUs."
- The structural advantage nobody else had: "At Berkshire we have never played the
  lay-it-off-at-a-profit game and, until recently, that put us at a severe
  disadvantage in certain lines. Now the tables are turned: we have the
  underwriting capability whereas others do not."
- Concretely: "we are perfectly willing to risk losing $10 million of our own money
  on a single event, as long as we believe that the price is right and that the
  risk of loss is not significantly correlated with other risks we are insuring."
- And the warning built into it: "this period of tightness will pass; insurers and
  reinsurers will return to underpricing."
- Capacity is not physical: "in this industry, unlike most, capacity is an
  attitudinal concept, not a physical fact."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Concepts/Moat]].

### Reserving errors, admitted for the second year running

- "Our combined ratio has improved - from 134 in 1984 to 111 in 1985 - but
  continues to reflect past misdeeds."
- "Naturally, I made this promise thinking my future record would be much improved.
  So far this has not been the case."
- The "Except-For Insurance Company" story closes with the sharpest line in the
  letter: "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor."

## 💬 Notable Quotes

> "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a
> reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business
> that remains intact."

> "a good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far more a function
> of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row"

> "Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to
> changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching
> leaks."

> "Ponder this: the economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo -
> or a single See's candy store - considerably exceeds the proceeds we received
> from this massive collection of tangible assets that not too many years ago,
> under different competitive conditions, was able to employ over 1,000 people."

> "If the widget company consistently earned a superior return on capital
> throughout the period, or if capital employed only doubled during the CEO's
> reign, the praise for him may be well deserved. But if return on capital was
> lackluster and capital employed increased in pace with earnings, applause should
> be withheld."

> "In selecting common stocks, we devote our attention to attractive purchases, not
> to the possibility of attractive sales."

> "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Capital Cities/ABC — 3 million shares at $172.50

- Bought at a full price, and said so: "Our Cap Cities purchase was made at a full
  price, reflecting the very considerable enthusiasm for both media stocks and
  media properties that has developed in recent years... it's no field for
  bargains."
- Berkshire gave away its own votes: "for an extended period Tom, as CEO (or Dan,
  should he be CEO) votes our stock. This arrangement was initiated by Charlie and
  me, not by Tom."
- The reasoning: "the first-class managers with whom we have aligned ourselves can
  focus their efforts entirely upon running the businesses and maximizing
  long-term values for owners."
- On the ABC acquisition's near-term economics: "This bothers us not an iota; we
  can be very patient."
- And the candour that makes the letter worth reading: "some of you probably wonder
  why we are now buying Cap Cities at $172.50 per share given that your Chairman,
  in a characteristic burst of brilliance, sold Berkshire's holdings in the same
  company at $43 per share in 1978-80."

### Scott & Fetzer — about $320 million

- "Scott Fetzer is a prototype - understandable, large, well-managed, a good
  earner."
- Scale: "The company has sales of about $700 million derived from 17 businesses,
  many leaders in their fields."
- World Book, ~40% of sales, is described as the reason the price was acceptable:
  "This combination of exceptional products and modest prices at World Book, Inc.
  helped make us willing to pay the price demanded for Scott Fetzer"
- On its CEO: "Ralph's operating and capital-allocation record is superb"

### Fireman's Fund quota-share — 7% participation from 1 September 1985

- Four-year contract; "$92.7 million" of unearned premium reserve transferred at
  initiation against "$29.4 million" paid for expenses incurred.
- Follows Jack Byrne, whom Buffett credits with reviving GEICO: "Jack's performance
  in reviving GEICO from near-bankruptcy was truly extraordinary"
- And credits with something rarer: "he found managers to succeed him who have
  talents as valuable as his own."

### Sale of General Foods — $338 million pre-tax gain

- Four factors named: "a bargain purchase price, a business with fine underlying
  economics, an able management concentrating on the interests of shareholders,
  and a buyer willing to pay full business value."
- Only the fourth produces reported earnings, and Buffett says the first three are
  what actually matter.

### Washington Post, held since 1973

- The purchase: "We bought all of our WPC holdings in mid-1973 at a price of not
  more than one-fourth of the then per-share business value of the enterprise."
- The edge was not analytical: "Our advantage, rather, was attitude"
- And what management added: "Kay Graham, CEO of WPC, had the brains and courage to
  repurchase large quantities of stock for the company at those bargain prices"
- The "triple dip" — rising business value, rising per-share value from buybacks,
  and a narrowing discount — is the clearest worked example of compounding in the
  early letters.
