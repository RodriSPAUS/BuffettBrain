# 2021 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share market value grew 29.6% in 2021 against the S&P 500's 28.7% total return (book value per share remains retired as a metric; the performance table shows only market value). Buffett describes 2021 as a year with "little action" beyond steady progress in intrinsic value, no major acquisitions, and continued heavy share repurchases ($51.7 billion over 2020–2021 combined, retiring 9% of shares outstanding). The letter is organized around "Our Four Giants" (insurance, Apple, BNSF, BHE), a "Surprise, Surprise" section on Berkshire's scale as a taxpayer and infrastructure owner, and "A Wonderful Man and a Wonderful Business" — the story of TTI founder Paul Andrews, which Buffett traces directly to Berkshire's acquisition of BNSF.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Surprise, Surprise: Berkshire as Taxpayer and Infrastructure Owner
Berkshire's U.S. property, plant and equipment ($158 billion at year-end) exceeds that of any other American corporation. Berkshire paid $3.3 billion in federal income tax in 2021 against $402 billion collected from all U.S. corporations that year, prompting Buffett's line: "'I gave at the office' is an unassailable assertion when made by Berkshire shareholders." [[Sources/2021ltr]] He contrasts this with Berkshire's own textile-era history: in the nine years following the 1955 Berkshire Fine Spinning/Hathaway Manufacturing merger, net worth fell from $51.4 million to $22.1 million and the company paid the U.S. Treasury only $337,359 in income tax over that period — "a pathetic $100 per day." The Hathaway solicitation's confident merger pitch aged poorly:

> "The combination of the resources and managements will result in one of the strongest and most efficient organizations in the textile industry." [[Sources/2021ltr]]

After the 1965 change in management, Berkshire now pays the Treasury roughly $9 million daily.

### Insurance Float Reaches $147 Billion
From the 1967 National Indemnity purchase, Berkshire's insurance float has grown from $19 million to $147 billion, described as having cost Berkshire "less than nothing" cumulatively over 55 years of underwriting. Float grew $9 billion in 2021 alone. Buffett retells his 1986 hiring of Ajit Jain in the most compressed version yet:

> "He replied, 'None.' I said, 'Nobody's perfect,' and hired him." [[Sources/2021ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

### Our Four Giants
Buffett names the four assets holding most of Berkshire's value. The insurance operation (effectively 100% owned) leads, its scale unreplicable by competitors. Apple, the "runner-up Giant" by year-end market value, is highlighted for the mechanics of costless ownership growth: Berkshire's Apple stake rose from 5.39% to 5.55% during 2021 purely through Apple's own buybacks, with Berkshire's reported GAAP dividend income of $785 million dwarfed by its true economic share of Apple's earnings, about $5.6 billion. BNSF, the third Giant, posted record earnings of $6 billion in 2021 (carrying 143 million train-miles and 535 million tons of cargo), calculated the way Buffett prefers — after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all compensation — prompting a jab at "adjusted earnings" elsewhere in the market: "bull markets breed bloviated bull". [[Sources/2021ltr]] BHE, the fourth Giant (91.1% owned), earned a record $4 billion, more than 30-fold its $122 million in 2000, the year Berkshire first invested.

### Investments: A Trio of Japanese Trading Houses
Berkshire's top-15 holdings (cost/market, in millions) included American Express ($1,287/$24,804, 19.9%), Apple ($31,089/$161,155, 5.6%), Bank of America ($14,631/$45,952, 12.8%), Coca-Cola ($1,299/$23,684, 9.2%), Moody's ($248/$9,636, 13.3%), and U.S. Bancorp ($5,384/$8,058, 9.7%). New this year were stakes in two more Japanese trading houses alongside Itochu — Mitsubishi Corporation ($2,102/$2,593) and Mitsui & Co. ($1,621/$2,219) — for total equity investments of $104,605M cost / $350,719M market. Beyond the ranked table, Berkshire held a $10 billion Occidental Petroleum preferred/warrant position (valued at $10.7 billion), a 26.6% equity-method stake in Kraft Heinz ($13.1 billion carrying value), and 38.6% of Pilot Corp. (2021 revenue $45 billion), with an agreement already in place to raise Pilot ownership to 80% in early 2023, triggering full consolidation.

### U.S. Treasury Bills and a $144 Billion Cash Pile
Berkshire held $144 billion in cash and equivalents (excluding BNSF/BHE), of which $120 billion sat in Treasury bills — about 0.5% of the entire publicly-held U.S. national debt. Buffett reiterates the standing $30 billion minimum-cash pledge and frames the large cash balance not as a market call but as a temporary consequence of finding too few businesses meeting Berkshire's price/quality bar, adding a fresh data point on his own lifelong equity preference: since his first stock purchase in 1942 (three Cities Service preferred shares for $114.75, "all of my savings"), he has kept at least 80% of his own net worth in equities, with 100% his preferred allocation.

### Share Repurchases and Float-Per-Share
Berkshire repurchased 9% of its shares outstanding (as of year-end 2019) over 2020–2021 combined, spending $51.7 billion, leaving continuing shareholders with about 10% more of every Berkshire business. Buffett notes repurchases mechanically raised float per "A" share by 25% over the same period, from $79,387 to $99,497. He is explicit that repurchases only make sense at the right price, and that Berkshire's high-quality, buy-and-hold shareholder base actually limits repurchase opportunities compared to a more short-term-oriented shareholder register.

### A Wonderful Man and a Wonderful Business: Paul Andrews and TTI
A tribute to the late Paul Andrews, who founded Tex-Tronics (later TTI) in Fort Worth in 1971 with $500 after being laid off by General Dynamics — growing it from $112,000 in first-year sales to $7.7 billion in annual volume by the time of his death. In 2006, weighing a sale, Andrews rejected both a strategic buyer (which would have cut TTI's Fort Worth staff for "synergies") and a leveraged-buyout firm (focused on an eventual "exit"), telling Buffett:

> "After a year of pondering the alternatives, I want to sell to Berkshire because you are the only guy left." [[Sources/2021ltr]]

Buffett traces a direct causal chain from that 2006 deal to Berkshire's largest acquisition: a 2009 board visit to TTI's Fort Worth headquarters led Buffett to also meet BNSF CEO Matt Rose for the first time, and — after a weak BNSF earnings report shook the market that same week — Buffett made an offer for the whole railroad eleven days later.

### Thanks: Teaching and Long-Term Shareholders
Buffett reflects on seven decades of teaching (retired from the classroom in 2018) and credits Charlie Munger's "orangutan effect" — explaining an idea to someone else clarifies the explainer's own thinking regardless of the listener — for shaping his own communication style. He closes by distinguishing Berkshire's long-term, "til death do us part" individual shareholder base from index funds and short-term traders, calling that fifth "bucket" of owners (introduced in the 2020 letter) the group he and Munger have always had in mind.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Per-share market value +29.6%
- **Insurance**: Float grew to $147 billion, up $9 billion in the year
- **Apple**: Ownership grew from 5.39% to 5.55% at no incremental cost, via Apple's own buybacks
- **BNSF**: Record $6 billion earnings; **BHE**: record $4 billion earnings, more than 30x its 2000 level
- **New equity positions**: Mitsubishi Corporation and Mitsui & Co. added alongside the existing Itochu stake
- **Share repurchases**: $51.7 billion spent over 2020–2021, retiring about 9% of shares outstanding
- **Pilot Corp.**: Agreement reached to raise ownership from 38.6% to 80% in early 2023
