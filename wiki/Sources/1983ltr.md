# 1983 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1983: book value up 32% to $975.83 per share, the Blue Chip merger
completed, and Nebraska Furniture Mart bought from a 90-year-old woman who
arrived from Russia with no schooling and no English. The letter opens with the
business principles that became Berkshire's owner's manual, separates book value
from intrinsic value for the first time, and closes with the argument for never
splitting the stock.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The catechism — the principles written down for 1,000 new shareholders

Registered shareholders had grown from about 1,900 to about 2,900, mostly through
the Blue Chip merger. Buffett takes the occasion to write down the manager-owner
relationship. He calls it a catechism himself. It is the origin of Berkshire's
owner's manual, and most of it survives into the letters of the 1990s.

The foundation is a claim about what a corporation is:

- "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership. Charlie Munger and
  I think of our shareholders as owner-partners, and of ourselves as managing
  partners."
- "We do not view the company itself as the ultimate owner of our business assets
  but, instead, view the company as a conduit through which our shareholders own
  the assets."

Backed by exposure rather than assertion: "our directors are all major
shareholders of Berkshire Hathaway. In the case of at least four of the five, over
50% of family net worth is represented by holdings of Berkshire. We eat our own
cooking."

The rest is a set of constraints Buffett binds himself to, each with a cost
attached:

- The goal, and the unit it is measured in: "We do not measure the economic
  significance or performance of Berkshire by its size; we measure by per-share
  progress."
- Accounting is subordinated outright: "When acquisition costs are similar, we much
  prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard
  accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable."
- Leverage: "We rarely use much debt and, when we do, we attempt to structure it on
  a long-term fixed rate basis. We will reject interesting opportunities rather
  than over-leverage our balance sheet." He says plainly that this conservatism
  "has penalized our results".
- Diversification is the shareholder's job, not management's: "We will only do with
  your money what we would do with our own, weighing fully the values you can
  obtain by diversifying your own portfolios through direct purchases in the stock
  market."
- The retention test, the most falsifiable commitment in the list: "We feel noble
  intentions should be checked periodically against results. We test the wisdom of
  retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers
  shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained. To date, this test
  has been met. We will continue to apply it on a five-year rolling basis."
- Share issuance, carried forward from [[Sources/1982ltr]] and widened to cover
  options and convertibles: "We will not sell small portions of your company - and
  that is what the issuance of shares amounts to - on a basis inconsistent with the
  value of the entire enterprise."

Two principles are unusual enough to isolate. The first is offered as a weakness
rather than a virtue: "regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling
any good businesses that Berkshire owns, and are very reluctant to sell sub-par
businesses as long as we expect them to generate at least some cash and as long as
we feel good about their managers and labor relations." Buffett flags it as an
attitude "that hurts our financial performance", pairs it with a warning against
rescuing bad businesses with capital — "The projections will be dazzling - the
advocates will be sincere - but, in the end, major additional investment in a
terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand" — and
still declines the alternative: "gin rummy managerial behavior (discard your least
promising business at each turn) is not our style."

The second sets the standard for the report itself: "Our guideline is to tell you
the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed. We
owe you no less." With a self-interested reason underneath the ethical one, which
is what makes it durable: "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually
mislead himself in private."

See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Book value is not intrinsic value

Berkshire reports progress in book value, and Buffett spends a section explaining
why that is a proxy rather than the thing itself — "a conservative but reasonably
adequate proxy for growth in intrinsic business value - the measurement that
really counts."

The distinction, in the sentence the wiki has cited ever since:

- "Book value is an accounting concept, recording the accumulated financial input
  from both contributed capital and retained earnings. Intrinsic business value is
  an economic concept, estimating future cash output discounted to present value.
  Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates
  what can be taken out."

The analogy makes the gap intuitive: "Assume you spend identical amounts putting
each of two children through college. The book value (measured by financial input)
of each child's education would be the same. But the present value of the future
payoff (the intrinsic business value) might vary enormously - from zero to many
times the cost of the education."

Buffett then applies it to himself in both directions. In 1965 the $19.46 of book
value overstated intrinsic value, because all of it sat in textile assets: "In the
terms of our analogy, the investment in textile assets resembled investment in a
largely-wasted education." By 1983 intrinsic value considerably exceeded book
value, for two stated reasons — non-insurance equities carried at the lower of cost
or market, $70 million pre-tax below market, and, more importantly, economic
Goodwill far exceeding the accounting Goodwill on the balance sheet.

On the one-year figure, and why five years is the test: "why should the time
required for a planet to circle the sun synchronize precisely with the time
required for business actions to pay off?" With the tripwire specified in advance:
"Red lights should start flashing if the five-year average annual gain falls much
below the return on equity earned over the period by American industry in
aggregate."

Nineteen years of compounding at 22.6% gets the standing disclaimer: "Considering
our present size, nothing close to this rate of return can be sustained. Those who
believe otherwise should pursue a career in sales, but avoid one in mathematics."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### The Goodwill conversion, told as a change of mind

The appendix on Goodwill is the technical centre of the letter. The body carries
something more useful — Buffett describing the intellectual reversal that produced
Berkshire's later strategy, and naming what it cost him.

- "My own thinking has changed drastically from 35 years ago when I was taught to
  favor tangible assets and to shun businesses whose value depended largely upon
  economic Goodwill. This bias caused me to make many important business mistakes
  of omission, although relatively few of commission."
- Why it took so long: "My escape was long delayed, in part because most of what I
  had been taught by the same teacher had been (and continues to be) so
  extraordinarily valuable."
- Where it landed: "business experience, direct and vicarious, produced my present
  strong preference for businesses that possess large amounts of enduring Goodwill
  and that utilize a minimum of tangible assets."

That last sentence is the hinge between the Graham-era Berkshire and the one that
buys See's and Nebraska Furniture Mart. See [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]].

### Nebraska Furniture Mart, and what an ideal business looks like

Rose Blumkin talked her way past a border guard to leave Russia at 23, with no
formal schooling and no English, and learned the language from her elder daughter
in the evenings. In 1937 she opened a furniture store with $500 of savings.
Buffett's opening line is a callback to the Pascal quotation he used in
[[Sources/1982ltr]] against restless acquirers: "Even Pascal would have left the
room for Mrs. Blumkin."

The biography is told as evidence about character, and two episodes carry it. When
her resources ran out, "she simply sold the furniture and appliances from her home
in order to pay creditors precisely as promised." When Omaha retailers pressured
manufacturers not to supply her and she was hauled into court under Fair Trade
laws, she won every case — and "At the end of one case, after demonstrating to the
court that she could profitably sell carpet at a huge discount from the prevailing
price, she sold the judge $1400 worth of carpet."

By 1983 the single 200,000 square-foot store did over $100 million of annual
sales, more furniture, carpet and appliances than all Omaha competitors combined.

The test Buffett applies is the part worth extracting:

- "One question I always ask myself in appraising a business is how I would like,
  assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it. I'd
  rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny."
- And the mechanism — a low-cost operator that passes the savings on rather than
  banking them: "They buy brilliantly, they operate at expense ratios competitors
  don't even dream about, and they then pass on to their customers much of the
  savings. It's the ideal business - one built upon exceptional value to the
  customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners."

Berkshire bought 90%, leaving 10% with the family members in management and
optioning 10% to key young family managers. Mrs. B, then 90, stayed as Chairman
and worked the sales floor seven days a week. On the succession: "Geneticists
should do handsprings over the Blumkin family."

See [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] and [[Concepts/Moat]] — this is the
wide-and-sustainable cost advantage of [[Sources/1982ltr]] with a face on it.

### Insurance: the mistakes were mine

The most direct mea culpa in the early letters, and its force comes from the
structure of the admission rather than its tone:

- "in aggregate, the companies we operate and whose underwriting results reflect
  the consequences of decisions that were my responsibility a few years ago, had
  absolutely terrible results. Fortunately, GEICO, whose policies I do not
  influence, simply shot the lights out. The inference you draw from this summary
  is the correct one. I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home
  to roost."

Berkshire's combined ratio was 121 against an industry 111. Mike Goldberg had
recently taken over the insurance operation, and Buffett explicitly refuses the
available excuse: "it would be nice for me if our shortcomings could be placed at
his doorstep rather than mine." The long lead time of insurance means "the roots of
the 1983 results are operating and personnel decisions made two or more years back
when I had direct managerial responsibility for the insurance group."

The forward view is unchanged from [[Sources/1982ltr]]: the poor 1983 experience is
expected to be "more or less typical for a good many years to come", with premium
volume needing to grow about 10% annually merely to hold loss ratios where they
are.

Against that, the letter identifies where Berkshire's balance sheet becomes the
product. Structured settlements and loss-reserve assumption are businesses in
which the buyer's real concern is whether the seller will still exist in thirty
years: "In such transactions our premier financial strength should make us the
number one choice of both claimants and insurers who must rely on the reinsurer's
promises for a great many years to come." And: "No other insurer we know of - even
those with much larger gross assets - has our financial strength."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Franchise, measured — the Buffalo News

The News section is the best worked example in the early letters of measuring a
moat rather than asserting one. The metric is penetration: the share of households
in the community buying the paper each day.

- "We believe a paper's penetration ratio to be the best measure of the strength of
  its franchise. Papers with unusually high penetration in the geographical area
  that is of prime interest to major local retailers, and with relatively little
  circulation elsewhere, are exceptionally efficient buys for those retailers.
  Low-penetration papers have a far less compelling message to present to
  advertisers."

For the six months to September 1983 the News ranked first in weekday penetration
among the 100 largest US papers, and third on Sundays. Sunday circulation had gone
from 270,000 in 1976, under the Courier-Express, to 376,000.

Buffett attributes it to three things and disowns credit for the first — Buffalo's
low population turnover, since "A stable population is more interested and involved
in the activities of its community than is a shifting population". The other two
are choices: editorial reputation, and a deliberately expensive product decision.
The news hole ran at 50% of content against an industry average in the high 30s,
giving readers over 25% more news page for page. "This news-rich mixture is by
intent. Some publishers, pushing for higher profit margins, have cut their news
holes during the past decade. We have maintained ours and will continue to do so."

The section also reports the bad news: the shift from run-of-press linage to
preprints has negative economics, since preprints are less profitable, more exposed
to alternative delivery, and shrink the absolute space given to news.

See [[Cases/BuffaloNews]] and [[Concepts/Moat]].

### See's — a good business with a problem Buffett will not solve by cutting quality

Sales of $133.5 million and after-tax operating profit of $13.7 million from 207
shops. The problem is stated without spin: poundage has been flat for four years,
and same-store volume fell 0.8% in 1983 — the best result since 1979, against a
cumulative decline of about 8%.

Two constraints are declared non-negotiable. Raw material costs are accepted as
uncontrollable because quality is not: "we will, as a matter of course, buy the
finest ingredients that we can, regardless of changes in their price levels. We
regard product quality as sacred." Other costs had risen faster than inflation, and
those are treated as the real failure.

The measurement discipline is worth noting on its own: "we regard the most
important measure of retail trends to be units sold per store rather than dollar
volume" — dollar sales had risen only because prices had.

And the pricing insight, in a parenthesis: "In candy, as in stocks, price and value
can differ; price is what you give, value is what you get."

See [[Cases/SeeCandies]].

### Why Berkshire will never split the stock

The closing essay argues that a shareholder register is an asset that can be
managed — and that a split degrades it.

The chain: "One of our goals is to have Berkshire Hathaway stock sell at a price
rationally related to its intrinsic business value." That requires rational holders
— "The key to a rational stock price is rational shareholders, both current and
prospective" — because "Manic-depressive personalities produce manic-depressive
valuations."

You cannot vet buyers: "Mrs. Astor could select her 400, but anyone can buy any
stock." What you can do is advertise honestly and let sorting happen: "self
selection will draw a far different crowd to a musical event advertised as an opera
than one advertised as a rock concert even though anyone can buy a ticket to
either." What Berkshire advertises for: "We want those who think of themselves as
business owners and invest in companies with the intention of staying a long time.
And, we want those who keep their eyes focused on business results, not market
prices."

Hence the conclusion: "Were we to split the stock or take other actions focusing on
stock price rather than business value, we would attract an entering class of
buyers inferior to the exiting class of sellers." And the reason it would persist:
"People who buy for non-value reasons are likely to sell for non-value reasons."

### The chair-changing tax

The same section contains the sharpest attack on trading costs in the corpus, and
it is arithmetic rather than moralising.

- On the sell side of the industry: brokers praise liquidity, but "those who cannot
  fill your pocket will confidently fill your ear". And: "what is good for the
  croupier is not good for the customer. A hyperactive stock market is the
  pickpocket of enterprise."
- The calculation: a company earning 12% on equity, 100% annual share turnover, 1%
  commission each way at book value — owners pay 2% of net worth a year to transfer
  ownership, and one sixth of the earnings is gone.
- Made visible: "Can you imagine the agonized cry that would arise if a
  governmental unit were to impose a new 16 2/3% tax on earnings of corporations or
  investors? By market activity, investors can impose upon themselves the equivalent
  of such a tax."
- At market scale: sustained 100-million-share days at 15 cents a share cost
  investors about $7.5 billion a year, roughly the combined 1982 profits of Exxon,
  General Motors, Mobil and Texaco. Management fees of over $2 billion consume the
  earnings of the five largest banks. "These expensive activities may decide who
  eats the pie, but they don't enlarge it."
- And the rebuttal to the efficiency defence: "Adam Smith felt that all
  noncollusive acts in a free market were guided by an invisible hand that led an
  economy to maximum progress; our view is that casino-type markets and
  hair-trigger investment management act as an invisible foot that trips up and
  slows down a forward-moving economy."

Berkshire's own figure closes it: roughly 3% true turnover a year at about a 2%
spread, some 6/100 of 1% of market value, about $900,000. "Splitting the stock
would increase that cost, downgrade the quality of our shareholder population, and
encourage a market price less consistently related to intrinsic business value. We
see no offsetting advantages."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Nebraska Furniture Mart — 90% of the business

Bought from Rose Blumkin, with 10% retained by the family members in management
and 10% optioned to key young family managers. Over $100 million of sales from one
store. Mrs. B stayed on as Chairman at 90. See [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]].

### Blue Chip Stamps merger — completed

Less than one-tenth of 1% of the shares of each company voted against, and no
appraisal requests were made. Buffett draws out the fact that matters for owners:
Berkshire had 1,146,909 shares outstanding against 1,137,778 at the start of fiscal
1965, so "all of today's assets - the News, See's, Nebraska Furniture Mart, the
Insurance Group, $1.3 billion in marketable stocks, etc. - have been added to the
original textile assets with virtually no net dilution to the original owners."

### GEICO — about one third, and $270 million of premium volume

"Geico's performance during 1983 was as good as our own insurance performance was
poor." A 96 combined ratio against the industry's 111, after a large voluntary
accrual for policyholder dividends. Buffett credits both the idea and the people:
"Its superiority reflects the combination of a truly exceptional business idea and
an exceptional management."

Berkshire's third of GEICO represented $270 million of premium volume, about 80%
more than Berkshire wrote itself — "the major portion of our total insurance
business comes from the best insurance book in the country. This fact does not
moderate by an iota the need for us to improve our own operation."

On the team: Jack Byrne and Bill Snyder for underwriting discipline including full
loss reserves, and "Lou Simpson is the class of the field among insurance
investment managers. The three of them are some team." See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### Structured settlements and loss-reserve assumption — a new business

Started deliberately, on the basis that financial strength is the product. Run by
Don Wurster at National Indemnity. Buffett commits in advance to reporting these on
a separate line, excluding them from the combined ratio, and front-ending no
profit — the accounting discipline is set before the business gets large.

### No stock split

Rejected with reasons, at a share price of about $1,300.

### Shareholder-designated contributions

96.4% of eligible shares participated; $3,066,501 went to 1,353 charities.

### The acquisition advertisement, repeated

The same six criteria as [[Sources/1982ltr]], deliberately re-run: "in our
communications businesses we tell our advertisers that repetition is a key to
results". Closing with "For the right business - and the right people - we can
provide a good home."
