# 2021 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

Berkshire's first "hybrid" annual meeting since the pandemic began, held in Los Angeles so Charlie Munger — then 97 and unwilling to travel — could attend in person alongside Warren Buffett, Greg Abel and Ajit Jain, with Becky Quick again relaying shareholder questions. The meeting opens with prepared remarks (Q1 2021 results, two lessons for new investors) before roughly 3.5 hours of Q&A and then the formal shareholder meeting itself. Recurring threads are the speculative excess of the 2020-21 market (SPACs, Robinhood, crypto), near-zero interest rates as the "gravity" pulling on all asset values, the emerging succession structure under Abel and Jain, and a frank admission that the Precision Castparts write-down was Buffett's own error, not "Berkshire's."

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A Speculative Market: SPACs, Robinhood, and Bitcoin
Buffett says the boom in blank-check acquirers is "a killer" for Berkshire's own ability to buy businesses, since SPAC sponsors must deploy capital within about two years regardless of price, and private-equity fee structures push in the same direction: "If you're running money for somebody else and you're getting paid a fee, and you get the upside and you don't have the downside, you're going to buy something." He reads Keynes's 1936 warning about markets becoming a casino, and both men are sharply critical of Robinhood-style trading apps and cryptocurrency, though for related rather than identical reasons — Buffett calls the gambling instinct human but declines comment on Bitcoin ("I'm all right on that one"), while Munger is unsparing on both:

> "Speculators may do no harm as bubbles on a steady stream of enterprise. But the position is serious when enterprise becomes the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation. When the capital development of the country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

> "Well, that is really waving the red flag at the bull. I think it's just godawful that something like that would draw investment from civilized men and decent citizens. It's deeply wrong. We don't want to make our money selling things that are bad for people." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

Munger also calls state lotteries worse than Robinhood ("The states in America replaced the Mafia as the proprietor in the numbers game") and, on crypto specifically:

> "So I think I should say modestly that I think the whole development is disgusting and contrary to the interest of civilization." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

### Near-Zero Rates as "Gravity" on Asset Values
Buffett frames the entire valuation environment through a single Treasury auction result — a four-week bill sold at essentially 0% — and a physics metaphor:

> "I mean interest rates basically are to the value of assets, what gravity is to matter essentially." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

He notes Berkshire's own Treasury-bill income fell from roughly $1.5 billion a year pre-pandemic to about $20 million at current rates, and recalls that economist Paul Samuelson's textbook considered negative rates barely conceivable even in theory. He and Munger agree they do not know how the current fiscal-and-monetary experiment ends, only that "you can never do one thing" in economics.

### The Vice Chairmen: Succession Under Abel and Jain
Introducing Greg Abel (non-insurance operations) and Ajit Jain (insurance) at length, Buffett credits Jain by name for building the world's largest property-casualty insurer by net worth since a chance 1986 Saturday meeting, and frames both men as central to how Berkshire will run without him and Munger. Asked how they work together compared with Buffett and Munger's 62-year partnership, both describe a functional but less constant relationship — quarterly check-ins plus ad hoc calls whenever an insurance or capital question crosses into the other's territory. On why the company isn't "too big to manage," Munger credits Berkshire's unusual degree of decentralization directly:

> "By the way, the Roman Empire worked as long as it did because it was so decentralized." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

### Q1 2021 Results and the Unrealized-Gains Accounting Quirk
First-quarter GAAP earnings were a positive $11.7 billion, against a loss of almost $50 billion in the same quarter of 2020 — a swing Buffett attributes almost entirely to the accounting-standards change requiring unrealized stock-market gains and losses to run through earnings, which he again argues investors should look past in favor of operating earnings. Berkshire repurchased about $25 billion of its own stock in the first quarter of 2021 "and more money since."

### Two Lessons for New Investors
Before Q&A, Buffett offers two illustrations aimed at the wave of new retail investors. First, he compares the 20 largest companies by market value on March 31, 2021 (led by Apple at slightly over $2 trillion) with the top 20 from 1989 (led by a $104 billion company): none of the 1989 list remains on the 2021 list. Second, he traces roughly 2,000 companies that entered the U.S. auto industry once it was obviously going to reshape the country — Berkshire's own Marmon among them, maker of the 1911 Indianapolis 500 winner — noting that by 2009 only three were left, two of which went bankrupt. The point in both cases: correctly identifying a transformative industry or era is not the same as being able to pick its long-term winner.

### COVID-19: Selling the Airlines and the Fed's Rescue
Asked why Berkshire sold its airline stakes near the bottom rather than buying more broadly during the March 2020 panic, Buffett does not defend the airline sales as a "great moment in Berkshire's history" but distinguishes it from the roughly 1%-1.5% of total business value Berkshire actually sold. He credits the Federal Reserve's March 23, 2020 intervention — a day after Berkshire itself effectively could not have issued bonds and money-market funds saw a run reminiscent of September 2008 — with reopening markets, and stresses Berkshire's structural need for self-sufficiency: "We're not a bank. We can't go to the Federal Reserves if we need money." He also recounts the wind-down of Haven, Berkshire's joint healthcare venture with JPMorgan and Amazon, which taught more about Berkshire's own decentralized operations (real savings found by comparing 60-70 of them) than it changed the wider 17%-of-GDP healthcare system.

### Precision Castparts: Owning the Mistake
Referencing the write-down disclosed in that year's chairman's letter, Buffett is explicit that responsibility is personal, not corporate:

> "Well, Berkshire didn't make a mistake. I made the mistake, incidentally." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

He attributes the error to overestimating Precision Castparts' normalized average earning power — not its management or competitive quality — and observes that disappointing acquisitions tend naturally to shrink as a share of Berkshire's overall business over time, while successful ones (he cites GEICO, now writing roughly 15 times the volume it did at the 1996 buyout) become proportionately larger.

### Capital Allocation: Buybacks, "So-So" Stocks, and the $70-80 Billion Question
Buffett and Munger defend repurchases as a way to let shareholders who want cash exit at a fair price without forcing the reinvestment-minded majority into an unwanted dividend, citing a past shareholder vote in which roughly 97% opposed paying one:

> "Well, I -- if you're repurchasing stock just to pull it higher, it's deeply immoral. But if you're repurchasing stock because it's a fair thing to do in the interest of your existing shareholders, it's a highly moral act. And the people who are criticizing it are bonkers." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

Pressed by Becky Quick, Buffett acknowledges Berkshire bought some equities in 2020 it does not have a distinct analytical edge on ("good companies... but do we know something about those companies... is the answer, I think"), preferring them to Treasury bills but not comfortable deploying tens of billions there. Berkshire was still sitting on roughly $70-80 billion in deployable cash, describing the company's cash cushion (10-15% of total assets) as deliberate protection for the shareholders who trust Berkshire with their savings.

### Insurance: Ajit Jain on Pandemic Risk and the Boy Scouts Claims
Jain says the industry, Berkshire included, structurally underpriced pandemic risk ("totally, totally underpriced by all of us in the industry") because it was treated as a once-in-a-century tail event, and that COVID has exposed poor aggregation of correlated risk across product lines (event-cancellation policies written without a distinct pandemic loading, for instance). By the end of Q1 2021, Berkshire had reserved about $1.6 billion for COVID-19 claims — a figure Jain expects to rise — against an industry-wide reserve of roughly $25-30 billion that some estimate will eventually reach $100 billion; Buffett notes Berkshire is not among the largest payers relative to its size. Separately, discussing long-tail liability risk generally, Buffett and Jain discuss claims tied to the Boy Scouts of America abuse case rising from roughly 1,100 to nearly 100,000 as states extended statutes of limitations, as an example of the "unpleasant surprises" inherent to long-tail underwriting. On competition, Jain says GEICO has closed most of its margin gap with Progressive but still lags on telematics-based rate-setting, while leading on brand and expense management.

### Berkshire Hathaway Energy in Texas: the Grid Proposal
Responding to a shareholder question citing Elon Musk's public criticism that battery storage would serve Texas better than new gas-fired generating capacity, Greg Abel defends BHE's proposal — built with Peter Kiewit and Sons, targeting delivery by November 2023, and backed by a $4 billion Berkshire penalty if the deadline is missed — as designed for multi-day outages like February 2021's, which Abel says caused $80-130 billion in economic damage to Texas, rather than the few hours of discharge a battery system provides. Buffett frames it as an insurance-like decision: "so you want a margin of safety in it." The two also review BHE's broader decarbonization record: its 2015 Paris-aligned pledge (a 26-28% carbon-footprint cut versus 2005, met in 2020), a newer 50-52% target for 2030, 16 coal units already closed with 16 more by 2030 and the remaining 14 by 2049, and BNSF's own committed 30% footprint reduction by 2030.

### Taxes, Estate Planning, and the "Class AA Stock"
Buffett declines to state a personal view on the Biden administration's proposed capital-gains, corporate-tax and stepped-up-basis changes, but argues corporate tax increases do not straightforwardly harm shareholders, framing the government's claim on future profits as an equity stake:

> "The U.S. government owns what I call the Class AA stock. It's a very special stock. They get a percentage of the earnings, but they don't own the assets." [[Sources/Conference_call_2021]]

He restates his intention that 99.7% of his estate go to philanthropy or the federal government, with his will to be public record, and that a small remaining slice will be invested roughly 90% in an S&P 500 index fund for his wife. Munger separately criticizes states (naming California implicitly, contrasted with Florida) for policies that drive out wealthy residents, while Buffett notes pension underfunding is a severe and largely unaddressed problem at the state level.

### China, and a Closing Note on Corporate Mythology
Asked about Chinese leadership's tolerance for capitalism given the Ant Group IPO block and Jack Ma's reprimand, Munger is admiring of the Communist Party's pragmatic pivot toward markets: "I don't care whether the cat is black and white as long as it catches mice. That's my kind of talk." Late in the meeting Buffett also describes, without naming examples, a phenomenon he calls corporate "mist" — a company's public narrative repeated by successive CEOs and investor-relations teams until it hardens into unquestioned internal mythology, a topic he says he may return to in a future letter. Both men agree the single biggest risk on any Berkshire subsidiary's plate is not competitive or regulatory but simply "the wrong management" taking over.

### The Dream Business: Low-Capital, High-Margin Compounders
Asked whether Berkshire's investments in Apple and Snowflake signal a broader shift toward high-margin, capital-light technology businesses, Buffett agrees that kind of business is what every investor is really looking for, naming Apple, Google, Microsoft and Facebook as examples that "grow a lot" while requiring comparatively little property, plant and equipment — Apple's $37 billion against Berkshire's roughly $170 billion, despite Berkshire being the far larger enterprise by revenue. He traces Berkshire's own recognition of this pattern back to buying See's Candy in 1972, a business needing little inventory or receivables. He also declines, on the same theme, to make investment managers Todd Combs and Ted Weschler available to shareholders for questioning, arguing they are competitively valuable Berkshire assets in the same way a pharmaceutical company would not expose its scientists' current research.

### The Formal Meeting: Directors Elected, Two Shareholder Proposals Fail
After Q&A, the formal shareholder meeting convened with 639,747 Class A and 1,335,074,355 Class B shares outstanding (456,040 Class A and 663,442,069 Class B represented by proxy). All 14 director nominees — including Buffett, Munger, Abel, Jain, Howard Buffett, Susan Decker and Meryl Witmer — were elected with at least 473,474 votes each. Both shareholder proposals opposed by the board failed: a climate-risk disclosure proposal (co-sponsored by Federated Hermes, CalPERS and CDPQ) lost 131,376 votes to 385,336, and a diversity-and-inclusion reporting proposal (from As You Sow) lost 124,842 votes to 391,662.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Q1 2021 GAAP earnings**: $11.7 billion, versus a loss of almost $50 billion in Q1 2020 (swing driven mostly by the mark-to-market accounting rule for unrealized equity gains/losses)
- **Share repurchases**: about $25 billion in Q1 2021 alone, "and more money since"
- **Deployable cash**: roughly $70-80 billion still uninvested, described as 10-15% of total assets, kept as a deliberate cushion
- **Bank stocks reduced** to below 10% ownership everywhere except Bank of America, where the stake was increased
- **Precision Castparts write-down**: attributed by Buffett personally to overestimating the business's normalized average earning power in 2016
- **Berkshire Hathaway Energy's Texas grid proposal**: targeting delivery by November 2023, backed by a $4 billion Berkshire penalty for missing the deadline, projected at a 9.3% return
- **COVID-19 insurance reserves**: approximately $1.6 billion reserved by Berkshire as of Q1 2021, against an industry-wide reserve of $25-30 billion (with some estimates of eventual industry losses near $100 billion)
- **Formal meeting**: all 14 director nominees elected; shareholder proposals on climate-risk disclosure (131,376 for / 385,336 against) and diversity-and-inclusion reporting (124,842 for / 391,662 against) both failed
