# 2006 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 18.4%, and net worth grew $16.9 billion — a record
one-year gain for any American business outside of merger accounting, by
Buffett's own count. He opens by discounting the achievement: insurance
"benefited from a large dose of luck" as the hurricanes of 2004-05 simply did
not recur, and GEICO alone delivered an "extraordinary" productivity gain that
he credits almost entirely to Tony Nicely. The letter's two long historical
set-pieces are the 40th anniversary of Berkshire's 1967 entry into insurance,
told through Jack Ringwalt's unexpired parking meter, and the Lloyd's of
London/Equitas retroactive-reinsurance deal, told from Edward Lloyd's 1688
coffee house forward. The year's acquisitions run from six-figure tuck-ins to
the $4 billion purchase of ISCAR, Berkshire's first business based outside the
U.S. Buffett also lays out, in more detail than before, a plan to hire and
test a successor for the investment side of his job.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A record gain, discounted by its own author

> "We believe that $16.9 billion is a record for a one-year gain in net worth –
> more than has ever been booked by any American business, leaving aside boosts
> that have occurred because of mergers (e.g., AOL's purchase of Time
> Warner)." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

The confession that follows is characteristic: the gain owed less to skill than
to the absence of catastrophe.

> "All that said, a confession about our 2006 gain is in order. Our most
> important business, insurance, benefited from a large dose of luck: Mother
> Nature, bless her heart, went on vacation." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Over 42 years, book value per share had compounded from $19 to $70,281, 21.4%
annually. Two yardsticks are restated as the ones that matter beyond book
value: per-share investments, which reached $80,636 (27.5% compounded since
1965), and pre-tax non-insurance earnings per share, which reached $3,625
(17.9% compounded since 1965, 31.7% just since 1995 as the acquisition
strategy accelerated).

### GEICO: the productivity story, again, and further

> "As I've told you before, Tony Nicely, GEICO's CEO, went to work at the
> company 45 years ago, two months after turning 18. He became CEO in 1992, and
> from then on the company's growth exploded." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Between yearend 2003 and yearend 2006, GEICO's policy count rose from 5.7
million to 8.1 million — 42% — while full-time-equivalent employment fell
3.5%, for a 47% productivity gain achieved from an already-lean base.
Advertising rose from $238 million in 2003 to $631 million in 2006 (and from
$31 million in 1995, when Berkshire took control), a spend Buffett frames as
funded by the cost advantage rather than competing with it. Berkshire director
Don Keough's suggestion, after reviewing the numbers, was to skip waiting for
new babies:

> "Forget births. Tell the shareholders to immediately change the names of
> their present children to Tony or Antoinette." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### Skepticism about size, stated by the person running the biggest thing he's run

Buffett and Munger, running a 217,000-employee, nearly-$100-billion-revenue
business, are explicit that they distrust bigness on principle:

> "Sitting in those seats, we both grew skeptical about the ability of big
> entities of any type to function well. Size seems to make many organizations
> slow-thinking, resistant to change and smug." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

He quotes Churchill — "We shape our buildings, and afterwards our buildings
shape us" — and notes that of the ten largest non-oil companies by market
capitalization in 1965, only one remained on the 2006 list. His own management
style is framed as delegation rather than command, with an explicit
acknowledgment that some giant-company CEOs (Ken Chenault, Jeff Immelt, Dick
Kovacevich) do a job he says he could not do himself and would not enjoy.

### Acquisitions: from a parking meter's worth of trust to $4 billion for ISCAR

Berkshire completed the three acquisitions pending at 2005 yearend — PacifiCorp,
Business Wire, Applied Underwriters — for about $6 billion combined at the
start of 2006. The year's headline deal came from an unsolicited letter:

> "The story here began on October 25, 2005, when I received a 1¼-page letter
> from Eitan, of whom I then knew nothing. The letter began, 'I am writing to
> introduce you to ISCAR,' and proceeded to describe a cutting-tool business
> carried on in 61 countries." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Eitan Wertheimer's letter named the reason directly — a family business facing
generational succession, choosing its buyer rather than being shopped:

> "We have for some time considered the issues of generational transfer and
> ownership that are typical for large family enterprises, and have given much
> thought to ISCAR's future. Our conclusion is that Berkshire Hathaway would be
> the ideal home for ISCAR." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Berkshire bought 80% of ISCAR for $4 billion, its first acquisition of a
business based outside the U.S.; the Wertheimer family retained the other 20%.
Buffett's verdict on the operation itself is that its edge is entirely human:

> "It's a business without magic except for that imparted by the people who
> run it." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

**TTI**, an electronic-components distributor Paul Andrews built from $112,000
of sales to $1.3 billion over 35 years, was brought to Buffett by John Roach —
the same intermediary behind the 2000 Justin Industries purchase. Andrews
explicitly rejected a strategic buyer (likely to dismantle the company chasing
synergies) and a private-equity buyer (likely to load it with debt and flip
it), choosing Berkshire instead. Buffett and Andrews made a deal before lunch
the same day they met. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

Tuck-ins followed at Fruit of the Loom (Russell Corp, about $1.2 billion, plus
an agreement to buy VF Corp's intimate-apparel business — together about $2.2
billion of added sales), MiTek (14 acquisitions since 2001, sales tripled,
employee-purchased stock up from $10,000 to $71,699 per share in five years),
CTB, Shaw and Clayton. Buffett is explicit that Berkshire still needs
"elephants," since tuck-ins alone cannot absorb its cash flow.

### Insurance: 40 years from a $8.6 million purchase to $50.9 billion of float

The letter marks the March 9, 1967 purchase of National Indemnity and National
Fire & Marine from Jack Ringwalt for $8.6 million, and tells the closing as a
character test:

> "When we were due to close the purchase at Charlie's office, Jack was late.
> Finally arriving, he explained that he had been driving around looking for a
> parking meter with some unexpired time. That was a magic moment for me. I
> knew then that Jack was going to be my kind of manager." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Float has grown from $17 million at that purchase to $50.9 billion at 2006
yearend, with a further $7 billion added by the Equitas contract signed just
after yearend (below). Buffett is candid that none of this was foreseen:

> "Naturally, I had no notion in 1967 that our float would develop as it has.
> There's much to be said for just putting one foot in front of the other every
> day." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

> "In 2006, though, everything went right in insurance – really right. ... When
> I recite their names, I feel as if I'm at Cooperstown, reading from the Hall
> of Fame roster." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

The reversal is not treated as the new normal. Buffett names the central
uncertainty about whether 2004-05 or 2006 is the aberration, and commits to a
number:

> "We remain prepared to lose $6 billion in a single event, if we have been
> paid appropriately for assuming that risk." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### Lloyd's, Equitas, and the "names" who backed it "to the last cufflink"

The letter's longest historical digression traces Lloyd's from Edward Lloyd's
1688 coffee house, through the rise of passive investor "names" who backed
underwriting syndicates with unlimited personal liability, to the asbestos and
environmental claims of the 1980s that nearly destroyed the institution.
Equitas was created in 1996 with £11.1 billion to absorb all pre-1993
liabilities — pooling the damage rather than fixing it. Thirteen years and
£11.3 billion of paid claims later, Buffett and Ajit Jain judged the patient
likely to survive and structured a retroactive reinsurance deal:

> "We said – and I'm simplifying – that if Equitas would give us $7.12 billion
> in cash and securities (this is the float I spoke about), we would pay all of
> its future claims and expenses up to $13.9 billion. That amount was $5.7
> billion above what Equitas had recently guessed its ultimate liabilities to
> be." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Scott Moser, Equitas's CEO, is quoted summarizing the trade in one line:

> "Names wanted to sleep easy at night, and we think we've just bought them the
> world's best mattress." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Buffett appends a rare accounting explainer, flagged with his usual
self-deprecation ("It's time to eat your broccoli"), on how retroactive
contracts always book an underwriting loss up front, offset over decades by
investment income on the cash received; the Equitas deal alone would raise
Berkshire's annual DCRA amortization charge to about $450 million.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: a structural decline named plainly

Shaw's founder, Bob Shaw, retired at 75, handing the company to 31-year veteran
Vance Bell. MiTek is "developing into a mini-conglomerate" five years after
Berkshire's purchase, sales tripled, with 55 employee co-investors holding
stock that rose from $10,000 to $71,699 per share.

The Buffalo News section is the letter's clearest statement that management
quality cannot overcome a dying business model:

> "When an industry's underlying economics are crumbling, talented management
> may slow the rate of decline. Eventually, though, eroding fundamentals will
> overwhelm managerial brilliance." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Buffett traces the newspaper industry's historical monopoly economics — one
paper per city, "Survival of the Fattest" — to its dissolution as cable,
satellite and the internet took the audience newspapers once had by default,
and cites his own 1991 warning as having proven out. He commits to keeping the
News short of an "irreversible cash drain," but is blunt about the trend: "the
days of lush profits from our newspaper are over." See [[Concepts/Moat]] for
the same argument as a general statement about eroding franchises.

NetJets turned a corner in Europe after a costly decade-long build-out — 80
customers after five years, a $212 million cumulative pre-tax loss by
mid-2006, then 589 net new customers added in 2005-06 alone under Mark
Booth — producing $143 million of worldwide pre-tax earnings for the year
despite a $19 million first-quarter loss.

### Regulated Utility Business: Western utilities added, real estate slows

MidAmerican's fully-diluted interest reached 86.6%, and the western utilities
(Pacific Power, Rocky Mountain Power) acquired via PacifiCorp added about 1.7
million customers across six states. HomeServices, the real estate brokerage
arm, saw volume fall 9% to $58 billion and profits fall 50% as loosely
underwritten "optional" and "teaser" mortgage terms began to unwind — a
dynamic Buffett names directly:

> "This is the Scarlett O'Hara scenario: 'I'll think about that tomorrow.' For
> many home owners, 'tomorrow' has now arrived." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Finance and Financial Products: the derivatives wind-down finally ends

> "You will be happy to hear – and I'm even happier – that this will be my last
> discussion of the losses at Gen Re's derivative operation. When we started to
> wind this business down early in 2002, we had 23,218 contracts outstanding.
> Now we have 197." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Cumulative pre-tax losses on the wind-down reached $409 million, with only $5
million of that in 2006 itself. Munger's line on how the loss should have been
classified on the 2001 balance sheet — "Good Until Reached For" — closes the
subject Buffett has revisited every year since 2002. Clayton Homes earned $513
million pre-tax even as the manufactured-housing industry recorded its lowest
unit sales since 1962, and paid Berkshire an $86 million fee for financing its
$10 billion receivables portfolio.

Buffett also discloses, in more detail than before, that he personally writes
a small number of derivative contracts (62 outstanding) that he judges
mispriced, kept free of counterparty credit risk, distinct from the Gen Re
book he has spent five years unwinding:

> "The answer is that derivatives, just like stocks and bonds, are sometimes
> wildly mispriced." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Two positions worth $1.9 billion combined are deliberately withheld because
Berkshire was still buying them:

> "I could, of course, tell you their names. But then I would have to kill
> you." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

American Express, Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble and Wells Fargo grew per-share
earnings 18%, 9%, 8% and 11% respectively in 2006, within or above the 6-8%
range Buffett had guided to the year before. Direct foreign-exchange positions
were largely eliminated ($186 million of 2006 pre-tax profit, $2.2 billion
cumulative since 2002), replaced by indirect currency exposure through
foreign-earning equities once the interest-rate spread turned unfavorable to
direct currency positions in 2005. A stray currency footnote: $82 million of
Enron bonds bought in 2002-03, partly Euro-denominated, had already returned
$179 million of distributions plus $173 million of remaining value — a $270
million gain, partly currency-driven.

The U.S. investment-income account turned negative for the first time since
1915, meaning foreigners now earn more on their U.S. holdings than Americans
earn abroad — the trade-deficit argument's next data point after
[[Sources/2004ltr]] and [[Sources/2005ltr]].

### Succession, on the investment side

Buffett states the CEO succession plan is settled — three internal candidates,
the board already agreed on one — but concedes the investment side is less
prepared, since Lou Simpson, GEICO's equity manager, is only six years younger
than Buffett himself. The plan is to hire one or more younger people to
eventually run Berkshire's portfolio, tested in real conditions rather than
selected on paper credentials:

> "But there is far more to successful long-term investing than brains and
> performance that has recently been good." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

> "We therefore need someone genetically programmed to recognize and avoid
> serious risks, including those never before encountered." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Lou Simpson himself is the model: recruited with Jack Byrne in 1979, paid for
sustained overperformance, and never left despite being able to earn more
managing larger sums elsewhere. See [[People/WarrenBuffett]].

### Board changes: Susan Decker, and "truly independent"

Malcolm "Kim" Chace, whose family's connection to Berkshire's predecessor
companies runs back to 1929, stepped down; Susan Decker, CFO of Yahoo!, joined.
Buffett restates the four criteria — owner-oriented, business-savvy,
interested, truly independent — with "truly" doing the work:

> "I say 'truly' because many directors who are now deemed independent by
> various authorities and observers are far from that, relying heavily as they
> do on directors' fees to maintain their standard of living." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

He contrasts this with how boards actually recruit — "We're looking for a
woman," "a Hispanic," "someone from abroad" — arguing the one question nobody
asks is whether a candidate thinks like an intelligent owner. See
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### CEO compensation, from the inside

Buffett describes himself as "the Typhoid Mary of compensation committees" —
seated on 19 outside boards but assigned to a comp committee only once, and
promptly outvoted — despite personally setting pay for roughly 40 Berkshire
operating CEOs with, he says, virtually no time spent and zero voluntary CEO
departures in 42 years. His stated rule: incentives track only what a manager
controls.

> "If a CEO bats .300, he gets paid for being a .300 hitter, even if
> circumstances outside of his control cause Berkshire to perform poorly. And
> if he bats .150, he doesn't get a payoff just because the successes of others
> have enabled Berkshire to prosper mightily." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

He names the mechanism that spreads excess anyway — comparative "peer group"
consulting reports that ratchet every year's outlier into next year's
baseline — the same critique carried from [[Sources/2005ltr]]'s Fred Futile
example. See [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Philanthropy: why not a perpetual foundation

Buffett explains the 2006 commitment of the bulk of his Berkshire holdings to
five foundations, and the will provision distributing the remainder within ten
years of his estate closing — a roughly 25-year total horizon. His stated
reason for rejecting perpetuity is a market test applied to charity itself:

> "Even if the people above ground make their decisions imperfectly, they
> should be able to allocate funds more rationally than a decedent six feet
> under will have ordained decades earlier." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

He separately dismisses concern that foundation sales would depress the stock,
noting Berkshire's unusually low share turnover leaves ample room, and states
plainly that tax considerations played no role in the decision or its timing.

### The Gotrocks family, one year later: 2-and-20

The Gotrocks parable from [[Sources/2005ltr]] continues, now aimed
specifically at hedge-fund fee structures:

> "It's a lopsided system whereby 2% of your principal is paid each year to the
> manager even if he accomplishes nothing – or, for that matter, loses you a
> bundle – and, additionally, 20% of your profit is paid to him if he succeeds,
> even if his success is due simply to a rising tide." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Buffett works the arithmetic: a manager delivering a 10% gross return keeps
3.6 points (2 off the top plus 20% of the remaining 8), leaving investors 6.4%
net — on a $3 billion fund, $108 million to the manager — even when an index
fund might have delivered 15% for a token fee. He calls the arrangement, and
the willing institutional money chasing it, self-defeating for the Gotrocks by
construction.

### Walter Schloss, and the theory he embarrassed

Buffett's tribute to his 90-year-old friend doubles as his sharpest critique of
efficient-market theory. Schloss, who never attended college, ran a
successful investment partnership from 1956 to 2002 with no salary unless
clients made money, working with just his son Edwin and no secretary,
bookkeeper or access to inside information — buying roughly 1,000 mostly
"lackluster" securities by simple statistical rules learned from Ben Graham:

> "When Walter and Edwin were asked in 1989 by Outstanding Investors Digest,
> 'How would you summarize your approach?' Edwin replied, 'We try to buy stocks
> cheap.'" — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

Buffett had already presented Schloss's record publicly in 1984 as a
refutation of efficient-market theory, and reports that business-school
faculties simply declined to study it:

> "Rather than opening their minds, they closed their eyes." — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

> "Typically, a finance instructor who had the nerve to question EMT had about
> as much chance of major promotion as Galileo had of being named Pope."
> — [[Sources/2006ltr]]

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **ISCAR** — 80% of the Israeli cutting-tools maker for $4 billion, Berkshire's
  first business based outside the U.S.; the Wertheimer family retained 20%.
- **TTI** — Fort Worth electronic-components distributor, agreed the same day
  Buffett and owner Paul Andrews met.
- **PacifiCorp, Business Wire, Applied Underwriters** — the three deals agreed
  in 2005, completed for about $6 billion combined.
- Tuck-ins: Russell Corp and an agreement for VF Corp's intimate-apparel unit
  (both at Fruit of the Loom), plus further additions at MiTek, CTB, Shaw and
  Clayton.

**Insurance**

- Signed a $7.12 billion retroactive reinsurance contract with Equitas, capping
  Berkshire's exposure at $13.9 billion — the largest deal of its kind in the
  letters to this point.
- Float grew to $50.887 billion (plus $7 billion more from Equitas just after
  yearend) at a $3.838 billion underwriting profit, reversing 2005's near-zero
  result.
- Sharply reduced super-cat wind exposure as pricing fell, while stating
  willingness to lose up to $6 billion in a single well-priced event.

**Portfolio**

- Increased Wells Fargo and Procter & Gamble positions; held two unnamed
  positions worth $1.9 billion combined, still being accumulated.
- Eliminated most of the direct foreign-currency position ($186 million 2006
  gain, $2.2 billion cumulative since 2002), shifting toward indirect currency
  exposure via foreign-earning equities.
- Maintains 62 personally-managed derivative contracts, judged mispriced and
  free of counterparty credit risk.

**Corporate**

- Committed the bulk of personal Berkshire holdings to five charitable
  foundations, with a roughly 25-year total distribution horizon.
- Added Susan Decker to the board; Malcolm "Kim" Chace stepped down.
