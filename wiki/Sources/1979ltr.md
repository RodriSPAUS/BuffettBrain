# 1979 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1979: operating earnings of 18.6% on beginning net worth, and fifteen
years of compounding at 20.5% that Buffett says may have bought shareholders
nothing. The letter introduces the investor's misery index, contains the frankest
admission of a mistake in the early letters — buying fifteen-year bonds — and ends
with the restaurant analogy for choosing your own shareholders.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Which denominator, and why earnings per share is still the wrong one

New accounting required insurance equities to be carried at market, which inflates
net worth. Buffett refuses to measure against it, and the reason is that the
distortion runs both ways:

- "a large decline in securities values could result in a very low "market value"
  net worth that, in turn, could cause mediocre operating earnings to look
  unrealistically good. Alternatively, the more successful that equity investments
  have been, the larger the net worth base becomes and the poorer the operating
  performance figure appears."

So single-year performance stays measured against beginning net worth with
securities at cost: 18.6%, down from 1978.

Earnings per share rose about 20%, and he dismisses it in the same paragraph that
reports it — sharper than the 1977 version of the same argument:

- ""Earnings per share" will rise constantly on a dormant savings account or on a
  U.S. Savings Bond bearing a fixed rate of return simply because "earnings" (the
  stated interest rate) are continuously plowed back and added to the capital base.
  Thus, even a "stopped clock" can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout
  ratio is low."
- The replacement, stated as a general principle: "The primary test of managerial
  economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital
  employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the
  achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Sources/1977ltr]].

### Fifteen years of 20.5% — and the half ounce of gold

For long-term measurement Buffett does use market values and does count capital
gains. Book value per share had gone from $19.46 at the end of fiscal 1964 to
$335.85, compounding at 20.5%.

He then does something no other annual report does: he argues the number may be
worthless. First he discloses that the starting point flattered him — the 1964 book
value "somewhat overstated the intrinsic value of the enterprise, since the assets
owned at that time on either a going concern basis or a liquidating value basis were
not worth 100 cents on the dollar."

Then the real argument, that inflation plus tax can consume the whole return:

- "a business earning 20% on capital can produce a negative real return for its
  owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail."
- Worked: at 14% inflation, a sustained 20% compounded gain leaves "your after-tax
  purchasing power gain... likely to be very close to zero", because most of the
  remaining six points goes to tax on conversion.

And the measurement that makes it concrete, credited to a reader rather than
claimed as his own insight: Berkshire's 1964 book value would have bought about half
an ounce of gold, and fifteen years later, "after we have plowed back all earnings
along with much blood, sweat and tears, the book value produced will buy about the
same half ounce."

- "The rub has been that government has been exceptionally able in printing money
  and creating promises, but is unable to print gold or create oil."
- The conclusion is an admission of powerlessness: "external conditions affecting
  the stability of currency may very well be the most important factor in
  determining whether there are any real rewards from your investment in Berkshire
  Hathaway."

### The investor's misery index

The letter's most transportable idea, and it is a definition rather than a
complaint:

- "That combination - the inflation rate plus the percentage of capital that must be
  paid by the owner to transfer into his own pocket the annual earnings achieved by
  the business (i.e., ordinary income tax on dividends and capital gains tax on
  retained earnings) - can be thought of as an "investor's misery index". When this
  index exceeds the rate of return earned on equity by the business, the investor's
  purchasing power (real capital) shrinks even though he consumes nothing at all."

Berkshire cannot fix it: "We have no corporate solution to this problem; high
inflation rates will not help us earn higher rates of return on equity." The idea
becomes the equity-value-added argument of [[Sources/1981ltr]], where the crossbar
is passive after-tax return rather than inflation alone. See
[[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

Buffett also notes what the record was achieved without: low financial and
operating leverage, and "without significant issuance or repurchase of shares.
Basically, we have worked with the capital with which we started." Thirteen
businesses bought outright and six started — and a remark about the sellers that is
easy to skip and worth keeping: "those who have sold to us have, almost without
exception, treated us with exceptional honor and fairness, both at the time of sale
and subsequently."

### Waumbec: turnarounds seldom turn

The clearest mistake report in the early letters, and the one that produces a rule.
Buffett had bought Waumbec Mills in Manchester, expanding the textile commitment:

- "By any statistical test, the purchase price was an extraordinary bargain; we
  bought well below the working capital of the business and, in effect, got very
  substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing. But the
  purchase was a mistake. While we labored mightily, new problems arose as fast as
  old problems were tamed."
- The rule extracted from it: "Both our operating and investment experience cause us
  to conclude that "turnarounds" seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent
  are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a
  poor business purchased at a bargain price."

That sentence is the pivot of Buffett's whole method, and it arrives here as the
lesson of a specific failed purchase rather than as philosophy.

The contrast is drawn with a business at the other extreme: "In some businesses - a
network TV station, for example - it is virtually impossible to avoid earning
extraordinary returns on tangible capital employed in the business. And assets in
such businesses sell at equally extraordinary prices, one thousand cents or more on
the dollar... Despite a fancy price tag, the "easy" business may be the better route
to go."

He also refuses to blame the managers: "This is not a reflection on the managers,
but rather on the industry in which they operate."

The same defence is made for Ken Chace six years later, as the mills finally close
in [[Sources/1985ltr]].

See [[Concepts/Moat]], [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Bonds: the last long fixed-price contract in an inflationary world

The bond section is an argument by inconsistency, and it is the sharpest writing in
the letter. Insurers had decided a twelve-month auto policy was too long to price in
an inflationary world and moved to six months —

- "But, having decided that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed
  price for insurance in an inflationary world, they then have turned around, taken
  the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed
  price for thirty or forty years."

Generalised: "The very long-term bond contract has been the last major fixed price
contract of extended duration still regularly initiated in an inflation-ridden
world." A buyer asking for a firm price on newsprint or office space through 1985 "would be
greeted with laughter".

Why nobody stopped it: "The buyers (borrowers) and middlemen (underwriters) of money
hardly could be expected to raise the question of whether it all made sense, and the
sellers (lenders) slept through an economic and contractual revolution."

Buffett then applies it to himself, and the self-assessment is unusually severe
because Berkshire had in fact been more cautious than the industry:

- "the mild degree of caution that we exercised was an improper response to the
  world unfolding about us. You do not adequately protect yourself by being half
  awake while others are sleeping. It was a mistake to buy fifteen-year bonds, and
  yet we did; we made an even more serious mistake in not selling them (at losses, if
  necessary) when our present views began to crystallize."
- He anticipates the obvious objection rather than waiting for it: "it would be fair
  for you to ask why we weren't writing about this subject last year."
- And he holds himself to a higher standard than the people who lost more: "we are
  taking our lumps in bonds and feel that, in a sense, our mistakes should be viewed
  less charitably than the mistakes of those who went about their business unmindful
  of the developing problems."
- The link back to Waumbec: "Harking back to our textile experience, we should have
  realized the futility of trying to be very clever (via sinking funds and other
  special type issues) in an area where the tide was running heavily against us."

The case against his own position is then stated fairly — inflation is man-made and
may be man-mastered, current rates may already compensate, and Berkshire "even may
miss large profits from a major rebound in bond prices." What settles it is a
consistency test rather than a forecast:

- "our unwillingness to fix a price now for a pound of See's candy or a yard of
  Berkshire cloth to be delivered in 2010 or 2020 makes us equally unwilling to buy
  bonds which set a price on money now for use in those years. Overall, we opt for
  Polonius (slightly restated): "Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender
  be.""

See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] and [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Underwriting discipline, with the cost of it spelled out

The 1978 forecast is scored honestly: the industry ratio rose over three points from
roughly 97.4% to 100.7%, as predicted, and Berkshire's own fell from 98.2% to 97.1%.
The 1980 forecast is worse for the industry and, this time, not better for Berkshire
— with a parenthesis that closes off the obvious temptation: "(Don't worry - we
won't hold back to try to validate that forecast.)"

Phil Liesche's segment produced $8.4 million of underwriting profit on about $82
million of earned premiums, on *lower* volume than 1978. Buffett makes the point
that the shrinkage is the achievement:

- "We hear a great many insurance managers talk about being willing to reduce volume
  in order to underwrite profitably, but we find that very few actually do so. Phil
  Liesche is an exception: if business makes sense, he writes it; if it doesn't, he
  rejects it."
- And what the policy costs, which is the part that makes it credible: "It is our
  policy not to lay off people because of the large fluctuations in work load
  produced by such voluntary volume changes. We would rather have some slack in the
  organization from time to time than keep everyone terribly busy writing business on
  which we are going to lose money."
- "We believe such strong-mindedness is as rare as it is sound - and absolutely
  essential to the running of a first-class casualty insurance operation."

On the industry's direction, Buffett disagrees with the consensus cycle view for a
structural reason that recurs through 1982–84: "Present interest rates encourage the
obtaining of business at underwriting loss levels formerly regarded as totally
unacceptable. Managers decry the folly of underwriting at a loss to obtain investment
income, but we believe that many will. Thus we expect that competition will create a
new threshold of tolerance for underwriting losses".

And he identifies the luck flattering current results: accident frequency had fallen
with driving habits under higher gas prices, and "This dosage of serendipity won't
last indefinitely." Forecast: an industry combined ratio around 105 over five years.

On reinsurance he is close to warning off the whole business. Much of it involves an
exceptionally long tail — "a characteristic that allows catastrophic current loss
experience to fester undetected for many years" — so entrants may be "blissfully
unaware until they are in over their heads". And: "It will be hard for us to be a
whole lot smarter than the crowd".

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### Choosing your shareholders — the restaurant

The financial-reporting section is the origin of Berkshire's stance on its own
shareholder register, five years before the stock-split argument of
[[Sources/1983ltr]].

Buffett describes the constituency he is writing for: about 98% of shares are held
at year end by people who held them at the start, and perhaps 90% are owned by
investors for whom Berkshire is their largest holding. So the report builds on
previous years rather than restating — "You get more useful information this way, and
we don't get bored."

The standard for who writes it:

- "when you do receive a communication from us, it will come from the fellow you are
  paying to run the business. Your Chairman has a firm belief that owners are
  entitled to hear directly from the CEO as to what is going on and how he evaluates
  the business, currently and prospectively. You would demand that in a private
  company; you should expect no less in a public company. A once-a-year report of
  stewardship should not be turned over to a staff specialist or public relations
  consultant".
- The symmetry that justifies it: "We don't expect a public relations document when
  our operating managers tell us what is going on, and we don't feel you should
  receive such a document."

Then the mechanism, credited to Phil Fisher — a corporation attracts shareholders
the way a restaurant attracts customers, and cannot keep changing its menu: "If the
business vacillated between French cuisine and take-out chicken, the result would be
a revolving door of confused and dissatisfied customers."

- The claim that follows: "In large part, companies obtain the shareholder
  constituency that they seek and deserve. If they focus their thinking and
  communications on short-term results or short-term stock market consequences they
  will, in large part, attract shareholders who focus on the same factors. And if
  they are cynical in their treatment of investors, eventually that cynicism is
  highly likely to be returned by the investment community."
- And why high trading volume is a strange thing to want: "such managements are
  saying that they want a good many of the existing clientele continually to desert
  them in favor of new ones - because you can't add lots of new owners (with new
  expectations) without losing lots of former owners."

See [[Principles/Fidelity]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Decentralisation, with the trade-off admitted

- "Your company is run on the principle of centralization of financial decisions at
  the top (the very top, it might be added), and rather extreme delegation of
  operating authority to a number of key managers at the individual company or
  business unit level. We could just field a basketball team with our corporate
  headquarters group (which utilizes only about 1500 square feet of space)."
- The cost is stated before the benefit: "This approach produces an occasional major
  mistake that might have been eliminated or minimized through closer operating
  controls. But it also eliminates large layers of costs and dramatically speeds
  decision-making."
- And the real payoff is recruitment: it "enables us to attract and retain some
  extraordinarily talented individuals - people who simply can't be hired in the
  normal course of events - who find working for Berkshire to be almost identical to
  running their own show."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The equity portfolio — $185.4 million at cost, $336.7 million at market

Thirteen positions above $5 million of market value. The largest:

| Company | Cost | Market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GEICO common (5,730,114 sh) | $28.3m | $68.0m |
| The Washington Post (1,868,000 sh) | $10.6m | $39.2m |
| Handy & Harman (1,007,500 sh) | $21.8m | $38.5m |
| SAFECO (953,750 sh) | $23.9m | $35.5m |
| Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical (1,211,834 sh) | $20.6m | $23.3m |
| Interpublic Group (711,180 sh) | $4.5m | $23.7m |
| F. W. Woolworth (771,900 sh) | $15.5m | $19.4m |

GEICO is now much the largest holding — the convertible preferred of 1977–78 is
gone and the common position has grown to 5,730,114 shares. New names include
Amerada Hess, General Foods, Media General and Woolworth. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

Buffett attaches a warning to the record rather than a boast: "We currently believe
that equity markets in 1980 are likely to evolve in a manner that will result in an
underperformance by our portfolio for the first time in recent years. We very much
like the companies in which we have major investments, and plan no changes to try to
attune ourselves to the markets of a specific year."

### Bonds — no net purchases of straight long-term issues

For several years the insurance companies had not been net buyers of straight
long-term bonds, concentrating instead on shorter issues with sinking funds and on
apparent mispricings. Net fixed-dollar commitments were limited to convertibles,
whose conversion rights give "a far shorter average life than implied by the maturity
terms of the issues".

The fifteen-year bonds already held are written off as a mistake that will be paid
for annually.

### Surety reinsurance — a new line under Chet Noble

Entered at year end, deliberately small, seeking client companies "who appreciate the
need for a long term "partnership" relationship with their reinsurers."

### Precision Steel — first full contribution

Appears in the earnings table for the first time, contributing $3.25 million pre-tax
in aggregate and $1.48 million to Berkshire's share.

### Illinois National Bank — the last year as a subsidiary

Earned approximately 2.3% on average assets, "a level again over three times that
achieved by the average major bank, and more than double that of banks regarded as
outstanding." The Bank Holding Company Act required divestiture by 31 December 1980.

The plan changed during the year: the Federal Reserve took the position that after a
spin-off no Berkshire officer or director could serve the bank, so a sale of 80–100%
was being investigated instead. The selection criterion is stated plainly and is not
price: "We will be most choosy about any purchaser, and our selection will not be
based solely on price. The bank and its management have treated us exceptionally
well and, if we have to sell, we want to be sure that they are treated equally as
well."

Buffett also tells shareholders what the sale will cost them: "we do not expect to be
able to fully, or even in very large part, replace the earning power represented by
the bank from the proceeds of the sale of the bank. You simply can't buy high quality
businesses at the sort of price/earnings multiple likely to prevail on our bank
sale."

### NASDAQ listing

Berkshire began trading on NASDAQ in 1979, which solved a dissemination problem —
before it, neither the Wall Street Journal nor the Dow-Jones ticker reported
Berkshire's earnings, "even though such earnings were one hundred or more times the
level of some companies whose reports they regularly picked up."
