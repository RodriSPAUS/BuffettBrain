# 1991 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1991: a 39.6% gain in net worth driven almost entirely by Coca-Cola and
Gillette, the H. H. Brown acquisition, and Buffett's interim chairmanship of
Salomon. Its lasting contribution is the definition of an economic franchise —
written while watching one disappear.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Sources of Reported Earnings, and look-through earnings restated

The 1991 segment table (Berkshire's after-tax share, in thousands) shows Insurance Underwriting -$77,229, Insurance Net Investment Income $285,173, the newly-acquired H. H. Brown $8,611, Buffalo News $21,841, Fechheimer $6,843, Kirby $22,555, Nebraska Furniture Mart $6,993, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing $15,901, See's Candies $25,575, Wesco (non-insurance) $8,777, World Book $15,487, plus goodwill amortization, interest expense, and Sales of Securities of $124,155, for Total Earnings — All Entities of $439,908 versus $394,093 in 1990. Buffett notes that with the business count growing, the letter will "rotate coverage" of non-insurance operations rather than discuss each one every year.

The look-through table walks investee by investee: Capital Cities/ABC (18.1% owned, $61M undistributed), Coca-Cola (7.0%, $69M), Freddie Mac (3.4%, $15M), Gillette (11.0% for nine months post-conversion, $23M), GEICO (48.2%, $69M), Washington Post (14.6%, $10M), and Wells Fargo (9.6%, -$17M — a loss, since Wells's negative retained earnings offset the dividends Berkshire received). Total look-through earnings came to $516 million, down from $602 million in 1990 — the 14% decline flagged earlier in this letter, driven by declining media earnings, the Gillette preferred's conversion, the Wells Fargo break-even, and lower insurance profits.

### The franchise definition

The point at which the moat idea becomes testable rather than metaphorical.

- "An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or
  desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is
  not subject to price regulation."
- How you know: "The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a
  company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and
  thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."
- The property that matters most: "Moreover, franchises can tolerate
  mis-management. Inept managers may diminish a franchise's profitability, but they
  cannot inflict mortal damage."

And the contrasting category, defined just as precisely:

- ""a business" earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if
  supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not
  last long."
- "With superior management, a company may maintain its status as a low-cost
  operator for a much longer time, but even then unceasingly faces the possibility
  of competitive attack. And a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor
  management."

That last sentence is the sharpest statement in the letters of how much a manager
can actually do — read against [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]], where the 1989
lesson said the opposite about bad businesses. Both are true: a franchise survives
a bad manager; a business does not.

See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]].

### Watching a franchise die

The definition is written in the course of explaining a loss, which is what makes
it credible.

- "The fact is that newspaper, television, and magazine properties have begun to
  resemble businesses more than franchises in their economic behavior."
- The cause is structural, not cyclical: "The economic strength of once-mighty
  media enterprises continues to erode as retailing patterns change and advertising
  and entertainment choices proliferate."
- Why nobody saw it: "In the business world, unfortunately, the rear-view mirror is
  always clearer than the windshield: A few years back no one linked to the media
  business - neither lenders, owners nor financial analysts - saw the economic
  deterioration that was in store for the industry."
- The mechanism: "consumers looking for information and entertainment (their
  primary interest being the latter) enjoy greatly broadened choices as to where to
  find them. Unfortunately, demand can't expand in response to this new supply: 500
  million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day are all that's available."
- What was lost, precisely: "Until recently, media properties possessed the three
  characteristics of a franchise and consequently could both price aggressively and
  be managed loosely."

### The valuation arithmetic of a lost franchise

Buffett shows what a change in assumption does to a price, and the answer is
brutal.

- The old view: "ownership of a media property could be construed as akin to owning
  a perpetual annuity set to grow at 6% a year. Say, next, that a discount rate of
  10% was used to determine the present value of that earnings stream. One could
  then calculate that it was appropriate to pay a whopping $25 million for a
  property with current after-tax earnings of $1 million."
- The revised view: "Under our revised assumption, $1 million of earnings,
  discounted by the same 10%, translates to a $10 million valuation."
- The conclusion: "Thus a seemingly modest shift in assumptions reduces the
  property's valuation to 10 times after-tax earnings"
- And the general principle underneath: "Dollars are dollars whether they are
  derived from the operation of media properties or of steel mills. What in the
  past caused buyers to value a dollar of earnings from media far higher than a
  dollar from steel was that the earnings of a media property were expected to
  constantly grow (without the business requiring much additional capital)"

A 60% loss of value from one changed assumption, with no change in current
earnings. See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### Holding anyway

- "one of the rules by which we run Berkshire is that we do not sell businesses -
  or investee holdings that we have classified as permanent - simply because we see
  ways to use the money more advantageously elsewhere."

Stated in the same letter that reports the value decline, which is the test of
whether such a rule means anything. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Look-through earnings

- The definition: "look-through earnings, which consist of: (1) the operating
  earnings reported in the previous section, plus; (2) the retained operating
  earnings of major investees that, under GAAP accounting, are not reflected in our
  profits, less; (3) an allowance for the tax that would be paid by Berkshire if
  these retained earnings of investees had instead been distributed to us."
- Why it is the target: "I've told you that over time look-through earnings must
  increase at about 15% annually if our intrinsic business value is to grow at that
  rate."
- The track record: "since present management took over in 1965, our look-through
  earnings have grown at almost the identical 23% rate of gain recorded for book
  value."
- And the bad news, reported first: "Last year, however, our look-through earnings
  did not grow at all but rather declined by 14%."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] — retained
earnings at an investee belong to the owner whether or not they are distributed.

### Size, again, with sharper numbers

- "When we were working with capital of $20 million, an idea or business producing
  $1 million of profit added five percentage points to our return for the year. Now
  we need a $370 million idea (i.e., one contributing over $550 million of pre-tax
  profit) to achieve the same result. And there are many more ways to make $1
  million than to make $370 million."
- The target restated: "If our growth in book value is to keep up with a 15% pace,
  we must earn $22 billion during the next decade. Wish us luck - we'll need it."

### Salomon, and what it revealed about Berkshire

- The framing: "On August 18 of last year, when I was elected Interim Chairman of
  Salomon Inc, it was a different story: I put my mouth where our money was."
- What taking the job proved: "My decision to take the job carried with it an
  implicit but important message: Berkshire's operating managers are so outstanding
  that I knew I could materially reduce the time I was spending at the company and
  yet remain confident that its economic progress would not skip a beat."
- His own role, reduced to two lines: "My job is merely to treat them right and to
  allocate the capital they generate."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### Insurance Operations: the cost-of-float table extended, and super-cat's first full year

The industry combined-ratio table runs through 1991 (est. 109.1), and Buffett's own cost-of-float table — tracking every year back to 1967 — shows 1991's underwriting loss of $119.6 million against average float of $1,895.0 million, an approximate cost of funds of 6.31%:

> "As you can see, our cost of funds in 1991 was well below the U. S. Government's cost on newly-issued long-term bonds. We have in fact beat the government's rate in 20 of the 25 years we have been in the insurance business, often by a wide margin." [[Sources/1991ltr]] [[Concepts/Float]]

Super-cat coverage had its first full year of real testing: Buffett estimates $100 million in annual premiums could swing between a $100 million profit and a $200 million loss depending on catastrophe activity, and that Berkshire prices the business expecting to pay out about 90% of premiums over the long run. The year's single qualifying super-cat was a September Japanese typhoon, not Hurricane Bob or the Oakland fire, generating an estimated industry loss of $4-5 billion — larger than Hurricane Hugo's.

### Marketable Common Stocks: Guinness, and the Keynes letter

The over-$100-million common stock table at 12/31/91 (cost/market, in thousands) added a new position: Capital Cities/ABC $517,500/$1,300,500, Coca-Cola $1,023,920/$3,747,675, Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corp. $77,245/$343,090, GEICO $45,713/$1,363,150, Gillette $600,000/$1,347,000, Guinness PLC $264,782/$296,755, Washington Post $9,731/$336,050, and Wells Fargo $289,431/$290,000.

> "Our Guinness holding represents Berkshire's first significant investment in a company domiciled outside the United States." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

Buffett justifies the concentrated, buy-and-hold approach at length, quoting a 1934 letter from John Maynard Keynes to F. C. Scott:

> "As time goes on, I get more and more convinced that the right method in investment is to put fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the management of which one thoroughly believes. It is a mistake to think that one limits one's risk by spreading too much between enterprises about which one knows little and has no reason for special confidence. . . ." [[Sources/1991ltr]] [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]]

### Mistake Du Jour: the $1.4 billion of Fannie Mae never bought

Extending the 1989 "Mistakes" essay into an occasional recurring feature, Buffett confesses to a 1988 decision to buy 30 million (split-adjusted) shares of Fannie Mae — a business he understood, run by a CEO he trusted — only to stop buying when the price rose after 7 million shares were acquired, and then, "in an even sillier move," sell the position outright.

> "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation for my amateurish behavior vis-a-vis Fannie Mae. But there isn't one. What I can give you is an estimate as of yearend 1991 of the approximate gain that Berkshire didn't make because of your Chairman's mistake: about $1.4 billion." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

### Fixed-Income Securities, and the USAir downgrade

1991's fixed-income changes: the Gillette preferred converted to common on its call; the RJR Nabisco holding was eliminated via exchange offer and call; new positions were taken in American Express (a "Perc" with an 8.85% dividend, mandatorily convertible into up to 12,244,898 shares) and First Empire State Corp. (9% preferred, convertible at $78.91, bought mainly out of respect for CEO Bob Wilmers). Salomon's preferred rose slightly in value despite the firm's 1991 crisis. USAir, by contrast, is marked down hard as the airline industry entered what Buffett calls a "decimating period," with Midway, Pan Am, and America West all filing for bankruptcy that year:

> "Last year I told you that our USAir investment "should work out all right unless the industry is decimated during the next few years." Unfortunately 1991 was a decimating period for the industry, as Midway, Pan Am and America West all entered bankruptcy." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### H. H. Brown — the year's acquisition

- Found by accident: "last spring Frank was playing golf in Florida with John
  Loomis, a long-time friend of mine as well as a Berkshire shareholder, who is
  always on the alert for something that might fit us."
- The decision speed: "Frank promptly gave me a call. I thought right away that we
  would make a deal and before long it was done."
- What actually sold it: "Much of my enthusiasm for this purchase came from Frank's
  willingness to continue as CEO. Like most of our managers, he has no financial
  need to work but does so because he loves the game and likes to excel."
- On why such people cannot be recruited conventionally: "Managers of this stripe
  cannot be "hired" in the normal sense of the word."
- The business, described without flattery: "Shoes are a tough business - of the
  billion pairs purchased in the United States each year, about 85% are imported -
  and most manufacturers in the industry do poorly."
- And the compensation scheme Buffett singles out: "A number of key managers are
  paid an annual salary of $7,800, to which is added a designated percentage of the
  profits of the company after these are reduced by a charge for capital employed."

That last arrangement — profit share net of a capital charge — is the incentive
design Buffett argued for in 1985 against fixed-price options, found in the wild.
See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Coca-Cola and Gillette drive the year

- "Our outsized gain in book value in 1991 resulted from a phenomenon not apt to be
  repeated: a dramatic rise in the price-earnings ratios of Coca-Cola and Gillette.
  These two stocks accounted for nearly $1.6 billion of our $2.1 billion growth in
  net worth last year."
- The compounding stated plainly: "When we loaded up on Coke three years ago,
  Berkshire's net worth was $3.4 billion; now our Coke stock alone is worth more
  than that."

### See's — pricing power identified in retrospect

- "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the
  business had untapped pricing power."
- The luck acknowledged: "First, the transaction was not derailed by our dumb
  insistence on a $25 million price."
- The capital story that makes it the standing example: "our beginning base of $7
  million has had to be supplemented by only $18 million of reinvested earnings.
  Meanwhile, See's remaining pre-tax profits of $410 million were distributed to
  Blue Chip/Berkshire during the 20 years for these companies to deploy"
- And the handshake: "we shook hands with Chuck on a compensation arrangement -
  conceived in about five minutes and never reduced to a written contract - that
  remains unchanged to this day."

Pricing power is condition (3) of the franchise test, identified in the same letter
that defines it. See [[Cases/SeeCandies]].
