# 2018 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's GAAP net income was $4.0 billion in 2018 — a figure Buffett immediately tells shareholders to largely ignore, since it is dominated by swings from a new mark-to-market accounting rule (a $20.6 billion non-cash loss from reduced unrealized gains, on top of $24.8 billion of real operating earnings, a $3.0 billion Kraft Heinz impairment, and $2.8 billion of realized gains). This is the letter that formally retires book value per share as Berkshire's headline performance metric after nearly three decades, replacing it going forward with market price, and introduces the "five groves" framework for understanding Berkshire's intrinsic value. It also carries "The American Tailwind," a long essay tracing 77 years of U.S. economic growth since Buffett's first stock purchase in 1942, and a tribute to Tony Nicely on his retirement as GEICO's CEO.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A New Accounting Rule Makes GAAP Earnings "Useless" for Analysis
A new GAAP rule forces net changes in unrealized gains/losses on Berkshire's ~$173 billion equity portfolio into reported net income, producing swings Buffett calls exactly what he predicted the year before:

> "Rather, both of us have consistently thought that at Berkshire this mark-to-market change would produce what I described as 'wild and capricious swings in our bottom line.'" [[Sources/2018ltr]]

Quarterly GAAP results bore this out: a $1.1 billion loss in Q1, a $12 billion profit in Q2, an $18.5 billion profit in Q3, and a $25.4 billion loss in Q4 — while actual operating earnings were steady throughout and, for the full year, exceeded 2016's prior high of $17.6 billion by 41%. His advice:

> "Focus on operating earnings, paying little attention to gains or losses of any variety." [[Sources/2018ltr]]

### Book Value Retired as Berkshire's Scorecard
After nearly three decades of opening each letter with the change in per-share book value, Buffett announces its retirement, citing three reasons: Berkshire's shift from marketable securities toward controlled operating businesses (carried on the books well below true value); the growing mismatch this creates between book and intrinsic value; and the mechanical fact that Berkshire share repurchases (expected to become more common) raise per-share intrinsic value while lowering per-share book value, pulling the two further apart. Going forward, Berkshire's stock market price becomes the primary performance yardstick.

### The Five Groves Framework
Buffett introduces a new lens for estimating Berkshire's intrinsic value: five "groves" that can each be appraised as a whole rather than tree-by-tree. The first and most valuable grove is the wholly- or majority-owned non-insurance operating businesses, which earned $16.8 billion in 2018 net of "all income taxes, interest payments, managerial compensation (whether cash or stock-based), restructuring expenses, depreciation, amortization and home-office overhead" — a deliberate contrast with Wall Street's fondness for adjusted EBITDA. Buffett invokes Lincoln against the practice of excluding stock-based compensation and restructuring costs from "earnings":

> "Abraham Lincoln once posed the question: 'If you call a dog's tail a leg, how many legs does it have?' and then answered his own query: 'Four, because calling a tail a leg doesn't make it one.'" [[Sources/2018ltr]]

The second grove is the marketable-equity portfolio (~$173 billion at year-end, versus a purchase-year total of $43 billion bought and $19 billion sold in 2018); the third is a set of jointly-controlled businesses (26.7% of Kraft Heinz, 50% of Berkadia and Electric Transmission Texas, 38.6% of Pilot Flying J, contributing about $1.3 billion); the fourth is cash and Treasury bills ($112 billion, plus $20 billion of other fixed income, with a self-imposed floor of at least $20 billion in cash equivalents); and the fifth — described separately later in the letter — is the insurance operation and its float. Buffett's summary of why the structure matters:

> "At Berkshire, the whole is greater–considerably greater–than the sum of the parts." [[Sources/2018ltr]]

Berkshire's five largest equity holdings retained far more in earnings than they paid in dividends in 2018 (American Express: $237M dividends / $997M retained; Apple: $745M / $2,502M; Bank of America: $551M / $2,096M; Coca-Cola: $624M / –$21M; Wells Fargo: $809M / $1,263M; total $2,966M / $6,837M), illustrating Buffett's long-standing claim that retained investee earnings have historically delivered Berkshire more than $1 of capital gains per $1 reinvested.

### Repurchases and the End of Quarterly-Number Culture
Buffett reiterates that repurchases only benefit continuing shareholders when made below intrinsic value, and explains Berkshire's aversion to managing toward quarterly Street expectations — noting Berkshire has no company-wide budget and may be alone in the Fortune 500 in not preparing monthly consolidated financials. He describes how "innocent" earnings management habitually escalates into fraud, from trade-loading to reserve manipulation.

### Non-Insurance Operations and Tax Reform's Windfall
Non-insurance pre-tax income rose 24% to $20.8 billion in 2018, but after-tax gains rose 47% because of the corporate tax-rate cut from 35% to 21%. Buffett frames the pre-2018 35% rate as the government effectively holding a special, non-voting class of Berkshire stock ("the AA shares") that received large "dividends" in the form of tax payments — 40% of that implicit ownership was returned to shareholders free of charge with the rate cut. BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (90.9% owned) earned a combined $9.3 billion pre-tax, up 6%; the next tier (Clayton Homes, IMC, Lubrizol, Marmon, Precision Castparts) earned $6.4 billion, up from $5.5 billion.

### Insurance: Float Past $122 Billion
Float reached $122.732 billion at year-end 2018 (including life, annuity and health insurance float for the first time):

| Year | Float ($M) |
| --- | --- |
| 1970 | $39 |
| 1980 | 237 |
| 1990 | 1,632 |
| 2000 | 27,871 |
| 2010 | 65,832 |
| 2018 | 122,732 |

Berkshire posted an underwriting profit in 15 of the past 16 years (the exception being 2017's hurricane-driven $3.2 billion loss), with $27 billion cumulative pre-tax underwriting gain over that span, $2 billion of it in 2018. The discipline behind that record is described in familiar terms: "At Berkshire it is a religion, Old Testament style." [[Sources/2018ltr]] Buffett also identifies deferred income taxes ($50.5 billion, including $14.7 billion tied to unrealized equity gains and $28.3 billion from accelerated depreciation) as an interest-free funding source alongside float.

### GEICO and Tony Nicely
A dedicated section traces GEICO's history: its brush with bankruptcy under mispriced policies before Jack Byrne's 1976 turnaround (during which Berkshire built a roughly 50% stake for just $47 million, largely through GEICO's own buybacks); Tony Nicely's 1993 promotion to CEO when GEICO ranked a distant seventh in U.S. auto insurance by volume; and Berkshire's 1995 purchase of the remaining half for $2.3 billion. By 2018, GEICO's sales were 1,200% above 1995 levels, with $15.5 billion of cumulative pre-tax underwriting profit and float grown from $2.5 billion to $22.1 billion since the buyout. Buffett estimates Nicely personally added more than $50 billion to Berkshire's intrinsic value, and announces his June 2018 retirement as CEO in favor of Bill Roberts, with Nicely remaining Chairman.

> "GEICO is now America's Number Two auto insurer, with sales 1,200% greater than it recorded in 1995." [[Sources/2018ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

### Investments: New Positions
The Kraft Heinz stake (325,442,152 shares, cost $9.8 billion, carried at $13.8 billion GAAP after a 2018 impairment, market value $14 billion) is again excluded from the ranked table. New positions appeared in JPMorgan Chase and the top table also showed Apple grown to $36,044M cost / $40,271M market (5.4%), Bank of America $11,650M/$22,642M (9.5%), American Express $1,287M/$14,452M (17.9%), and Wells Fargo $10,639M/$20,706M (9.8%), for total common stocks of $102,867M cost / $172,757M market.

### The American Tailwind
A major essay marking 77 years since Buffett's first stock purchase (three shares of Cities Service preferred, $114.75, at age 11 in March 1942). He calculates that $114.75 invested in a no-fee S&P 500 index fund with dividends reinvested would have grown to $606,811 by January 31, 2019 (a 5,288-for-1 gain), versus roughly $4,200 if instead used to buy gold — and notes that a hypothetical tax-free institutional investor's $1 million would have grown to $5.3 billion, or only $2.65 billion after a 1% annual fee to "helpers," cutting the result in half. He credits America's growth to compounding "savings" (retained earnings, in national-accounts terms) across seven Republican and seven Democratic presidencies through wars, a financial panic, and a 400-fold rise in the national debt, framing Berkshire's own success as substantially a product of what he calls The American Tailwind.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Metric change**: Book value per share retired as the headline performance measure; market price adopted going forward
- **GAAP earnings**: $4.0 billion (dominated by a new mark-to-market rule), versus $24.8 billion of actual operating earnings
- **Equities**: ~$43 billion of marketable equities bought in 2018 versus $19 billion sold; new position in JPMorgan Chase
- **Insurance**: Underwriting profit in 15 of the last 16 years; float grew to $122.732 billion
- **Tax reform windfall**: After-tax non-insurance earnings +47% on the corporate rate cut from 35% to 21%
- **GEICO succession**: Tony Nicely retired as CEO (June 2018) in favor of Bill Roberts, remaining Chairman
- **Cash position**: $112 billion in Treasury bills plus $20 billion in other fixed income, with a self-imposed $20 billion minimum-cash floor
