# 1987 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1987 — the year of the October crash — with a 19.5% gain in net worth.
This is the letter that contains the Mr. Market allegory in full, and the
"Sainted Seven" return-on-equity analysis.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Mr. Market

The canonical statement in the entire collection. Buffett attributes it to Graham
and builds his own investing stance on top of it.

- "Ben Graham, my friend and teacher, long ago described the mental attitude
  toward market fluctuations that I believe to be most conducive to investment
  success. He said that you should imagine market quotations as coming from a
  remarkably accommodating fellow named Mr. Market who is your partner in a
  private business."
- "Without fail, Mr. Market appears daily and names a price at which he will either
  buy your interest or sell you his. Even though the business that the two of you
  own may have economic characteristics that are stable, Mr. Market's quotations
  will be anything but."
- The diagnosis: "For, sad to say, the poor fellow has incurable emotional
  problems. At times he feels euphoric and can see only the favorable factors
  affecting the business."
- The crucial asymmetry: "Mr. Market has another endearing characteristic: He
  doesn't mind being ignored. If his quotation is uninteresting to you today, he
  will be back with a new one tomorrow. Transactions are strictly at your option.
  Under these conditions, the more manic-depressive his behavior, the better for
  you."
- The warning: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his
  pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful."
- And the precondition most readers skip: "Indeed, if you aren't certain that you
  understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't
  belong in the game. As they say in poker, "If you've been in the game 30 minutes
  and you don't know who the patsy is, you're the patsy.""

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]].

### The stance that makes it workable

Mr. Market only helps if you already think like an owner.

- "Whenever Charlie and I buy common stocks for Berkshire's insurance companies
  (leaving aside arbitrage purchases, discussed later) we approach the transaction
  as if we were buying into a private business."
- "We do not have in mind any time or price for sale. Indeed, we are willing to
  hold a stock indefinitely so long as we expect the business to increase in
  intrinsic value at a satisfactory rate."
- "When investing, we view ourselves as business analysts - not as market analysts,
  not as macroeconomic analysts, and not even as security analysts."
- The test of whether you mean it: "a prolonged suspension of trading in the
  securities we hold would not bother us any more than does the lack of daily
  quotations on World Book or Fechheimer."
- "Eventually, our economic fate will be determined by the economic fate of the
  business we own, whether our ownership is partial or total."

See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Voting machine, weighing machine

- "As Ben said: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long
  run it is a weighing machine." The speed at which a business's success is
  recognized, furthermore, is not that important as long as the company's intrinsic
  value is increasing at a satisfactory rate."
- With the inversion that makes it useful rather than merely patient: "In fact,
  delayed recognition can be an advantage: It may give us the chance to buy more of
  a good thing at a bargain price."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### The Sainted Seven — what a moat looks like in the accounts

Buffett measures his seven largest non-financial businesses the way he tells
readers to measure any business: return on the capital actually employed.

- The set-up: "In 1987, these seven business units had combined operating earnings
  before interest and taxes of $180 million."
- Why that figure alone means nothing: "By itself, this figure says nothing about
  economic performance. To evaluate that, we must know how much total capital -
  debt and equity - was needed to produce these earnings."
- The answer: "If these seven business units had operated as a single company,
  their 1987 after-tax earnings would have been approximately $100 million - a
  return of about 57% on equity capital."
- Benchmarked: "among the 500 largest industrial companies and 500 largest service
  companies, only six had averaged a return on equity of over 30% during the
  previous decade."
- And the distinction that keeps the analysis honest: "the managers of the units
  should be judged by the returns they achieve on the underlying assets; what we
  pay for a business does not affect the amount of capital its manager has to work
  with."
- The consequence for Berkshire: "because so little capital is required to run
  these businesses, they can grow while concurrently making almost all of their
  earnings available for deployment in new opportunities."

This is [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]] and [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] measured
rather than asserted.

### Management by not managing

- "With managers like ours, my partner, Charlie Munger, and I have little to do
  with operations. in fact, it is probably fair to say that if we did more, less
  would be accomplished."
- "We have no corporate meetings, no corporate budgets, and no performance reviews"
- "After all, what can we tell the Blumkins about home furnishings, or the Heldmans
  about uniforms?"

With a note on why the praise should be believed, given how routine such praise
is: "CEOs seldom tell their shareholders that they have assembled a bunch of
turkeys to run things." See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### Insurance: a bad industry Berkshire escapes on two counts

- The verdict: "The insurance industry is cursed with a set of dismal economic
  characteristics that make for a poor long-term outlook: hundreds of competitors,
  ease of entry, and a product that cannot be differentiated in any meaningful
  way."
- The narrow exception, the same one stated in 1982: "In such a commodity-like
  business, only a very low-cost operator or someone operating in a protected, and
  usually small, niche can sustain high profitability levels."
- Why the industry destroys its own good years: "most managers in commodity
  industries abhor shortage conditions - even though those are the only
  circumstances permitting them good returns. Whenever shortages appear, the
  typical manager simply can't wait to expand capacity and thereby plug the hole
  through which money is showering upon him."
- Berkshire's escape: "we differentiate our product by our financial strength,
  which exceeds that of all others in the industry."
- With an honest limit on that advantage: "This strength, however, is limited in
  its usefulness. It means nothing in the personal insurance field"
- And when it does count: "When a buyer really focuses on whether a $10 million
  claim can be easily paid by his insurer five or ten years down the road, and when
  he takes into account the possibility that poor underwriting conditions may then
  coincide with depressed financial markets and defaults by reinsurer, he will find
  only a few companies he can trust."
- The cycle called, correctly: "In 1988, the revenue gain is certain to be far
  below our 10% "equilibrium" figure. Clearly, the party is over."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Concepts/Moat]].

### October 1987 and portfolio insurance

- "During 1987 the stock market was an area of much excitement but little net
  movement: The Dow advanced 2.3% for the year."
- "Mr. Market was on a manic rampage until October and then experienced a sudden,
  massive seizure."
- Who he blames: "Instead of focusing on what businesses will do in the years
  ahead, many prestigious money managers now focus on what they expect other money
  managers to do in the days ahead. For them, stocks are merely tokens in a game,
  like the thimble and flatiron in Monopoly."
- On portfolio insurance: "This strategy - which is simply an exotically-labeled
  version of the small speculator's stop-loss order dictates that ever increasing
  portions of a stock portfolio, or their index-future equivalents, be sold as
  prices decline."
- Its scale: "According to the Brady Report, $60 billion to $90 billion of equities
  were poised on this hair trigger in mid-October of 1987."

### Ignoring GAAP on purpose

- "Our mental approach to this accounting schizophrenia is to ignore GAAP figures
  and to focus solely on the future earning power of both our controlled and
  non-controlled businesses."
- "It is this business value that we hope to increase at a reasonable (or,
  preferably, unreasonable) rate in the years ahead."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The three permanent holdings

Buffett formalises a category above ordinary holdings — businesses Berkshire does
not intend to sell regardless of price. It is stated as a policy rather than a
forecast: "We do not have in mind any time or price for sale."

### Selection framework

Five categories are named and ranked by nothing but expected return: "(1)
long-term common stock investments, (2) medium-term fixed-income securities, (3)
long-term fixed income securities, (4) short-term cash equivalents, and (5)
short-term arbitrage commitments."

- "We have no particular bias when it comes to choosing from these categories."
- The filter: "limiting ourselves always to investment alternatives we think we
  understand."
- The objective: "Our criteria have nothing to do with maximizing immediately
  reportable earnings; our goal, rather, is to maximize eventual net worth."

### When Berkshire does sell

- "Sometimes, of course, the market may judge a business to be more valuable than
  the underlying facts would indicate it is. In such a case, we will sell our
  holdings."
