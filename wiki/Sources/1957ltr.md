# 1957 Partnership Letter Summary

The second annual letter to the limited partners of the 1956 partnerships — Buffett
at 27, running money out of Omaha two decades before the first Berkshire letter. It
lays down, already fully formed, the machinery he would use for the next thirteen
years: a portfolio split between undervalued "generals" and event-driven
"work-outs", judged against the Dow rather than against zero, and a temperament that
*wants* its stocks to fall while it is buying. This is the [[People/BenGraham]] method
in practice, and the documented root of the "cigar-butt" era later letters describe.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The market is priced above intrinsic value — but that is not the point

Buffett reprints his prior-year warning that blue chips are "priced above intrinsic
value" [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and reports that 1957's moderate decline still
left the public "very bullish." He is explicit that this is context, not strategy:

> "Primary attention is given at all times to the detection of substantially undervalued securities." [[Sources/1957ltr]]

He refuses to forecast the market and says so repeatedly — the discipline is
bottom-up, not macro.

### Generals and work-outs — the two-bucket portfolio

The letter defines the vocabulary that organizes every partnership year. A "general"
is an undervalued security that needs a general price advance to pay off; a
"work-out" is different:

> "A work-out is an investment which is dependent on a specific corporate action for its profit rather than a general advance in the price of the stock as in the case of undervalued situations. Work-outs come about through: sales, mergers, liquidations, tenders, etc." [[Sources/1957ltr]]

Because work-out profits depend on corporate events, not the market, they are the
insulation that makes the portfolio outperform in bad years. The general/work-out
ratio moved from about 70-30 at the end of 1956 to 85-15 as the decline created more
undervalued bargains.

### Beat the Averages by ten points, and only over time

The benchmark discipline is stated as a numerical goal:

> "Over the years, I will be quite satisfied with a performance that is 10% per year better than the Averages" [[Sources/1957ltr]]

He insists relative performance will be *better in falling markets and merely equal
in rising ones*, so a good absolute year and a good relative year are not the same
thing — a distinction he would restate every year through 1969.

### Patience, and the "sterile stage"

Two positions have grown large enough to influence corporate decisions (one at
10–20% of the portfolio, one at ~5%). Accumulating them requires wanting the price
to stay down:

> "Obviously during any acquisition period, our primary interest is to have the stock do nothing or decline rather than advance." [[Sources/1957ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

A portion of the portfolio is therefore always in a "sterile stage" — bought, and
waiting.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Shift toward generals.** The 1957 decline let Buffett tilt the portfolio from a
  70-30 general/work-out mix toward 85-15, buying more undervalued situations.
- **Two accumulating control-type positions**, one at 10–20% of assets (intended to
  reach 20%) and one at ~5%, both expected to take three to five years and to lag in
  a rising market.
- **Results:** against the Dow's loss (499 to 435, roughly 8% including dividends),
  the three 1956 partnerships gained about 6.2%, 7.8% and 25% — the spread Buffett
  attributes to luck in the timing of when each got invested, not skill.
