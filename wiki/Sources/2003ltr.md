# 2003 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 2003: net worth up $13.6 billion, book value per share up 21% — and
28.7% for the S&P, so one of Berkshire's worst relative years, 7.7 points behind.
The letter buys Clayton Homes and McLane, spends its longest section on CEO pay
and what makes a director independent, and records float reaching $44.2 billion
at no cost at all.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A year Berkshire lost to the index, and why the comparison is fading anyway

Book value per share rose 21% against the S&P 500's 28.7% — a relative result of
**minus 7.7 points**, among the weakest in the 39-year table. Buffett does not
dispute the yardstick, but he does explain why it measures less than it used to:

- Equity holdings, including convertible preferreds, "have fallen considerably as
  a percentage of our net worth, from an average of 114% in the 1980s, for
  example, to an average of 50% in 2000-03. Therefore, yearly movements in the
  stock market now affect a much smaller portion of our net worth than was once
  the case."
- And the standard he still accepts: "Unless we achieve gains in per-share
  intrinsic value in the future that outdo the S&P's performance, Charlie and I
  will be adding nothing to what you can accomplish on your own."

Over 39 years book value went from $19 to $50,498, 22.2% compounded, and Buffett
argues intrinsic value grew faster still, because in 1964 the textile business was
worth less than book and by 2003 the company was worth far more.

Then he removes every excuse in advance:

- "If we fail, we will have no excuses. Charlie and I operate in an ideal
  environment."
- Managers are not the risk: "Any shortfall in Berkshire's results will not be
  caused by our managers."
- Nor is the shareholder base, which most CEOs must fight: "At Berkshire, neither
  history nor the demands of owners impede intelligent decision-making. When
  Charlie and I make mistakes, they are – in tennis parlance – unforced errors."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Too much money, not enough to buy

The size problem is stated as an arithmetic constraint on the opportunity set:
"the number of stocks that can be purchased in large enough quantities to move
the performance needle at Berkshire is a small fraction of the number that existed
a decade ago."

The preference when prices allow: "When valuations are similar, we strongly prefer
owning businesses to owning stocks." What a purchase must satisfy is the familiar
three-part test — favourable and enduring economics, talented and honest managers,
a sensible price — and Buffett concedes the cash is outrunning it: acquisitions
have not been "enough to fully employ the gusher of cash that has come our way."

The aside on the asset-management industry is the sharpest line in the section:
"Investment managers often profit far more from piling up assets than from
handling those assets well. So when one tells you that increased funds won't hurt
his investment performance, step back: His nose is about to grow."

He also warns the reader about the table he is about to show, which is unusual:
"growth-rate presentations can be significantly distorted by a calculated
selection of either initial or terminal dates."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

### Clayton Homes, and an industry that broke its own model

The acquisition arrived through 40 finance students from the University of
Tennessee who gave Buffett Jim Clayton's autobiography as a thank-you gift.

The analysis underneath the anecdote is what matters, and it is a case study in
distribution destroying a decent product. Manufactured housing "has accounted for
more than 15% of the homes built in the U.S." for decades, with quality and
variety improving throughout — but:

- "Progress in design and construction was not matched, however, by progress in
  distribution and financing. Instead, as the years went by, the industry's
  business model increasingly centered on the ability of both the retailer and
  manufacturer to unload terrible loans on naive lenders."
- Securitisation made it worse by "further distancing the supplier of funds from
  the lending transaction".
- The result, in the sentence that generalises beyond this industry: "Much of its
  volume a few years back came from buyers who shouldn't have bought, financed by
  lenders who shouldn't have lent."

Buffett had learned this by losing money: he had bought distressed junk debt of
Oakwood Homes without understanding "how atrocious consumer-financing practices
had become throughout most of the manufactured housing industry. But I learned:
Oakwood rather promptly went bankrupt."

Clayton was bought on remarkably little: "an offer for the business based solely
on Jim's book, my evaluation of Kevin, the public financials of Clayton and what I
had learned from the Oakwood experience." The seller's motive was financing —
lenders had fled the industry, and Clayton's earnings depended on securitisation.

The prescription for the industry is a lending standard, not a product change:
"Under a proper model – one requiring significant down payments and shorter-term
loans – the industry will likely remain much smaller than it was in the 90s. But
it will deliver to home buyers an asset in which they will have equity, rather
than disappointment, upon resale."

See [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### McLane — a good business bought in two hours

Wal-Mart's distribution subsidiary, offered through Byron Trott of Goldman Sachs.
Sales of about $23 billion "but operates on paper-thin margins – about 1% pre-tax
– and will swell Berkshire's sales figures far more than our income."

The purchase is Berkshire's acquisition style at its most extreme, and Buffett
tells it as a statement about the seller's integrity rather than his own speed:
"I had a single meeting of about two hours with Tom Schoewe, Wal-Mart's CFO, and
we then shook hands... Twenty-nine days later Wal-Mart had its money. We did no
"due diligence." We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be
– and it was."

The competitive logic is worth keeping: some retailers had shunned McLane while it
belonged to their largest competitor, so the change of owner is itself the growth
opportunity.

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### CEO pay as the test of whether anything has changed

The longest argument in the letter, written a year after Sarbanes-Oxley, and the
claim is that reform can be measured in one place: "In judging whether Corporate
America is serious about reforming itself, CEO pay remains the acid test. To date,
the results aren't encouraging."

The mechanism is an asymmetry of attention, not villainy:

- "When management hires employees, or when companies bargain with a vendor, the
  intensity of interest is equal on both sides of the table. One party's gain is
  the other party's loss, and the money involved has real meaning to both. The
  result is an honest-to-God negotiation."
- Against: "when CEOs (or their representatives) have met with compensation
  committees, too often one side – the CEO's – has cared far more than the other
  about what bargain is struck." A difference between options on 100,000 and
  500,000 shares is monumental to one side and unimportant to the other,
  especially when neither grant hits reported earnings — so "the negotiation often
  has a "play-money" quality."
- How it spread: "Overreaching by CEOs greatly accelerated in the 1990s as
  compensation packages gained by the most avaricious– a title for which there was
  vigorous competition – were promptly replicated elsewhere."
- And who carried it: "The couriers for this epidemic of greed were usually
  consultants and human relations departments, which had no trouble perceiving who
  buttered their bread."

The consultant's own words close it: "There are two classes of clients you don't
want to offend – actual and potential."

### What makes a director independent — ownership, not rules

Buffett's definition sets a much higher bar than the regulatory one:

- "True independence – meaning the willingness to challenge a forceful CEO when
  something is wrong or foolish – is an enormously valuable trait in a director. It
  is also rare."
- Where to look for it: "among high-grade people whose interests are in line with
  those of rank-and-file shareholders – and are in line in a very big way."

At Berkshire, eleven directors each hold — with family — more than $4 million of
stock, six of them at hundreds of millions dating back three decades, and all of it
bought in the market: "we've never passed out options or restricted shares. Charlie
and I love such honest-to-God ownership. After all, who ever washes a rental car?"

The asymmetry is deliberately made worse for them than for shareholders: fees are
nominal, and "we carry no directors and officers liability insurance. Therefore, if
something really catastrophic happens on our directors' watch, they are exposed to
losses that will far exceed yours."

- The summary: "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big. Our approach might
  be called owner-capitalism."
- And, characteristically, the counter-example against himself: "This structure
  does not guarantee perfect behavior, however: I've sat on boards of companies in
  which Berkshire had huge stakes and remained silent as questionable proposals
  were rubber-stamped."

On what a director needs besides independence: business savvy, a shareholder
orientation and genuine interest — and "The rarest of these qualities is business
savvy – and if it is lacking, the other two are of little help."

See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### Float at a record, and free

- Definition, restated: float is "money that doesn't belong to us but that we
  temporarily hold", arising because premiums come first and losses are settled
  later — sometimes much later, since "it sometimes takes years for losses to be
  reported (think asbestos), negotiated and settled."
- The condition on its value: "Float is wonderful – if it doesn't come at a high
  price. The cost of float is determined by underwriting results".
- The industry's problem: it "as a whole regularly operates at a substantial
  underwriting loss, and therefore often has a cost of float that is unattractive."

Berkshire's record is given with its failures included — five terrible years in
which float cost more than 10% — against 18 of 37 years at an underwriting profit,
"meaning we were actually paid for holding money."

Float reached $44,220 million at year end, from $20 million in 1967, and 2003 cost
nothing: "Float reached record levels and it came without cost as all major
segments contributed to Berkshire's $1.7 billion pre-tax underwriting profit."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### Two unwindings, one profitable and one not

MidAmerican gets an accounting complaint: an 80.5% fully diluted interest reported
as a single line because PUHCA caps Berkshire's vote at 9.9%. "Instead of our fully
including its assets, liabilities, revenues and expenses in our statements, we
record only a one-line entry in both our balance sheet and income account."

Gen Re Securities is the opposite of a good business bought well — a derivatives
book inherited with General Reinsurance and being liquidated since early 2002. It
had 23,218 outstanding tickets with 884 counterparties, "some having names I
couldn't pronounce, much less creditworthiness I could evaluate", and two years of
skilled unwinding had reduced that to 7,580.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Clayton Homes — acquired

The class act of the manufactured housing industry, bought after Buffett read the
founder's autobiography and spoke to Kevin Clayton, the CEO. Clayton subsequently
agreed to buy the assets of Oakwood Homes, whose junk debt Berkshire had bought at
a deep discount and which "will probably return a small profit to us."

### McLane — acquired from Wal-Mart

About $23 billion of sales at roughly 1% pre-tax margins. Run by Grady Rosier.
Closed 29 days after a two-hour meeting, with no due diligence performed.

### The equity portfolio — $8,515 million at cost, $35,287 million at market

Positions above $500 million of market value:

| Company | % owned | Cost | Market |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Coca-Cola Company | 8.2 | $1,299m | $10,150m |
| American Express | 11.8 | $1,470m | $7,312m |
| The Gillette Company | 9.5 | $600m | $3,526m |
| Wells Fargo | 3.3 | $463m | $3,324m |
| Moody's Corporation | 16.1 | $499m | $1,453m |
| The Washington Post | 18.1 | $11m | $1,367m |
| PetroChina | 1.3 | $488m | $1,340m |
| H&R Block | 8.2 | $227m | $809m |
| HCA | 3.1 | $492m | $665m |
| M&T Bank | 5.6 | $103m | $659m |

Only Wells Fargo was added during the year. Buffett notes when the others last
moved: Coca-Cola 1994, American Express 1998, Gillette 1989, Washington Post 1973,
Moody's 2000 — "Brokers don't love us."

### The admission about the bubble

The clearest mistake report in the letter, and it concerns inaction rather than
action: "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during
The Great Bubble. If these stocks are fully priced now, you may wonder what I was
thinking four years ago when their intrinsic value was lower and their prices far
higher. So do I."

The current stance is neither bullish nor bearish: "We own pieces of excellent
businesses – all of which had good gains in intrinsic value last year – but their
current prices reflect their excellence."

### Junk bonds — about $8 billion bought in 2002, and the window closed

"In 2002, junk bonds became very cheap, and we purchased about $8 billion of
these. The pendulum swung quickly though, and this sector now looks decidedly
unattractive to us. Yesterday's weeds are today being priced as flowers."

### MidAmerican Energy Holdings — 80.5% fully diluted, 9.9% of the vote

Yorkshire Electricity and Northern Electric (3.7 million UK customers), MidAmerican
Energy (689,000 Iowa customers), and the Kern River and Northern Natural pipelines,
carrying 7.8% of US natural gas. Berkshire held $1.578 billion of MidAmerican's
most junior debt at 11%. Partners: Dave Sokol, Greg Abel and Walter Scott, who
holds the controlling vote.
