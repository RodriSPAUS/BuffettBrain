# 2019 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share market value grew 11.0% in 2019 against the S&P 500's 31.5% total return (book value per share, retired as a metric the year before, no longer appears in the performance table at all). GAAP net income was $81.4 billion, but Buffett again tells shareholders to discount most of it — $53.7 billion came from the same mark-to-market unrealized-gains rule that produced a $20.6 billion loss the year before, a roughly 1,900% year-over-year swing in reported GAAP earnings that says nothing about the underlying business. The letter's major essay, on the power of retained earnings, revisits Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book on compounding, and a long section on corporate board governance — drawing on Buffett's 62 years serving as director of 21 public companies — is new to the letter's format. Buffett also lays out, in detail, his estate plan for his Berkshire shares.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### GAAP Earnings Swing 1,900% on Paper Alone
2018's unrealized-gains rule produced a $20.6 billion loss that year and a $53.7 billion gain in 2019, pushing reported GAAP earnings from $4 billion to $81.4 billion even though Berkshire's equity holdings "grew steadily and substantially" across both years in economic terms. Buffett and Munger's advice is unchanged: focus on operating earnings (little changed in 2019) and ignore realized or unrealized investment gains/losses entirely.

### The Power of Retained Earnings
Buffett revisits Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book *Common Stocks as Long Term Investments*, which set out to argue stocks beat bonds in inflation and lose to them in deflation, and instead discovered the opposite — a finding Smith opened his book by confessing:

> "These studies are the record of a failure – the failure of facts to sustain a preconceived theory." [[Sources/2019ltr]]

Buffett quotes John Maynard Keynes's review identifying Smith's real insight — that well-managed companies retaining and reinvesting a portion of earnings compound value beyond what dividends alone show:

> "I have kept until last what is perhaps Mr. Smith's most important, and is certainly his most novel, point." [[Sources/2019ltr]]

Applying the lesson to Berkshire's own ten largest marketable-stock holdings, Buffett tabulates dividends received versus Berkshire's share of retained earnings for 2019 (in millions): American Express ($261 dividends / $998 retained), Apple ($773/$2,519), Bank of America ($682/$2,167), Bank of New York Mellon ($101/$288), Coca-Cola ($640/$194), Delta Air Lines ($114/$416), JPMorgan Chase ($216/$476), Moody's ($55/$137), U.S. Bancorp ($251/$407), and Wells Fargo ($705/$730) — a combined $3,798M of reported dividends against $8,332M of unreported retained earnings working on Berkshire's behalf.

### Non-Insurance Operations and the Lubrizol Fire
Non-insurance net income rose 3% to $17.7 billion. BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (91% share) earned a combined $8.3 billion, up 6%. Quoting Berkshire director Tom Murphy's advice on acquisitions — that reputation as a good manager mainly requires buying good businesses in the first place — Buffett reflects candidly on his uneven acquisition record using a marriage analogy, noting that "it is usually the buyer who encounters unpleasant surprises." [[Sources/2019ltr]] He also recounts a September 2019 fire at a French plant owned by Lubrizol (Berkshire-owned since 2011), with the twist that one of Lubrizol's largest insurers turned out to be another Berkshire company:

> Referencing Matthew 6:3 — "Let not the left hand know what the right hand doeth" — Buffett notes wryly that he has clearly behaved as ordered. [[Sources/2019ltr]]

### Insurance: Float Reaches $129 Billion, and a Surprise in Wilkes-Barre
Float grew to $129.423 billion at year-end 2019:

| Year | Float ($M) |
| --- | --- |
| 1970 | $39 |
| 1980 | 237 |
| 1990 | 1,632 |
| 2000 | 27,871 |
| 2010 | 65,832 |
| 2018 | 122,732 |
| 2019 | 129,423 |

Berkshire posted an underwriting profit in 16 of the last 17 years (2017's hurricane year the exception), with $27.5 billion cumulative pre-tax gain, $400 million of it in 2019. The now-familiar formulation returns: "At Berkshire it is a religion, Old Testament style." [[Sources/2019ltr]] Buffett highlights a small 2012 deal as an example of finding value in unlikely places: Ajit Jain's purchase of GUARD Insurance Group in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania for $221 million, led by CEO Sy Foguel — by 2019, GUARD's premium volume had grown 379% to $1.9 billion and its float 265%, all while delivering underwriting profit.

### Berkshire Hathaway Energy at 20 Years
Marking BHE's 20th year under Berkshire ownership, Buffett highlights that Iowa residential rates have risen less than 1% annually since the 2000 purchase (versus 61%-higher rates, soon to be 70%, at the state's other major investor-owned utility), driven by BHE's wind buildout — expected to reach full wind self-sufficiency for its Iowa retail load by 2021. BHE has never paid Berkshire a dividend, instead retaining $28 billion of earnings since acquisition, an outlier among utilities that typically pay out large dividend percentages.

### Investments: Occidental Petroleum Preferred Debuts
The Kraft Heinz stake (325,442,152 shares, carried at $13.8 billion GAAP, market value only $10.5 billion at year-end) remained excluded from the ranked table. The "Others" line for the first time included a new $10 billion investment in Occidental Petroleum preferred stock and warrants. Top holdings (cost/market, in millions) included Apple ($35,287M/$73,667M, 5.7%), Bank of America ($12,560M/$33,380M, 10.7%), American Express ($1,287M/$18,874M, 18.7%), Coca-Cola ($1,299M/$22,140M, 9.3%), Wells Fargo ($7,040M/$18,598M, 8.4%), and a new small stake in Visa, for total equity investments of $110,340M cost / $248,027M market.

### The Road Ahead: Buffett's Estate Plan
Buffett states his will directs executors and successor trustees never to sell any of his Berkshire shares, instead converting a portion of his A shares to B shares annually for distribution to philanthropic foundations, a process he estimates will take 12 to 15 years to complete after his death. He notes he holds 99% of his net worth in Berkshire stock and has never sold shares outside charitable giving, with one historical exception — a 1980 exchange tied to a bank-holding-company law change. He frames Berkshire's readiness for succession around five factors: diversified high-return businesses, the advantages of the single-entity structure, financial strength built to withstand shocks, devoted top managers, and a board focused on preserving Berkshire's culture.

### Boards of Directors: A Governance Essay
Drawing on 62 years of service on 21 public boards, Buffett criticizes several trends in modern corporate governance: escalating director compensation that quietly compromises "independence" more than ownership stakes do; the tendency for boards to favor compliant "cocker spaniel" directors over challenging ones; the rarity of CEOs bringing in a genuine critic to argue against a proposed acquisition; and generous age limits functioning as the real mechanism for removing underperforming directors. He notes this year marks the 100th anniversary of the 19th Amendment while women remain underrepresented in boardrooms relative to their long-guaranteed voting rights.

### Short Subjects: Repurchases and Taxes
Berkshire repurchased about 1% of the company (~$5 billion) in 2019 under its policy of buying only when shares trade below intrinsic value with ample cash remaining afterward. Berkshire paid $3.6 billion in U.S. federal income tax in 2019, about 1.5% of the $243 billion the U.S. Treasury collected from all corporate income tax that year — a contrast Buffett notes against Berkshire's founding-era history of paying no federal tax at all, following a decade of net losses in the business before 1965.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Per-share market value +11.0% (book value no longer reported)
- **GAAP earnings**: $81.4 billion, dominated by a $53.7 billion mark-to-market swing from the unrealized-gains accounting rule
- **New equity positions**: $10 billion Occidental Petroleum preferred stock and warrants; new small position in Visa
- **Insurance**: Underwriting profit in 16 of the last 17 years; float grew to $129.423 billion
- **Share repurchases**: About 1% of shares outstanding (~$5 billion) bought back in 2019
- **Succession**: Estate plan detailed — no Berkshire shares sold, gradual A-to-B conversion and foundation distribution over 12–15 years after Buffett's death
- **Taxes**: Berkshire paid $3.6 billion in federal income tax, about 1.5% of all corporate income tax collected in the U.S.
