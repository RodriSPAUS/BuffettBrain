# 2019 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

The year Kraft Heinz's write-down forces Buffett to publicly grade his own mistake ("we paid too much for Kraft") and, prompted by two separate shareholders, Munger to say flatly "we screwed up" on Google. Buybacks dominate the opening questions, with Buffett laying out the same-moment-before-and-after intrinsic-value test he would repeat for years. Wells Fargo is graded against Salomon's 1991 bond-trading scandal ("he heard about a pyromaniac and he let him even keep the box of matches"). Greg Abel and Ajit Jain, both present, field questions directly for the first time in this corpus — Abel on NV Energy customer defections, Jain on how he prices unconventional catastrophe risk — while Buffett is unusually candid that "those guys are better than we are." The $10 billion Occidental Petroleum preferred-stock commitment for the Anadarko acquisition, put together over a single weekend, is discussed as a template for what only Berkshire's balance sheet and speed can do. The formal meeting re-elects all 14 directors with no shareholder proposals on the ballot.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Share Repurchases: The Same-Moment-Before-and-After Test
Answering a question about why Berkshire bought so little stock during a four-month period when it traded under $207, Buffett lays out the rule he applies to every repurchase, illustrated with a three-partner analogy:

> "We formulate -- we used to have a policy of tying it to book value there, but that became really obsoleted, that did not -- the real thing is to buy stock, repurchase shares only when you think you're doing it and at a price where the remaining shareholders have had -- are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He estimates intrinsic value as a range with roughly a 10% band, insists disclosure to selling shareholders has to match what he and Munger themselves know, and separately tells Jay Gelb he floated the $100 billion buyback figure to the Financial Times off the cuff, "in about 3 seconds." Munger predicts Berkshire will "get a little more liberal in repurchasing shares." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

### Precision Scheduled Railroading, and BNSF's Margin Gap to Union Pacific
Asked why BNSF's operating ratio trails Union Pacific's by more than 400 basis points despite similar scale, Buffett credits the four other North American railroads that adopted Hunter Harrison's precision-scheduled model (Canadian National, Canadian Pacific, CSX) with dramatically improved margins, while still weighing customer-service tradeoffs:

> "...we are not above copping anything that is successful." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

See also [[Cases/BNSF]].

### Wells Fargo Graded Against Salomon
Asked why Buffett has stayed quiet on Wells Fargo compared to his public role in the 1991 Salomon crisis, he distinguishes the incentive failure from the failure to act once it surfaced, drawing the parallel explicitly to Salomon CEO John Gutfreund:

> "Because essentially, he heard about a pyromaniac and he let him even keep the box of matches." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He notes the FDIC "has not cost the United States government a penny" since 1934 — bank shareholders, not taxpayers, have absorbed every loss the fund ever paid — and that Wells's shareholders, not its executives, bore the real cost. Munger defends departed CEO Tim Sloan as "an accidental casualty that didn't deserve the trouble." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

### Politics, Socialism, and "A Card-Carrying Capitalist"
Asked how his own Democratic politics square with fiduciary duty amid rising socialism-versus-capitalism rhetoric among 2020 candidates, Buffett separates personal political speech from Berkshire's institutional voice:

> "On your question about the -- I will just say I'm a card-carrying capitalist." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He states Berkshire has never in 54 years contributed to a presidential candidate, and ties the Berkshire-Amazon-JPMorgan healthcare venture (Haven, following on from the 2018 "tapeworm" framing) to the private sector needing to solve what the public sector has not.

### Kraft Heinz: "We Paid Too Much for Kraft"
Responding to a question about whether large consumer brands still have a moat against retailer house brands and Amazon, Buffett states the mistake plainly rather than defending the deal:

> "We paid too much money for Kraft." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He distinguishes this from the Heinz side of the 2015 merger, priced appropriately, and argues the underlying $6 billion-pretax-on-$7 billion-of-tangible-assets business remains wonderful — "you can turn any investment into a bad deal by paying too much" — while conceding retailer private-label brands like Costco's Kirkland ($39 billion in sales through roughly 775 stores, more than Coca-Cola) have gained real power relative to manufacturer brands. [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

### Missing Google and Amazon
Two separate shareholder questions probe the Google and Amazon misses. On Amazon, Buffett calls the fact that Berkshire's own managers were already watching Google's advertising economics at GEICO "not the whole story," crediting Jeff Bezos's execution as extraordinary. On Google specifically, Munger is blunt:

> "We screwed up." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

> "But I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google better." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Buffett recounts that GEICO itself was paying Google per click with zero marginal cost to Google and "we just sat there sucking our thumbs" rather than connecting the observation to an investment.

### "All Investing Is Value Investing"
Asked whether buying Amazon and StoneCo signals a philosophical shift away from value investing, Buffett rejects the premise, tracing every purchase — bank stocks at a discount to book value or Amazon at a high reported multiple — back to the same calculation:

> "In the end, it all goes back to Aesop, who in 600 BC said that a bird in the hand is worth 2 in the bush." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

The distinction, he says, is only how many birds are in the bush, how certain that is, and how long it will take to get there — never whether the calculation itself changes.

### Float, and Ajit Jain's "Acid Test"
Buffett describes Berkshire's $124 billion insurance float as money "given to us for nothing," arguing a fully-reserved-for-worst-case standalone insurer is a bad business, while Berkshire's balance sheet lets it hold that risk and invest the float productively — a structural advantage he says "it'd be very, very, very hard for anybody" to replicate. Asked how he safely prices unconventional catastrophe risk, Ajit Jain (answering directly for the first time in this corpus) describes the process and its final step:

> "And then finally, the absolute acid test is, I pick up the phone and call Warren." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Buffett estimates Jain has personally added more than $50 billion to Berkshire's balance sheet since walking into his office uninvited in the mid-1980s, and calls the skill "not very teachable." See also [[People/AjitJain]], [[Concepts/Float]].

### Succession Made Visible: "Those Guys Are Better Than We Are"
Asked whether Greg Abel and Ajit Jain might join the stage at future meetings so shareholders can question them directly, Buffett says the idea has already been discussed and both men are in the room that day, fielding questions live for the first time:

> "Those guys are better than we are." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He explicitly rules out Ted Weschler and Todd Combs answering investment-specific questions, calling Berkshire's holdings proprietary information the company owes no obligation to disclose beyond required filings. See also [[Synthesis/Succession]], [[People/GregAbel]].

### NV Energy: Customer Defections and the Case for Rate Stability
A detailed question about Nevada casinos leaving NV Energy's system despite exit fees draws a direct answer from Greg Abel: five customers have left in roughly five years, the utility recovers substantial exit fees, and the underlying driver is customers no longer wanting to bear the cost of the state's renewable-energy mandate after their contractual obligation period ends. Abel contrasts this with Iowa, where rates have not risen since Berkshire acquired the utility in 1999 and are not expected to rise again before 2030 or 2031, and reports Nevada's coal fleet will be fully retired within a year of the meeting. [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]] See also [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Leverage and Alternative Investments: A Warning, Not an Endorsement
Asked about private-equity and infrastructure returns for a public pension fund, Buffett acknowledges leveraged returns can beat unleveraged ones in an up market with weak debt covenants, but warns that even the smartest, most experienced operators can be destroyed by it, citing Long-Term Capital Management by name:

> "And I mean really, really smart people working with their own money and with years and years of experience of what they were doing and it all turned to pumpkins and mice in 1998." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Munger is sharper about fee-driven fund managers who report committed-but-uninvested cash as if it were earning a return: "While all they're are doing is lying a little bit to make the money come in." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

### American Express: "We Own the World"
Discussing Amex's competitive position against Apple Card and other new entrants, Buffett recounts the company's 1850 founding by Wells and Fargo, its brush with irrelevance after traveler's checks declined, and its transformation into a premium-card franchise with industry-leading renewal rates. Munger's assessment:

> "I think we own the world as long as the technology stays the same." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Buffett notes Berkshire's share of Amex's annual earnings will soon equal the full cost of the position, with the share count still shrinking through buybacks. See also [[Cases/AmericanExpress]].

### Occidental Petroleum: A $10 Billion Weekend
Explaining the $10 billion preferred-stock commitment financing Occidental's acquisition of Anadarko, Buffett frames Berkshire's speed and balance sheet as a standing offer no institution can match:

> "But if there are any $10 billion or $20 billion or maybe even $50 billion 2-day transactions that are in the world, well, let me know, think of Berkshire Hathaway, for sure, in terms of what number to call." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

The deal, agreed by phone on a Friday and signed by Sunday with no material-adverse-change clause, is presented as evidence Berkshire will do more transactions like it, "not identical," possibly larger. See also [[Cases/OccidentalPetroleum]].

### No Formulas for Risk, and a Story About Fabricated Earnings
Asked how Berkshire's risk-evaluation process differs from spreadsheet-driven institutional deal committees, Buffett states flatly:

> "It's very disappointing, we have no formulas around Berkshire." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He illustrates with an unnamed seller's investment bankers who proposed adding $10 million of stock-option compensation back into reported earnings as if it were free: "But by -- essentially by sort of lying about our accounting, we could add $10 billion in earning, and they wanted us to pay them because they couldn't do it and we could do it." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]] A separate private-equity firm accidentally copied Berkshire on internal instructions telling a manager to inflate projections by 15% "to offset this conservatism" it expected from Buffett.

### Expanding the Circle of Competence, Cautiously
Asked whether investors should actively widen their circle of competence as the world changes faster, Buffett agrees in principle but Munger sets the limit:

> "Where I can't feel that, ought to be pretty cautious." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Buffett says he has expanded his own circle "a little bit" in energy, working alongside Walter Scott and Greg Abel, but warns against forcing expertise: "you can't force it." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]] See also [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### See's Candies: Why It Never Became Mars or Hershey's
Asked why See's has not scaled to national or global size despite decades of strong returns, Buffett explains regional taste differences (milk versus dark chocolate, chunks versus miniatures) have defeated a dozen expansion attempts, and that boxed chocolate is overwhelmingly a gift product people rarely buy to eat themselves. Munger's summary:

> "Well, we failed in turning our old candy company into Mars or HERSHEY'S for the same reason that you failed to get the Nobel Prize in physics and achieve immortality." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

Buffett notes the original $25 million purchase has generated over $2 billion of pretax income used to fund other acquisitions — proof, he says, against any one-size-fits-all capital-allocation formula. See also [[Cases/SeeCandies]].

### Sixty Years, No Argument
Asked how Buffett and Munger resolve conflicts between themselves, Buffett states there has essentially never been one to resolve:

> "Charlie and I, literally, and people find this hard to believe, but in 60 years, we never had an argument." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

He credits deference on subjects where one partner has spent more time than the other, and neither ever second-guessing the other's calls after the fact. Munger adds that succession of the partnership itself, not just the business, "is going to work fine." [[Sources/Conference_call_2019]]

### The Formal Meeting: All 14 Directors Re-Elected, No Shareholder Proposals
The meeting convened with 724,765 Class A and 1,371,697,551 Class B shares outstanding. All 14 director nominees — Buffett, Munger, Abel, Howard Buffett, Burke, Decker, Gates, Gottesman, Guyman, Jain, Murphy, Olson, Scott and Witmer — were elected with at least 543,703 votes each. Unlike 2018, no shareholder proposals appeared on the ballot.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Share repurchases**: over $1 billion in Q1 2019, applying an intrinsic-value-band test rather than a fixed spending target; Buffett floats a possible future $100 billion figure
- **Occidental Petroleum**: $10 billion in 8% preferred stock plus warrants, committed over a single weekend to help finance Occidental's acquisition of Anadarko
- **Kraft Heinz**: explicitly named as overpaid on the Kraft side of the merger, though operating performance has improved under current management
- **Amazon**: a position initiated by one of Ted Weschler or Todd Combs, disclosed as pending in the next 13F filing
- **Apple**: Berkshire's largest holding; company-level buybacks increasing Berkshire's proportional ownership without Berkshire spending a dime
- **American Express**: Berkshire's share of annual earnings soon to equal the full cost basis of the position
- **10% ownership threshold**: Berkshire continues trimming bank holdings (including Wells Fargo) to stay under the regulatory disclosure and short-swing-profit threshold
- **Formal meeting**: all 14 director nominees re-elected; no shareholder proposals on the ballot
