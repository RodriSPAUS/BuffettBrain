# 2005 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 6.4% against the S&P 500's 4.9% — a "decent" year by
Buffett's own word, delivered despite Hurricane Katrina, which cost Berkshire an
estimated $2.5 billion and, with Rita and Wilma, $3.4 billion in total — the
worst hurricane season in insurance-industry history. Float was nonetheless
costless, on the strength of GEICO's underwriting. The letter's structural
argument is that Berkshire's own reporting has grown too complex for
consolidated numbers to mean much, so results are grouped into four sectors from
here on; its two set-piece essays are "widening the moat" as a daily, almost
invisible act, and How to Minimize Investment Returns, the Gotrocks family
parable about frictional costs. Four acquisitions closed or were agreed —
Medical Protective, Forest River, Business Wire, Applied Underwriters — plus the
much larger PacifiCorp purchase through MidAmerican, still awaiting regulatory
approval at yearend.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Four businesses joined by fax, letter and phone call

None of the year's deals involved issuing Berkshire stock — a point Buffett
underscores because a stock-funded acquisition quietly sells off a slice of
everything the acquirer already owns. He states his own record on this bluntly:
"I've made this kind of deal a few times myself – and, on balance, my actions
have cost you money."

**Medical Protective (MedPro)**, a 106-year-old medical-malpractice insurer
bought June 30, arrived with the underwriting-discipline culture shared by all
Berkshire insurers and the financial strength to reassure doctors that a
long-tail claim will not boomerang back onto them if the insurer fails.

**Forest River**, closed August 31, is the letter's best acquisition story — the
full case is built out on its own page, see [[Cases/ForestRiver]]. Pete Liegl
sent a two-page fax on June 21 explaining why his $1.6 billion recreational-
vehicle manufacturer fit Berkshire's six criteria; Buffett asked for more
figures the next morning, made an offer that afternoon, and shook hands on June
28.

**Business Wire** reached Berkshire the same way — unsolicited, from the
target's own president after she read a Wall Street Journal article about
Berkshire's acquisition style:

> "As president of Business Wire, I'd like to introduce you to my company, as I
> believe it fits the profile of Berkshire Hathaway subsidiary companies as
> detailed in a recent Wall Street Journal article." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Cathy Baron Tamraz's letter closed with the line Buffett says sealed it — "We
run a tight ship and keep unnecessary spending under wraps. No secretaries or
management layers here." Founder Lorry Lokey, 78, had built the company from
scratch in 1961 into a service disseminating information in 150 countries for
25,000 clients.

**Applied Underwriters**, 81% acquired in December, combines payroll services
with workers'-compensation insurance for small businesses, mostly in
California. It had already entered a large reinsurance contract with Ajit Jain
before the acquisition talks began, and its founders Sid Ferenc and Steve
Menzies retain 19% of the company.

**PacifiCorp**, agreed the prior spring through 80.5%-owned MidAmerican Energy,
serves six Western states; Berkshire's share requires buying $3.4 billion of
MidAmerican common stock, with MidAmerican borrowing $1.7 billion more to close
the gap. The letter restates the entrance strategy plainly: "Unlike many
business buyers, Berkshire has no 'exit strategy.' We buy to keep."

### Hurricane Katrina, and the mega-cat wager

Berkshire absorbed $2.5 billion from Katrina and a further $0.9 billion from
Rita and Wilma combined — $3.4 billion in hurricane losses for the year, against
a 2004 in which float had cost less than nothing. Yet 2005's float was still
costless, entirely on the strength of GEICO. Buffett frames the underlying
uncertainty about storm frequency as a decision under total ignorance, reaching
for Pascal's wager as the only honest framework:

> "Joe, Ajit and I don't know the answer to these all-important questions. What
> we do know is that our ignorance means we must follow the course prescribed by
> Pascal in his famous wager about the existence of God. As you may recall, he
> concluded that since he didn't know the answer, his personal gain/loss ratio
> dictated an affirmative conclusion." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

The practical consequence: mega-cat policies would now be written only at much
higher prices, and only with aggregate exposure Berkshire could absorb without
distress if storms worsen — while retaining, at the right price, "both the
ability and the appetite to be the largest writer of mega-cat coverage in the
world." See [[Concepts/Float]].

Float rose to $49 billion — "the $20 million of float that came with our 1967
entry into insurance has now increased ... to $49 billion" — carried at a total
underwriting profit of only $53 million because General Re and National
Indemnity's reinsurance both swung to losses under the hurricanes, offset almost
entirely by GEICO's swing the other way. See [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

The smaller primary insurers, MedPro included, are flagged as understated in
the table: Berkshire added about $125 million to MedPro's loss reserves
immediately after acquiring it, a conservative first move Buffett explains with
an old insurance rule about asymmetric surprise:

> "One thing, though, we have learned – the hard way – after many years in the
> business: Surprises in insurance are far from symmetrical. You are lucky if you
> get one that is pleasant for every ten that go the other way."
> — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

### GEICO: the productivity story

> "Credit GEICO – and its brilliant CEO, Tony Nicely – for our stellar insurance
> results in a disaster-ridden year. One statistic stands out: In just two years,
> GEICO improved its productivity by 32%. Remarkably, employment fell by 4% even
> as policy count grew by 26% – and more gains are in store."
> — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Auto policies in force grew 12.1%, lifting market share from about 5.6% to
6.1% — each point worth $1.6 billion in premium. Advertising spend rose from $31
million in 1996, when Berkshire took control, to $502 million in 2005, and
Buffett records the highest internet/phone conversion rate in company history as
evidence the price advantage is real, not just louder marketing. New Jersey, off
limits since Jack Byrne's 1976 withdrawal, is by now generating the highest
retention rate of any state GEICO writes in. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### Widening the moat

The letter's clearest statement of the phrase, framed as something that happens
in small increments rather than in a single competitive event:

> "When our long-term competitive position improves as a result of these
> almost unnoticeable actions, we describe the phenomenon as 'widening the
> moat.' And doing that is essential if we are to have the kind of business we
> want a decade or two from now." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

The trade-off with short-term earnings is explicit, and irreversible once lost:

> "If a management makes bad decisions in order to hit short-term earnings
> targets, and consequently gets behind the eight-ball in terms of costs,
> customer satisfaction or brand strength, no amount of subsequent brilliance
> will overcome the damage that has been inflicted." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Buffett cites the auto and airline industries as the visible casualties of
decades of moat-narrowing decisions their current managements did not make but
must live with. See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]].

### Gillette, Kilts, and what Duracell actually cost

The P&G-Gillette merger forced a $5.0 billion pre-tax bookkeeping gain that
Buffett tells shareholders to ignore entirely, since Berkshire neither intended
to sell Gillette before the merger nor intends to sell P&G now, and no tax was
triggered. The substantive point is about the CEO who preceded the merger:

> "It's hard to overemphasize the importance of who is CEO of a company. Before
> Jim Kilts arrived at Gillette in 2001, the company was struggling, having
> particularly suffered from capital-allocation blunders. In the major example,
> Gillette's acquisition of Duracell cost Gillette shareholders billions of
> dollars, a loss never made visible by conventional accounting."
> — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Buffett's verdict on Kilts's own pay: "it's difficult to overpay the truly
extraordinary CEO of a giant enterprise. But this species is rare." See
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### Fred Futile: how fixed-price options pay CEOs for standing still

The letter's sharpest compensation critique is a worked numerical example. A
CEO holding a ten-year fixed-price option on 1% of a company that earns a flat
$1 billion a year on $10 billion of net worth — no growth at all — can still
make $158 million simply by skipping dividends and using all earnings to
repurchase stock, because share count falls and per-share earnings rise even
though the business itself has not improved:

> "Simply by withholding earnings from owners, Fred gets very rich, making a
> cool $158 million, despite the business itself improving not at all.
> Astonishingly, Fred could have made more than $100 million if Stagnant's
> earnings had declined by 20% during the ten-year period." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

The structural flaw: fixed-price options never reflect the automatic build-up
in value that comes from retaining earnings, and CEOs who privately understand
that capital carries a cost never mention to shareholders that fixed-price
options give them capital for free. Getting fired can pay even better than
performing: "Today, in the executive suite, the all-too-prevalent rule is that
nothing succeeds like failure." Compensation committees are indicted for
following comparative pay surveys that ratchet every year's excess into the
next year's baseline — the corporate version of "But, Mom, all the other kids
have one." Buffett's counter-example is Hank Greenberg's reply when told a
prospective client had hit .246 the year before: "Ask for a uniform." See
[[Applications/RedFlags]].

### How to Minimize Investment Returns: the Gotrocks parable

Buffett's fullest statement of frictional cost as the enemy of the ordinary
investor. He starts from a closed system — all of corporate America owned by
one family, the Gotrocks, who grow richer exactly as fast as their companies
earn, "generation after generation," until Helpers arrive:

> "With unimportant exceptions, such as bankruptcies in which some of a
> company's losses are borne by creditors, the most that owners in aggregate can
> earn between now and Judgment Day is what their businesses in aggregate earn."
> — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Four waves of Helpers are introduced in sequence — brokers who profit from
trading activity, managers hired to out-trade the family, consultants hired to
pick the managers, and finally "hyper-Helpers" wearing "sewn-on sexy names like
HEDGE FUND or PRIVATE EQUITY" who promise magical powers "similar to those
acquired by mild-mannered Clark Kent when he changed into his Superman
costume." Each layer subtracts from the family's share of what the businesses
actually earn:

> "A record portion of the earnings that would go in their entirety to owners –
> if they all just stayed in their rocking chairs – is now going to a swelling
> army of Helpers." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

He estimates the aggregate frictional toll at perhaps 20% of American business
earnings:

> "The burden of paying Helpers may cause American equity investors, overall,
> to earn only 80% or so of what they would earn if they just sat still and
> listened to no one." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

He then proposes, in Isaac Newton's honour and at his expense (Newton lost a
fortune in the South Sea Bubble), a Fourth Law of Motion: for investors as a
whole, returns decrease as motion increases.

### NetJets: "I was dead wrong," again

> "Operating results at NetJets were a different story. I said last year that
> this business would earn money in 2005 – and I was dead wrong."
> — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

European contracts grew 37% with a narrowing loss, but the U.S. operation
"dipped far into the red" on falling efficiency and rising costs, a problem
Buffett attributes to Rich Santulli to solve without compromising the safety
and service standard that led Buffett to use, and then buy, the company in the
first place.

### The derivatives wind-down: "cost me a lot of money"

Gen Re Securities lost a further $104 million pre-tax reducing its book from
2,890 to 741 contracts, bringing cumulative losses on the exit to $404 million.
Buffett names the mistake as his own, not the trading desk's:

> "The hard fact is that I have cost you a lot of money by not moving
> immediately to close down..." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

> "Both Charlie and I knew at the time of the Gen Re purchase that it was a
> problem and told its management that we wanted to exit the business. It was
> my responsibility to make sure that happened. Rather than address the
> situation head on, however, I wasted several years while we attempted to
> sell the operation." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

> "Fault me for dithering. (Charlie calls it thumb-sucking.) When a problem
> exists, whether in personnel or in business operations, the time to act is
> now." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

One liquidated contract had run for 100 years, which Buffett uses to show how a
long-dated, multi-variable derivative exists mainly to serve "the need of a
compensation-conscious trader to have a long-dated contract on his books" — the
same marking-to-model danger the corpus first names in [[Sources/2002ltr]]. He
frames Berkshire's own experience as unusually favourable — a relatively minor
book, liquidated in a benign market, free of forced-selling pressure — and
therefore a warning rather than reassurance for larger, more troubled books
elsewhere: "This is a scenario to which much attention should be given now
rather than after the fact. The time to have considered – and improved – the
reliability of New Orleans' levees was before Katrina."

### PUHCA's repeal and the MidAmerican structure

The Public Utility Holding Company Act, which had capped Berkshire's
MidAmerican vote at 9.9% regardless of its 80.5% economic interest, was
repealed August 8, 2005 — allowing Berkshire to convert its preferred stock into
voting common on February 9, 2006 and lifting its votes to 83.4%, with all
future Berkshire financials consolidating MidAmerican. The underlying
partnership, Buffett stresses, is unaffected by who holds how many votes:

> "It's unimportant how many votes each party has; we will make major moves
> only when we are unanimous in thinking them wise." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

MidAmerican's HomeServices brokerage handled $64 billion of transactions, up
6.5% from 2004, through 19,200 agents in 18 locally-branded firms. See
[[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Debt: the only three doors Berkshire will use

Buffett restates a standing policy prompted by MidAmerican's consolidation
inflating the reported balance sheet: Berkshire itself shuns debt except for
repos on the most liquid government securities, borrowing against
interest-bearing receivables whose risk is understood (Clayton's manufactured-
housing portfolio, financed at $9.3 billion of interest-bearing liabilities by
yearend), and MidAmerican's own non-guaranteed utility debt, serviced by
diversified and uncorrelated regulated earnings. The reasoning for staying
debt-averse even at a cost of return is about promises already made:

> "There are people who have been permanently injured to whom we owe insurance
> payments that stretch out for fifty years or more. To these and other
> constituencies we have promised total security, whatever comes: financial
> panics, stock-exchange closures (an extended one occurred in 1914) or even
> domestic nuclear, chemical or biological attacks." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

> "A long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always
> equals zero." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

### Management succession, addressed directly

Buffett answers, in order, what happens if he dies tonight and what happens if
he merely fades. On the first: the businesses have their own momentum and
management, and three internal candidates are ready, with the board having
already agreed unanimously on a successor if a change were needed today. On the
second, harder question — a board's willingness to tell an ageing CEO he must
go — he pre-commits the board to act, and frames continuing to hold the job past
competence as a threat to his own philanthropic intentions:

> "Every share of Berkshire that I own is destined to go to philanthropies, and
> I want society to reap the maximum good from these gifts and bequests. It
> would be a tragedy if the philanthropic potential of my holdings was
> diminished because my associates shirked their responsibility to (tenderly, I
> hope) show me the door." — [[Sources/2005ltr]]

See [[People/WarrenBuffett]] and [[People/CharlieMunger]], described here as "a
wonder at 82."

### See's Candies changes hands

At yearend Chuck Huggins, 34 years running See's, handed the CEO role to Brad
Kinstler, who had previously run Cypress Insurance and Fechheimer for
Berkshire — an unusual instance of Berkshire moving a manager between
subsidiaries rather than promoting from within the unit itself. See
[[Cases/SeeCandies]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired or agreed**

- **Medical Protective Company (MedPro)** — 106-year-old medical-malpractice
  insurer, bought June 30; loss reserves increased about $125 million
  immediately after purchase.
- **Forest River** — recreational-vehicle manufacturer, $1.6 billion sales,
  closed August 31 on terms reached within a week of first contact.
- **Business Wire** — news-distribution service, bought after founder Lorry
  Lokey and CEO Cathy Baron Tamraz approached Berkshire directly.
- **Applied Underwriters** — 81% acquired, payroll and workers'-compensation
  services, founders retaining 19%.
- **PacifiCorp** — agreed through MidAmerican, requiring $3.4 billion of
  Berkshire common-stock investment plus $1.7 billion of MidAmerican borrowing;
  still pending regulatory approval at yearend.
- Bolt-ons: about $550 million at Shaw for vertical-integration purchases;
  further additions at XTRA and Clayton Homes.

**Portfolio**

- Gillette holding converted into Procter & Gamble via the merger; American
  Express spun off Ameriprise.
- Substantially increased the Wells Fargo position.
- Established new positions in Anheuser-Busch and Wal-Mart.
- Reduced direct foreign-currency contracts somewhat, partly offset by buying
  equities with material non-dollar earnings exposure — a preferred substitute
  once U.S. rates rose enough to make direct currency positions carry a
  negative "carry."
- Currency positions cost $955 million pre-tax in 2005 even as the cumulative
  position remained $2.0 billion in the black since inception.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew to $49.287 billion at a total underwriting profit of only $53
  million, GEICO's gain offsetting Katrina losses at General Re and B-H
  Reinsurance.
- MidAmerican's preferred converted to voting common following PUHCA's repeal,
  raising Berkshire's vote to 83.4% (economic interest unchanged at 80.5%).

**Management**

- Brad Kinstler succeeded Chuck Huggins as CEO of See's Candies.
- Board unanimously agreed on a CEO successor, held in reserve and updated as
  circumstances change.
