# 2000 Annual Letter

The acquisition year — eight businesses for about $8 billion, 97% of it in cash — and the letter
in which Buffett writes the single clearest statement of how he values anything: Aesop's bird in
the bush, three questions, and the claim that the formula has not changed since 600 B.C. and will
not be changed by the Internet. Written as the bubble burst, it also contains the most quotable
description of speculation in the corpus and two frank admissions of error, on GEICO advertising
and on Dexter Shoe.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A decent year, and eight businesses

Net worth rose $3.96 billion, lifting per-share book value 6.5% against the S&P's −9.1% — a
relative gain of 15.6 points. Over 36 years book value per share went from $19 to $40,442, 23.6%
compounded annually; the facing-page table gives an overall 1964-2000 gain of 207,821% against
the index's 5,383%. Buffett judges, subjectively, that the gain in per-share intrinsic value
moderately exceeded the gain in book value.

Two acquisitions negotiated in 1999 closed and six more were initiated: about $8 billion in total,
97% paid in cash and 3% in stock, for businesses with aggregate sales of about $13 billion and
58,000 employees — with no debt incurred and shares outstanding up only one-third of 1%.

> "Try to control your excitement." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

That is the punchline to his announcement that Berkshire has embraced the 21st century by entering
brick, carpet, insulation and paint.

The negatives are stated in the same breath. GEICO's policyholder growth slowed to a halt and new
business became much more expensive:

> "I told you last year that we would get our money's worth from stepped-up advertising at GEICO
> in 2000, but I was wrong." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The equity portfolio is only mildly attractive: excellent businesses, but most holdings fully
priced and unlikely to deliver more than moderate returns — and the long-term prospect for equities
generally is far from exciting.

### Why the managers stay

At most large companies the truly talented divisional managers do not have the job they want; they
yearn to become CEOs, and staying put reads as failure. At Berkshire the all-stars have exactly
the jobs they want and expect to keep for their business lifetimes, so they concentrate solely on
the long-term value of the businesses they own and love.

> "And they stick with us: In our last 36 years, Berkshire has never had a manager of a
> significant subsidiary voluntarily leave to join another business." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The counterweight is the head office: after eight acquisitions more than doubled the worldwide
workforce to about 112,000, headquarters went to 13.8 people — Munger keeping Ben Franklin's
warning about small leaks in view. That group handled the eight acquisitions, a tax return running
4,896 pages, 25,000 annual-meeting tickets and cheques to 3,660 charities, for a company with a
$40 billion revenue run-rate and more than 300,000 owners. See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### Acquisitions of 2000: eight businesses, and what sourced them

> "Our acquisition technique at Berkshire is simplicity itself: We answer the phone."
> — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

- **MidAmerican Energy (76%)** — closed from the 1999 agreement. Regulatory constraints on voting
  mean only a one-line consolidation; full consolidation would have added $5 billion to reported
  revenues without changing net income. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].
- **CORT Business Services** — began with a one-page fax on 23 November 1999 from Bruce Cort, an
  airplane broker who had sold Berkshire a jet in 1986 and had no connection to the company beyond
  his name. Buffett printed the SEC filings the same day, met CEO Paul Arnold on 29 November, and
  bought: a fine though unglamorous business, an outstanding manager, and a price anchored by the
  failed buyout. National leader in *rent-to-rent* furniture from 117 showrooms — explicitly
  distinguished from rent-to-own — bought for Wesco, Berkshire's 80%-owned subsidiary, for about
  $386 million in cash.
- **U.S. Liability** — introduced by Ron Ferguson of General Re. The Berry family had owned it 49
  years; a medium-sized, highly respected writer of excess and surplus lines, agreed by phone,
  half stock and half cash. Tom Nerney manages it and has combined excellent growth with unusual
  profitability. Buffett's aside is the useful part: "Few property-casualty companies are
  outstanding businesses."
- **Ben Bridge Jeweler** — bought by phone before any face-to-face meeting, on Barnett Helzberg's
  strong recommendation. Ed and Jon Bridge are the fourth generation of a business started in
  Seattle 89 years earlier; 65 stores on the West Coast; same-store sales up 9%, 11%, 13%, 10%,
  12%, 21% and 7% over seven years. What mattered to the family was that the company keep
  operating as before — no other chain coming in with ideas about synergy and cost saving — and
  Buffett told them they would be in charge. The Bridges then allocated a substantial part of
  their proceeds to the co-workers who built it.
- **Justin Industries** — July, $570 million in cash, from a 4 May fax by a stranger, Mark Jones,
  proposing Berkshire join a group; Buffett declined to invest with others but offered a
  commission, and the mystery company turned out to be Justin. Leading maker of Western boots
  (Justin, Tony Lama, Nocona, Chippewa) and premier brick producer in Texas and five neighbouring
  states. John Justin, forced to retire by ill health and dead by late February, had groomed
  Harrold Melton at Acme and Randy Watson at Justin Boot, each running his company autonomously.
  Acme makes more than a billion bricks a year at 22 plants, about 11.7% of national output, and
  when Texans are asked to name a brand of brick, 75% say Acme against 16% for the runner-up — a
  brand fact Buffett uses to show a commodity can carry a moat regionally. See [[Concepts/Moat]].
- **Shaw Industries** — the world's largest carpet manufacturer, and the deal that came out of a
  refusal. Bob Shaw and Julian Saul arrived with a merger candidate carrying huge asbestos
  liabilities, wanting Berkshire to write a policy covering all future asbestos costs. Buffett
  said Berkshire could write an exceptionally large policy — larger than any other insurer would
  contemplate — but would never issue one without a cap. Shaw and Saul concluded that if Berkshire
  would not bet the ranch on the liability, neither would they, and their deal died. Berkshire
  then bought 87.3% of Shaw, with both men retaining at least 5%. Annual sales about $4 billion:
  "Leaving aside our insurance operation, Shaw is by far our largest business."
- **Benjamin Moore Paint** — a July call from director Bob Mundheim, a meeting with past and
  present CEOs Richard Roob and Yvan Dupuy in late August, and a $1 billion cash offer on the
  spot; board approval in October, completion in December. Making paint for 117 years, with
  thousands of independent dealers as a vital asset.
- **Johns Manville** — about $1.8 billion, late December. The company had declared bankruptcy in
  1982 over asbestos health claims, and the court-established victims' trust, wanting to diversify,
  had agreed to sell to an LBO buyer that then failed to raise financing. The deal collapsed on
  Friday 8 December; Buffett and Munger called trust chairman Bob Felise on the Monday with an
  all-cash offer carrying no financing contingencies; the trustees voted tentatively the next day
  and a contract was signed a week later. Nation's leading producer of commercial and industrial
  insulation, sales above $2 billion, with CEO Jerry Henry persuaded to postpone retirement.

**Why the year was fertile.** Two economic factors: many owners foresaw near-term slowdowns — and
Buffett notes plainly that several of the companies bought will see earnings decline from 1999 or
2000 peaks, which does not matter to him since all businesses have ups and downs. And the junk
bond market dried up as defaults ballooned, so financial buyers wanting to buy with a sliver of
equity could no longer borrow what they needed, or only at a high price.

> "Because we analyze purchases on an all-equity basis, our evaluations did not change, which
> means we became considerably more competitive." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

**Being the buyer of choice.** The most transferable passage in the section, and a direct addition
to [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]]: Buffett finds it meaningful when an owner cares whom
he sells to. Emotional attachment signals honest accounting, pride of product, respect for
customers and a loyal group of associates with a strong sense of direction — and the reverse is
apt to be true, since a business auctioned by an owner indifferent to what follows has frequently
been dressed up for sale, particularly by a financial owner. Berkshire's structure lets promises
be kept: when Buffett tells John Justin the business stays headquartered in Fort Worth, or assures
the Bridge family its operation will not be merged with another jeweler, those promises can be
taken to the bank.

> "We'll leave the auctions to others." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

### Float, and two things that make Berkshire's numbers unlike anyone else's

The three determinants and the float definition are restated, with the caution sharpened: both
the income statements and balance sheets of insurers can be minefields, and

> "And we warn you that there is nothing symmetrical about surprises in the insurance business:
> They almost always are unpleasant." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

| Year | GEICO | General Re | Other Reinsurance | Other Primary | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1967 | | | | 20 | 20 |
| 1977 | | | 40 | 131 | 171 |
| 1987 | | | 701 | 807 | 1,508 |
| 1997 | 2,917 | | 4,014 | 455 | 7,386 |
| 1998 | 3,125 | 14,909 | 4,305 | 415 | 22,754 |
| 1999 | 3,444 | 15,166 | 6,285 | 403 | 25,298 |
| 2000 | 3,943 | 15,525 | 7,805 | 598 | 27,871 |

*(Yearend float, $ millions.)* Float grew; its cost did not please. A $1.6 billion underwriting
loss produced a float cost of 6%, against a long-run cost very close to zero — and against 1984,
when the cost was a staggering 19%.

Two items distort the comparison with other insurers, and Buffett flags them precisely so readers
do not compare naively:

1. **Pain-today, gain-tomorrow business.** Some insurers with large current losses offload a
   portion onto Berkshire in a way that penalises current earnings but supplies float for years
   afterwards, with no further cost after the first-year loss. That accounted for $400 million of
   the 1999 underwriting loss (27.8% of it) and $482 million in 2000 (34.4%). Other reinsurers
   have little taste for it because they cannot stomach what the reported loss does to their
   numbers, even when the overall economics are certain to be favourable.
2. **Retroactive insurance.** Berkshire assumes a company's *past* losses — the example given is a
   policy covering the first $1 billion of losses from events in 1995 and earlier — always with a
   cap. Under GAAP this neither helps nor hurts current earnings; instead a deferred-charge asset
   is set up for the gap between premium received and higher expected losses, then amortized
   through annual charges that create equivalent underwriting losses stretching into decades. The
   offset is float, and lots of it.

The conclusion is honest about the trade: the cost of float should be very attractive in future
but will rarely return to zero, because retroactive reinsurance lays an annual charge on it — and
what ultimately matters is not only float's cost but how effectively it is deployed. See
[[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

**Ajit Jain** wrote nearly all of the retroactive business.

> "Don't worry about my health; worry about his." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

His year included a $2.4 billion reinsurance premium, perhaps the largest in history, on a policy
retroactively covering a major U.K. company; a policy protecting the Texas Rangers against Alex
Rodriguez becoming permanently disabled, after a record $252 million signing; and a deal with
Grab.com insuring a $1 billion prize with a $170 million present value, which nobody won though
the possibility was far from nil.

> "Charlie and I, however, like any proposition that makes compelling mathematical sense,
> regardless of its effect on reported earnings." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The structural point beneath it: such policies are unique enough that no insurer can lay off the
shock loss through standard reinsurance, so any other CEO writing them runs a real risk of a
horrible quarterly number he would have to explain. Berkshire's indifference to that is the
advantage.

At General Re the news turned better — Ron Ferguson with Joe Brandon and Tad Montross significantly
repriced or dropped severely unprofitable business, and float cost should fall materially absent a
mega-catastrophe. The smaller insurance operations produced an excellent underwriting profit, as
they have for more than a decade.

### GEICO: an error acknowledged and dissected

| Year | New auto policies | Auto policies in force |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1997 | 868,430 | 2,949,439 |
| 1998 | 1,249,875 | 3,562,644 |
| 1999 | 1,648,095 | 4,328,900 |
| 2000 | 1,472,853 | 4,696,842 |

The extra advertising money did not produce a commensurate increase in inquiries, and the
conversion rate fell for the first time in many years — together, a sharp rise in per-policy
acquisition cost. Buffett then turns the admission into a general point:

> "Agonizing over errors is a mistake. But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful, though
> that practice is rare in corporate boardrooms." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

He notes he has almost never witnessed a candid post-mortem of a failed decision in a boardroom,
with The Washington Post Company a notable exception for reviewing its acquisitions three years
on. Elsewhere triumphs are trumpeted while dumb decisions get no follow-up or are dumped into
restructuring charges waved off as nonrecurring:

> "When it comes to corporate blunders, CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth."
> — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Four candidate causes, all probably contributing: advertising frequency was pushed too hard in
some media, where the third ad in an hour on a cable channel cannot match the first; much of the
low-hanging fruit — people already willing to buy insurance direct — may be picked, though a new
generation will find the savings too compelling to ignore; underwriting was deliberately tightened
as loss frequency and severity rose; and rate increases narrowed GEICO's price advantage, since
prospects will switch to save $200 but not to save $50.

The fourth cause is a competitor's choice. **State Farm**, with about 19% of the personal auto
market, has been very slow to raise prices while its costs rose with everyone's — taking an 18%
underwriting loss on auto insurance against GEICO's 4%, and an estimated float cost of about 23%
against GEICO's 6.1%. The largest player's willingness to bear that cost makes the economics hard
for everyone else.

Buffett then does something unusual and praises the company that is hurting him. State Farm was
launched in 1922 by a 45-year-old semi-retired Illinois farmer, against established insurers with
overwhelming advantages in capital, reputation and distribution; as a mutual it had no access to
capital markets during its growth years, and no stock options or lavish salaries — and by 1999 had
amassed a tangible net worth exceeding that of all but four American businesses. He urges it be
studied in business schools precisely because it defies their dogma, adding that studying
counter-evidence is highly useful though not always welcomed at citadels of learning.

The reason he still expects GEICO to win is a single sentence:

> "And, when a company is selling a product with commodity-like economic characteristics, being
> the low-cost producer is all-important." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

That advantage GEICO already possessed in 1951, when a 20-year-old student first became enamoured
with its stock. Profit sharing for the year, on the same two variables as always, came to 8.8% of
salary — $40.7 million. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### Aesop, and how to value anything

The portfolio at 12/31/00, holdings above $1 billion:

| Shares | Company | Cost ($m) | Market ($m) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 151,610,700 | American Express | 1,470 | 8,329 |
| 200,000,000 | Coca-Cola | 1,299 | 12,188 |
| 96,000,000 | Gillette | 600 | 3,468 |
| 1,727,765 | The Washington Post | 11 | 1,066 |
| 55,071,380 | Wells Fargo | 319 | 3,067 |
| | Others | 6,703 | 9,501 |
| | **Total** | **10,402** | **37,619** |

Nearly all Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae shares were sold; 15% positions were established in several
mid-sized companies; some high-yield bonds were bought — "very few — the category is not labeled
junk without reason" — and mortgage-backed holdings added. There are no bargains among current
holdings: content with what is owned, far from excited by it.

**Why whole businesses beat stocks**, beyond preference: the tax code. Owning 80% or more means a
subsidiary's after-tax dollar reaches Berkshire untaxed as a dividend, and retained earnings raise
the tax cost on any eventual sale. A 10% stake earning the same dollar bears about $140,000 of
further tax if distributed (a 14% rate on most dividends) or at least $350,000 if retained and
later realised as a capital gain at about 35%, sometimes approaching 40%.

> "In effect, the government is our 'partner' twice when we own part of a business through a stock
> investment, but only once when we own at least 80%." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Leaving tax aside, the valuation formula for stocks and whole businesses is identical — and
unchanged since about 600 B.C., when Aesop stated it incompletely:

> "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Three questions complete it: how certain are you that there are birds in the bush; when will they
emerge and how many will there be; and what is the risk-free interest rate, which Buffett takes as
the yield on long-term U.S. bonds. Answer those and you know the maximum value of the bush.

> "And, of course, don't literally think birds. Think dollars." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The axiom applies to farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets and manufacturing
plants, and neither the steam engine, electricity, the automobile nor the Internet changed it one
iota. Two corollaries follow, and they are the core of [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]:

> "Common yardsticks such as dividend yield, the ratio of price to earnings or to book value, and
> even growth rates have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to
> the amount and timing of cash flows into and from the business." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

> "Indeed, growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or
> enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later
> years." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

> "Market commentators and investment managers who glibly refer to 'growth' and 'value' styles as
> contrasting approaches to investment are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication."
> — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Growth is simply a component of the value equation — usually a plus, sometimes a minus.

The practical limits are stated too. Precise numbers are foolish; work with a range. Usually the
range is so wide no useful conclusion can be reached — but occasionally even very conservative
estimates show a price startlingly low against value, which Buffett names the Inefficient Bush
Theory. Reaching that conclusion needs general understanding of business economics and independent
thought, but "the investor does not need brilliance nor blinding insights." At the other extreme,
new businesses and rapidly changing industries defeat even the most brilliant investor, and any
capital committed there must be labelled speculative. See [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]] and
[[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### Speculation, written as the bubble burst

Speculation — focusing on what the next person will pay rather than what an asset will produce —
is neither illegal, immoral nor un-American, but not a game Buffett and Munger wish to play: "We
bring nothing to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?"

> "Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The Cinderella image is the letter's most quoted passage. Participants know that continuing to
speculate in companies with gigantic valuations relative to the cash they are likely to generate
will eventually bring pumpkins and mice, so they all plan to leave just before midnight:

> "There's a problem, though: They are dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands."
> — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

The evidence he cites is a Paine Webber-Gallup survey of December 1999 in which investors
averaged an expected 19% annual return for the decade ahead — for American business as a whole,
there could not possibly be enough birds in the 2009 bush. Worse were the valuations placed on
businesses almost certain to end up worth little or nothing, as if a virus had decoupled the
values of stocks from the businesses underlying them.

On the era's talk of value creation, he draws the distinction sharply: much true value has been
created by new and young businesses and more will be, but value is destroyed, not created, by any
business that loses money over its lifetime however high its interim valuation. What actually
occurs is wealth transfer on a massive scale — promoters merchandising birdless bushes have moved
billions from the public's pockets to their own, and a bubble market has allowed the creation of
bubble companies designed to make money off investors rather than for them, with the IPO as the
goal and the chain letter as the business model, investment bankers acting as eager postmen.

> "But a pin lies in wait for every bubble." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

> "Second, speculation is most dangerous when it looks easiest." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Berkshire makes no attempt to pick the winners from an ocean of unproven enterprises — "We're not
smart enough to do that, and we know it" — and Buffett closes the argument by listing his own
misjudgments: "I'm the fellow, remember, who thought he understood the future economics of trading
stamps, textiles, shoes and second-tier department stores." The most promising bushes are now
negotiated purchases of whole businesses, which will at best provide reasonable returns; really
juicy results come only when capital markets are severely constrained and the business world
pessimistic, and "We are 180 degrees from that point."

### Reported earnings, and the Dexter admission

Operating earnings were $1,699 million pre-tax and $936 million after taxes and minority
interests, against $1,085 million and $671 million in 1999. Capital gains of $3,955 million
brought total earnings to $5,654 million pre-tax and $3,328 million after. GEICO's underwriting
swung to a $(224) million pre-tax loss; MidAmerican contributed $197 million.

The exception among the operating businesses was shoes, particularly Dexter, where keeping the
bulk of production in domestic factories has cost dearly.

> "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded
> that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

All remaining accounting goodwill attributable to the Dexter transaction was charged off. This is
the direct sequel to [[Sources/1997ltr]]'s confession that stock-financed mergers have cost
shareholders money, now attached to a specific deal.

FlightSafety spent $272 million on flight simulators, with a similar amount planned:

Buffett uses it to make a point about depreciation: anyone who thinks the annual charge is not a
real cost, every bit as real as payroll or raw materials, "should get an internship at a simulator
company" ([[Sources/2000ltr]]).

Every year the company spends its depreciation charge simply to stay in the same economic place,
and more to grow. Al Ueltschi is 83 and Buffett plans to split his age two-for-one at 100.
Executive Jet's recurring revenue grew 49% after 1999's 46%, on slim margins partly from
developing Europe. Safety spending is unconstrained, with a stated conflict of interest: Buffett,
his family, all but one director and at least nine Berkshire managers fly NetJets. Pilots average
23 days of training a year, fly around 800 hours, and each flies only one model of aircraft.
Customers will take more than 50 new jets in 2001, 7% of world output.

### Full and fair reporting

Full reporting means giving shareholders what Buffett would want if positions were reversed: all
the important facts about current operations plus the CEO's frank view of the long-term economic
characteristics of the business, in the CEO's own words rather than a public relations
department's. No pictures of personnel, plants or products; deep suspicion of vague accounting,
since it usually means management wishes to hide something.

> "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

Fair reporting means reaching 300,000 partners simultaneously — financials posted between Friday's
close and Saturday morning so everyone has time to digest them before Monday. He applauds Arthur
Levitt's crackdown on selective disclosure, under which companies guided analysts and large
holders to earnings expectations through hints, winks and nods, giving speculatively-minded
institutions an edge over investment-oriented individuals — and adds that the reform arriving
through coercion rather than conscience should shame CEOs and their investor relations departments.

Then the argument against growth predictions, which belongs in [[Applications/RedFlags]]. Buffett
calls it deceptive and dangerous for CEOs to predict growth rates. The empirical test he offers:
take the 200 highest-earning companies of 1970 or 1980 and count how many have grown per-share
earnings 15% annually since — only a handful — and he would wager a very significant sum that
fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies of 2000 will manage it over the next 20 years.
The deeper harm is not misplaced optimism but corroded behaviour: CEOs undertake uneconomic
operating manoeuvres to hit announced targets, then accounting games when the manoeuvres run out,
and the games snowball because moving earnings between periods requires ever more heroic follow-up.

> "These can turn fudging into fraud." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

He also documents a specific press failure at length: The Wall Street Journal reported on 29
December, on the front page and without qualification, that Berkshire was buying Conseco and
Finova bonds, several hundred million dollars in each. Berkshire had bought Finova bonds and bank
debt, though the amount reported was wildly inaccurate — and had never bought a share or bond of
Conseco. Other outlets repeated it in lemming-like manner and Conseco stock advanced sharply on
volume that put it ninth on the NYSE most-active list. The word "rumor" never appeared. His
conclusion: the First Amendment allows the media to print almost anything, and journalism's first
principle should require scrupulousness about what that will be.

### Miscellaneous, and Ralph Schey's record

- **Pooling resolved.** The FASB adopted an alternative approach Buffett calls very sound; if
  finalised, Berkshire will no longer incur a large annual charge for amortization of intangibles
  and reported earnings will more closely reflect economic reality — with no effect at all on
  intrinsic value. This is the outcome he argued for in [[Sources/1999ltr]].
- **Ralph Schey.** Berkshire bought Scott Fetzer in 1985 when Schey was 61; most companies,
  focused on the calendar rather than ability, would have had a few years of him. He ran it 15
  years to retirement at the end of 2000, distributing $1.03 billion to Berkshire against a net
  purchase price of $230 million — funds redeployed into other businesses.
- **Fifty years since Graham's class.** Buffett had loved analysing, buying and selling stocks
  through the preceding decade with no better than average results; from 1951 performance
  improved, and the only new ingredient was Ben's ideas.

  > "Quite simply, a few hours spent at the feet of the master proved far more valuable to me than
  > had ten years of supposedly original thinking." — [[Sources/2000ltr]]

- **Contributions** — about 97% of eligible shares participated, contributing $16.9 million; $164
  million cumulatively over 20 years, with subsidiaries giving $18.3 million including $3 million
  in kind.
- **The annual meeting** — Civic Auditorium, Saturday 28 April 2001. NFM's Berkshire Weekend grew
  from $5.3 million in 1997 to $9.1 million in 2000. Bob Shaw has made a 3×5 rug bearing a likeness
  of Munger. Patrick Wolff returns for blindfold chess and Bill Robertie, twice backgammon world
  champion, joins the Borsheim's Olympiad.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired — eight businesses, about $8 billion, 97% cash**

| Business | Terms | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MidAmerican Energy (76%) | from the 1999 agreement | regulated utility; one-line consolidation |
| CORT Business Services | ~$386m cash, via Wesco | rent-to-rent furniture, 117 showrooms |
| U.S. Liability | half stock, half cash | excess and surplus lines insurance |
| Ben Bridge Jeweler | half cash, half stock | 65-store West Coast jeweler |
| Justin Industries | $570m cash | Western boots; Acme brick |
| Shaw Industries (87.3%) | Shaw and Saul retain 5%+ | world's largest carpet manufacturer, ~$4bn sales |
| Benjamin Moore Paint | $1bn cash | paint, thousands of independent dealers |
| Johns Manville | ~$1.8bn all cash | insulation and roofing, >$2bn sales |

No debt was incurred and shares outstanding rose only one-third of 1% — the discipline promised
after the 1998 confession on share issuance, now demonstrated across eight deals.

**Declined**

- **The uncapped asbestos policy** Shaw's merger candidate needed. Berkshire would write an
  exceptionally large policy but never one without a cap, which killed that merger and led instead
  to Berkshire buying Shaw itself.

**Portfolio**

- Sold nearly all Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae shares.
- Established 15% positions in several mid-sized companies (unnamed).
- Bought high-yield bonds of a few issuers, and added high-grade mortgage-backed securities.
- No bargains among current holdings; content but not excited.

**Written off**

- All remaining accounting goodwill on **Dexter Shoe**, with the mistake named twice: the price
  paid in 1993, and compounding it by paying in Berkshire shares.

**Insurance underwriting**

- $1.6 billion underwriting loss, float cost 6%, of which $482 million came from deliberately
  accepted pain-today, gain-tomorrow business.
- Large retroactive covers written, including a $2.4 billion premium on a U.K. company — perhaps
  the largest reinsurance premium in history — plus the Alex Rodriguez disability policy and the
  Grab.com $1 billion prize cover.
- General Re business severely repriced or dropped.
