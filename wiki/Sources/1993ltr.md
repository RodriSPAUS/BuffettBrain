# 1993 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1993: book value up 14.3%, the Dexter Shoe acquisition, and the letter
that redefines risk. It is where Buffett rejects beta, argues that concentration
lowers rather than raises risk, and describes brand strength as "a protective moat
around their economic castles".*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Risk redefined

The central argument of the letter, and it starts with a dictionary.

- "we define risk, using dictionary terms, as "the possibility of loss or injury.""
- Against the academic definition: "Academics, however, like to define investment
  "risk" differently, averring that it is the relative volatility of a stock or
  portfolio of stocks - that is, their volatility as compared to that of a large
  universe of stocks."
- The methodological complaint: "In their hunger for a single statistic to measure
  risk, however, they forget a fundamental principle: It is better to be
  approximately right than precisely wrong."
- The reductio: "under beta-based theory, a stock that has dropped very sharply
  compared to the market - as had Washington Post when we bought it in 1973 -
  becomes "riskier" at the lower price than it was at the higher price. Would that
  description have then made any sense to someone who was offered the entire company
  at a vastly-reduced price?"
- What a beta purist ignores: "a beta purist will disdain examining what a company
  produces, what its competitors are doing, or how much borrowed money the business
  employs. He may even prefer not to know the company's name."

### The real risk, in five factors

- The definition: "the real risk that an investor must assess is whether his
  aggregate after-tax receipts from an investment (including those he receives on
  sale) will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much
  purchasing power as he had to begin with, plus a modest rate of interest on that
  initial stake."
- The five determinants, quoted in condensed form: the certainty with which the
  business's long-term economics can be evaluated; the certainty with which
  management can be evaluated "both as to its ability to realize the full potential
  of the business and to wisely employ its cash flows"; the certainty that
  management will "channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather
  than to itself"; "The purchase price of the business"; and "The levels of taxation
  and inflation that will be experienced".
- On the objection that none of this is quantifiable: "These factors will probably
  strike many analysts as unbearably fuzzy, since they cannot be extracted from a
  data base of any kind. But the difficulty of precisely quantifying these matters
  does not negate their importance nor is it insuperable."

Note that three of the five are about management and one is about price — the same
components as [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]], [[Principles/Fidelity]] and
[[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### Concentration reduces risk

- The premise: "Charlie and I decided long ago that in an investment lifetime it's
  just too hard to make hundreds of smart decisions."
- The strategy: "we adopted a strategy that required our being smart - and not too
  smart at that - only a very few times. Indeed, we'll now settle for one good idea
  a year. (Charlie says it's my turn.)"
- The claim: "We believe that a policy of portfolio concentration may well decrease
  risk if it raises, as it should, both the intensity with which an investor thinks
  about a business and the comfort-level he must feel with its economic
  characteristics before buying into it."

### Volatility as an asset, restated

- "In fact, the true investor welcomes volatility. Ben Graham explained why in
  Chapter 8 of The Intelligent Investor. There he introduced "Mr. Market," an
  obliging fellow who shows up every day to either buy from you or sell to you,
  whichever you wish."
- "The more manic-depressive this chap is, the greater the opportunities available
  to the investor."
- The test of the stance: "After we buy a stock, consequently, we would not be
  disturbed if markets closed for a year or two. We don't need a daily quote on our
  100% position in See's or H. H. Brown to validate our well-being. Why, then,
  should we need a quote on our 7% interest in Coke?"

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]].

### The moat as a castle defence

The image is completed here — 1986 gave the moat, 1993 gives what it protects.

- "The might of their brand names, the attributes of their products, and the
  strength of their distribution systems give them an enormous competitive
  advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles."
- The contrast: "The average company, in contrast, does battle daily without any
  such means of protection."
- The evidence offered is market share: "Worldwide, Coke sells about 44% of all soft
  drinks, and Gillette has more than a 60% share (in value) of the blade market."
- And the rhetorical test: "Is it really so difficult to conclude that Coca-Cola and
  Gillette possess far less business risk over the long term than, say, any computer
  company or retailer?"
- With a borrowed warning: "As Peter Lynch says, stocks of companies selling
  commodity-like products should come with a warning label: "Competition may prove
  hazardous to human wealth.""

Note that this is a *brand* moat, where 1986 described a *cost* moat. Both qualify;
the test is the same. See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]].

### Why you do not sell a wonderful business

- "we continue to think that it is usually foolish to part with an interest in a
  business that is both understandable and durably wonderful. Business interests of
  that kind are simply too hard to replace."
- The inconsistency he points at: "A parent company that owns a subsidiary with
  superb long-term economics is not likely to sell that entity regardless of price.
  "Why," the CEO would ask, "should I part with my crown jewel?" Yet that same CEO,
  when it comes to running his personal investment portfolio, will offhandedly - and
  even impetuously - move from business to business"
- The broker's line he singles out: "The worst of these is perhaps, "You can't go
  broke taking a profit." Can you imagine a CEO using this line to urge his board to
  sell a star subsidiary?"
- The rule: "An investor should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding
  business with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of
  that business."

See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Coca-Cola, and the cost of thinking you are too late

- The 1938 Fortune article, quoted: "Several times every year a weighty and serious
  investor looks long and with profound respect at Coca-Cola's record, but comes
  regretfully to the conclusion that he is looking too late. The specters of
  saturation and competition rise before him."
- What happened next: "in 1938 The Coca-Cola Co. sold 207 million cases of soft
  drinks (if its gallonage then is converted into the 192-ounce cases used for
  measurement today) and in 1993 it sold about 10.7 billion cases, a 50-fold
  increase in physical volume from a company that in 1938 was already dominant in
  its very major industry."
- The arithmetic for a 1938 buyer: "Though the $40 invested in 1919 in one share had
  (with dividends reinvested) turned into $3,277 by the end of 1938, a fresh $40
  then invested in Coca-Cola stock would have grown to $25,000 by yearend 1993."
- And the durability of the description: "In the 55 years that have since passed,
  Coke's product line has broadened somewhat, but it's remarkable how well that
  description still fits."

A direct rebuttal of the instinct that a business already recognised as great is
therefore fully priced. See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Dexter Shoe

The third shoe acquisition in three years, and the one Buffett would later call
his worst deal — though nothing here anticipates that.

- The sequence: "What we did last year was build on our 1991 purchase of H. H.
  Brown, a superbly-run manufacturer of work shoes, boots and other footwear."
- The assessment at the time: "Dexter, I can assure you, needs no fixing: It is one
  of the best-managed companies Charlie and I have seen in our business lifetimes."
- The founder: "Harold Alfond, who started working in a shoe factory at 25 cents an
  hour when he was 20, founded Dexter in 1956 with $10,000 of capital."
- The scale: "a business that now produces over 7.5 million pairs of shoes annually,
  most of them made in Maine and the balance in Puerto Rico."
- And the industry risk, noticed and dismissed: "As you probably know, the domestic
  shoe industry is generally thought to be unable to compete with imports from
  low-wage countries. But someone forgot to tell this to the ingenious managements
  of Dexter and H. H. Brown"

> Worth reading against [[Concepts/Moat]]: the defence claimed here is management
> quality against a structural cost disadvantage — precisely the combination the
> 1991 letter says a *business* (as opposed to a franchise) cannot survive
> indefinitely.

### What Berkshire offered the sellers

- "Harold and Peter know that at Berkshire we can keep our promises: There will be
  no changes of control or culture at Berkshire for many decades to come."
- "Harold and Peter can be sure that they will get to run their business - an
  activity they dearly love - exactly as they did before the merger. At Berkshire,
  we do not tell .400 hitters how to swing."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Cases/ForestRiver]].

### The portfolio, deliberately unchanged

- "Considering the similarity of this year's list and the last, you may decide your
  management is hopelessly comatose."
- Holdings include Coca-Cola at 93,400,000 shares (cost $1,023,920 thousand, market
  $4,167,975 thousand) and GEICO at 34,250,000 shares (cost $45,713 thousand,
  market $1,759,594 thousand).
