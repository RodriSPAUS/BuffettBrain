# 2009 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 19.8%, a $21.8 billion gain in net worth, capped by
the year's headline event: Berkshire's acquisition of Burlington Northern
Santa Fe, its largest deal ever and one that added at least 65,000 new
shareholders. Because so many owners were new to Berkshire, Buffett writes the
letter partly as an orientation — restating how the company measures itself,
listing what it deliberately refuses to do, and walking through each operating
sector from first principles. Two set-piece essays anchor the letter:
Charlie's "invert, always invert" discipline applied to acquisitions and
risk, and a sustained argument against paying for acquisitions with
undervalued stock, titled An Inconvenient Truth (Boardroom Overheating).
Buffett also confesses a credit-card fiasco of his own design and narrates
2009's other crisis, at NetJets, in full.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### How Berkshire measures itself, explained for new owners

Buffett justifies book value as the tracking proxy for intrinsic value despite
its flaws, and states the discipline behind using a fixed yardstick rather
than a moving one:

> "That keeps us from the temptation of seeing where the arrow of performance
> lands and then painting the bull's eye around it." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

The record against that yardstick: in 41 overlapping five-year periods from
1965-69 through 2005-09, Berkshire's book-value gain never once failed to
beat the S&P 500's, and its relative advantage has been driven by defense —
outperforming in every one of the eleven years the index fell — rather than
offense. Buffett does not let the record stand unqualified:

> "The big minus is that our performance advantage has shrunk dramatically as
> our size has grown, an unpleasant trend that is certain to continue."
> — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

### What We Don't Do

The section is framed by Charlie Munger's inversion discipline, traced to the
Prussian mathematician Jacobi:

> "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."
> — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

Four refusals follow. Berkshire avoids businesses whose long-run competitive
economics can't be judged, even when growth is obvious — Buffett cites autos
in 1910, aircraft in 1930 and televisions in 1950 as industries whose winners
were nearly impossible to pick in advance, "even the survivors tended to come
away bleeding." Berkshire refuses to depend on the kindness of strangers:

> "Too-big-to-fail is not a fallback position at Berkshire." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

At the peak of the September 2008 panic, Berkshire supplied rather than
sought liquidity — $15.5 billion deployed, $9 billion of it steadying three
previously secure American businesses and $6.5 billion completing the Wrigley
financing without pause while panic reigned elsewhere. Berkshire also refuses
central control over its subsidiaries, even at the cost of being slow to spot
problems:

> "We would rather suffer the visible costs of a few bad decisions than incur
> the many invisible costs that come from decisions made too slowly – or not
> at all – because of a stifling bureaucracy." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

And it refuses to court Wall Street, seeking instead shareholder-partners who
share the business's own time horizon.

A fourth story illustrates the cost of sound-bite journalism: the 2008 letter's
warning that "the economy will be in shambles throughout 2009" was widely
reported without its second half — that this said nothing about where the
stock market would go. Investors who acted on the truncated headline paid for
it as the Dow rose from 7,063 on the day of the letter to 10,428 by yearend.

### Insurance: from $16 million to $62 billion

> "Our float has grown from $16 million in 1967, when we entered the
> business, to $62 billion at the end of 2009." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

Seven consecutive years of underwriting profit mean that float, in effect,
functions as $62 billion of interest-free deposits available for investment.
Buffett is careful to state this is not the industry's norm:

> "In most years, premiums have been inadequate to cover claims plus
> expenses." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

**GEICO**'s history is told in full: about a third of the company bought for
$47 million in 1976-80, growing to roughly half through the company's own
buybacks with no further Berkshire purchases, then the remaining half bought
for $2.3 billion in cash on January 2, 1996 — about 50 times the original
cost. Market share rose from 2.5% at that acquisition to 8.1% by 2009, a net
gain of seven million policyholders, moving GEICO from the sixth-largest auto
insurer in 1995 to third. Buffett tells the old Wall Street joke about a
broker who insists a stock that quintupled is now an even better buy, closing
with the customer's line: "Damn, I knew I should have waited." He dates his
own connection to GEICO precisely:

> "I became excited about GEICO in January 1951, when I first visited the
> company as a 20-year-old student." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

See [[Cases/GEICO]].

**Ajit Jain**'s arrival is dated to a specific Saturday in 1985:

> "Ajit Jain came into our office in Omaha – and I immediately knew we had
> found a superstar." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

His 30-person operation had by then absorbed Lloyd's $7.1 billion retroactive
liability transfer (the Equitas deal from [[Sources/2006ltr]]) and, during
2009, negotiated a life-reinsurance contract projected to produce $50 billion
of premium over 50 or so years. Buffett's tribute is unqualified:

> "If Charlie, I and Ajit are ever in a sinking boat – and you can only save
> one of us – swim to Ajit." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

**General Re**, under Tad Montross, delivered both an outstanding underwriting
year and unusually large float per dollar of premium, and completed full
ownership of Cologne Re, a partial holding since 1995.

### A painful confession: the GEICO credit card

> "For many years I had struggled to think of side products that we could
> offer our millions of loyal GEICO customers. Unfortunately, I finally
> succeeded, coming up with a brilliant insight that we should market our own
> credit card." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

The card attracted the wrong customers — GEICO's own managers had warned him
it would — producing about $6.3 million of pre-tax operating losses before
Buffett stopped, followed by the sale of a $98 million troubled-receivables
portfolio at 55 cents on the dollar, a further $44 million loss. His summary:

> "I was just older." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

### Regulated Utility Business: BNSF folds in, a "social compact"

MidAmerican's interest reached 89.5%. Among 43 U.S. pipelines ranked by
Mastio, Kern River and Northern Natural tied for second place. Over the prior
three years, Iowa and Western utilities earned $2.5 billion while spending $3
billion on wind generation — reinvestment rather than distribution, as
MidAmerican has never paid Berkshire a dividend since the 2000 purchase.
Buffett announces that BNSF will be folded into this section going forward,
on the strength of a shared economic structure with the utilities:

> "We see a 'social compact' existing between the public and our railroad
> business, just as is the case with our utilities. If either side shirks its
> obligations, both sides will inevitably suffer." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

Both businesses require investment "that greatly exceeds depreciation
allowances for decades to come" and both depend on regulators willing to
guarantee a fair return in exchange. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: who improved profits while sales fell

McLane, under Grady Rosier, delivered record pre-tax earnings of $344 million
on $31.2 billion of sales — about a penny of profit per dollar, on an asset
base of 3,242 trailers, 2,309 tractors and 55 distribution centers. A named
roll of managers who grew profits despite falling sales: Denis Abrams
(Benjamin Moore), Susan Jacques (Borsheims), Jim Issler (H. H. Brown), Vic
Mancinelli (CTB), John Gainor (Dairy Queen), Ron and Irv Blumkin (Nebraska
Furniture Mart), Marla Gottschalk (Pampered Chef), Brad Kinstler (See's) and
Bill Kimbrell (Star Furniture). Marmon (Frank Ptak) posted a record 13.5%
pre-tax margin despite a 27% sales decline; Iscar remained profitable
throughout the year even as its two largest competitors operated at a loss
for much of it, while absorbing the Tungaloy integration from
[[Sources/2008ltr]]. Construction-linked businesses (Shaw, Johns Manville,
Acme Brick, MiTek) fell 82.5% in combined pre-tax earnings from their 2006
peak, to $227 million.

**NetJets** was the year's other crisis. Eleven years of ownership had
produced a cumulative pre-tax loss of $157 million and debt that had grown
from $102 million at purchase to $1.9 billion by April 2009:

> "It's clear that I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this
> condition." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

Dave Sokol, moved over from MidAmerican, became CEO in August; debt fell to
$1.4 billion and the company turned solidly profitable after a $711 million
loss earlier in 2009. Buffett is explicit that safety and service standards
set by predecessor Rich Santulli were untouched by the turnaround, and offers
the same personal-use test cited in earlier letters: Berkshire's own
directors and managers, including Buffett, pay full price and receive no
special treatment.

### Finance: the mortgage-rate gap Clayton can't close

Manufactured-housing volume fell from 382,000 units (1999) to 60,000 (2009),
and Buffett attributes the collapse to two causes — a necessary, economy-wide
housing correction, and a structural one specific to the industry: government-
backed agencies insure conventional site-built mortgages at about 5¼%, while a
factory-built home buyer, lacking that backing, pays about 9%:

> "For the all-cash buyer, Clayton's homes offer terrific value. If the buyer
> needs mortgage financing, however – and, of course, most buyers do – the
> difference in financing costs too often negates the attractive price of a
> factory-built home." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

Clayton's own borrowers, chosen for the intention to live in rather than flip
or refinance their homes, kept delinquencies manageable through the downturn.
At yearend, Berkshire became a 50% owner of Berkadia Commercial Mortgage
(formerly Capmark) alongside Leucadia's Joe Steinberg and Ian Cumming — the
same partners from the 2001 Finova rescue, referenced in [[Sources/2001ltr]] —
prompting Buffett's own joke about a coming "Son of Berkadia."

### Derivatives: quarterly swings, and where risk control sits

Derivatives float — separate from the $62 billion of insurance float — stood
at $6.3 billion at yearend, against a peak collateral-posting requirement of
just $1.7 billion at the worst of the 2009 credit markets. Quarterly
mark-to-market swings on the contracts ranged from a $1.517 billion loss in
Q1 to a $2.357 billion gain in Q2. Buffett restates who is accountable for
that volatility, in terms that generalize past Berkshire:

> "Charlie and I believe that a CEO must not delegate risk control. It's
> simply too important." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

At Berkshire, Buffett writes, he personally initiates and monitors every
derivatives contract on the books.

> "If Berkshire ever gets in trouble, it will be my fault. It will not be
> because of misjudgments made by a Risk Committee or Chief Risk Officer."
> — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

He extends the principle into a governance standard for the industry: boards
of large financial institutions are "derelict" if they let a CEO offload risk
accountability, and argues the CEOs and directors of the era's failed
institutions — unlike their shareholders, who lost over $500 billion across
the four largest fiascos — emerged largely unscathed, a mismatch he wants
corrected with real personal consequences. See [[Applications/RedFlags]] and
[[Sources/2008ltr]].

### An Inconvenient Truth: paying for acquisitions with undervalued stock

The letter's fullest statement of why Berkshire dislikes issuing its own
shares in a deal:

> "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current
> market price, why in the world should we 'sell' a significant part of the
> company at that same inadequate price by issuing our stock in a merger?"
> — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

A worked example: two equal companies, both intrinsically worth $100 a share
but both trading at $80. If Company A offers 1¼ shares for each share of B
(correctly valuing B at $100), the deal actually costs A's shareholders $125
of intrinsic value per B share received, handing B's owners 55.6% of the
combined company for 50% of the combined value — a loss visible to no one
except A's shareholders, since its CEO now runs a company twice as large as
his original domain, "in a world where size tends to correlate with both
prestige and compensation." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

For BNSF, about 30% of the price was ultimately paid in
Berkshire stock, a tradeoff Buffett calls a close call rather than an easy
one — "If we had needed to use more stock to make the acquisition, it would
in fact have made no sense."

Buffett's structural fix for board deliberations facing a stock-for-stock
proposal is a second, deal-contingent advisor:

> "Directors should hire a second advisor to make the case against the
> proposed acquisition, with its fee contingent on the deal not going
> through." — [[Sources/2009ltr]]

He tells a bank-merger story from his own investing history to make the point
concrete: a well-run bank he owned overpaid a much smaller rival roughly three
times book value in stock once regulatory restrictions on acquisitions were
lifted, purely to "show that we are in the hunt." Munger's verdict at the
time: "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a
Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?" See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and
[[People/CharlieMunger]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Burlington Northern Santa Fe** — Berkshire's largest acquisition to date,
  about 30% paid in Berkshire stock and the remainder in cash, folded into
  the regulated-utility reporting sector going forward.
- **Berkadia Commercial Mortgage** (formerly Capmark) — 50% owned alongside
  Leucadia, servicing a $235 billion mortgage portfolio.
- Non-traded positions in Dow Chemical, General Electric, Goldman Sachs,
  Swiss Re and Wrigley, all purchased within the prior 18 months for $21.1
  billion, carried at $26.0 billion, yielding $2.1 billion annually.

**Sold**

- Large reductions in ConocoPhillips, Moody's, Procter & Gamble and Johnson &
  Johnson, partly to fund the Dow/Swiss Re purchases and partly to raise cash
  ahead of the BNSF acquisition.

**Insurance**

- Float grew to $61.911 billion at a $1.559 billion underwriting profit, the
  seventh consecutive profitable year.
- Ajit Jain negotiated a life-reinsurance contract projected at $50 billion
  of premium over roughly 50 years.
- Closed the GEICO credit-card business, total losses of about $50 million
  between operating losses and the discounted receivables sale.

**Utilities**

- MidAmerican's interest rose to 89.5%; Iowa and Western utilities spent $3
  billion on wind generation over three years against $2.5 billion earned.
