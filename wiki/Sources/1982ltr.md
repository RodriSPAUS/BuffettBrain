# 1982 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1982: operating earnings of $31.5 million, 9.8% of beginning equity —
the worst ratio of the era — against a 40% gain in net worth. Buffett discards
his own headline yardstick and explains why, then writes the two essays this
letter is remembered for: why an industry with excess capacity and an
undifferentiated product cannot earn well, and why issuing shares to make an
acquisition is usually a transfer of wealth away from your own owners.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Retiring a yardstick, with the conflict of interest stated first

Operating earnings fell to 9.8% of beginning equity from 15.2%, far below the
19.4% of 1978. Buffett had previously called that ratio the most important
measure of single-year performance. He now abandons it — and opens by telling
shareholders not to take the argument at face value:

- "You should be suspicious of such an assertion. Yardsticks seldom are discarded
  while yielding favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers
  favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager."
- The bad version of what he is doing: "just shoot the arrow of business
  performance into a blank canvas and then carefully draw the bullseye around the
  implanted arrow. We generally believe in pre-set, long-lived and small
  bullseyes."

The reason he thinks his own case is the exception is item (3) of the three
causes he lists for the decline: a growing share of Berkshire's resources sat in
partially-owned businesses whose earnings accounting rules exclude. That is a
measurement problem, not a performance excuse — and the next section is spent
proving it.

This is the clearest instance in the corpus of [[Principles/Fidelity]] applied
against Buffett's own interest: naming the incentive to cheat before doing the
thing that looks like cheating.

### Accounting earnings versus economic earnings

The 20% ownership threshold decides whether a shareholder's proportionate
earnings appear at all. Berkshire owned about 35% of GEICO but had assigned its
voting rights, so GEICO was treated as a sub-20% holding: $3.5 million of
after-tax dividends entered reported earnings and $23 million of Berkshire's
share of GEICO's undistributed earnings did not.

Buffett makes the absurdity concrete rather than abstract. If GEICO had earned
*less* and paid $1 million more in dividends, Berkshire's reported earnings would
have risen. If GEICO had earned an additional $100 million and retained it,
reported earnings would have been unchanged.

- The principle: "In our view, the value to all owners of the retained earnings of
  a business enterprise is determined by the effectiveness with which those
  earnings are used - and not by the size of one's ownership percentage."
- The limit of the criticism: "This is not a criticism of accounting procedures.
  We would not like to have the job of designing a better system. It's simply to
  say that managers and investors alike must understand that accounting numbers
  are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."

The 1981 forecast of $35 million came in "well over $40 million" across GEICO,
General Foods, The Washington Post and an enlarged R. J. Reynolds stake — more
than Berkshire's total reported earnings, which contained only the $14 million of
dividends from the same four companies.

He then declines to over-claim, which is what makes the number usable: realizing
those earnings is "subject to very substantial, but indeterminate, taxation", and
the translation into market value has been "both extraordinarily uneven among
companies and irregular and unpredictable in timing."

See [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### Why unevenness is an advantage, not a defect

The irregularity Buffett has just conceded is then turned into the case for
buying fractions of businesses rather than whole ones. The buyer of a fractional
interest may choose from nearly every major American corporation, "including many
far superior to virtually any of the businesses that could be bought in their
entirety in a negotiated deal."

And the seller on the other side is not a negotiator: "fractional-interest
purchases can be made in an auction market where prices are set by participants
with behavior patterns that sometimes resemble those of an army of
manic-depressive lemmings."

The job that follows is stated as a testable standard, not an aspiration —
"Within this gigantic auction arena, it is our job to select businesses with
economic characteristics allowing each dollar of retained earnings to be
translated eventually into at least a dollar of market value." Buffett credits
the aggregate result partly to offsetting, not to precision: some positions
returned nothing on retained earnings, others two dollars or more, and "our
corporate over-achievers have more than offset the laggards."

The preference underneath is unchanged: "Satisfactory as our partial-ownership
approach has been, what really makes us dance is the purchase of 100% of good
businesses at reasonable prices."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]] and [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### The deal that fell through, reported as the year's achievement

Reviewing the acquisitions others made in 1982, Buffett writes that "our reaction
is not envy, but relief that we were non-participants", diagnosing the failure as
temperamental rather than analytical: "The thrill of the chase blinded the
pursuers to the consequences of the catch."

Then he puts himself in the same category. "Your Chairman left the room once too
often last year and almost starred in the Acquisition Follies of 1982." A large
purchase Berkshire had firmly committed to could not be completed for reasons
outside its control, and he calls that the major accomplishment of the year: "If
we were to introduce graphics to this report, illustrating favorable business
developments of the past year, two blank pages depicting this blown deal would be
the appropriate centerfold."

The strategy's dependence on price is stated without flinching. A
partial-ownership approach works only as long as portions of attractive
businesses can be bought at attractive prices — and a rising market would take
that away. Buffett notes that in 1972, at the top of the two-tier market,
Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries held only $18 million of equities, about 15%
of investments against 80% in 1982: "There were as many good businesses around in
1972 as in 1982, but the prices the stock market placed upon those businesses in
1972 looked absurd."

- "The market, like the Lord, helps those who help themselves. But, unlike the
  Lord, the market does not forgive those who know not what they do."
- "For the investor, a too-high purchase price for the stock of an excellent
  company can undo the effects of a subsequent decade of favorable business
  developments."

See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Commodity economics — the moat argument in its original form

The insurance section builds a general theory before applying it, and this is
where the wiki's moat concept actually originates. The setup: businesses in
industries with both substantial over-capacity and an undifferentiated product
are prime candidates for profit trouble, unless prices or costs are administered
— legally through regulation, illegally through collusion, or through cartels.

- Absent that: "If, however, costs and prices are determined by full-bore
  competition, there is more than ample capacity, and the buyer cares little about
  whose product or distribution services he uses, industry economics are almost
  certain to be unexciting. They may well be disastrous."
- Differentiation is the escape, and it is not always available — it works for
  candy bars, which customers buy by brand name, and not for sugar.
- The other escape, in the words the later letters reuse verbatim: "A few
  producers in such industries may consistently do well if they have a cost
  advantage that is both wide and sustainable. By definition such exceptions are
  few, and, in many industries, are non-existent."

On why over-capacity keeps returning: corrections come late, and the recovery
produces "a pervasive enthusiasm for expansion that, within a few years, again
creates over-capacity and a new profitless environment. In other words, nothing
fails like success."

Applied to insurance, the diagnosis is structural and permanent: "in the insurance
business, to return to that subject, capacity can be instantly created by capital
plus an underwriter's willingness to sign his name." Supply is mental rather than
physical, so it cannot shrink the way a factory closes.

The historical profitability of underwriting is then explained away rather than
extrapolated. From 1950 through 1970 the industry combined ratio averaged 99.0,
because rates were effectively negotiated between industry bureaus and state
regulators: "Dignified haggling occurred, but it was between company and regulator
rather than between company and customer." That regime had ended — new capacity
outside the old structure competes on price and "relishes that use. In the
process, customers have learned that insurance is no longer a one-price business.
They won't forget."

Which is why Buffett rejects the cycle framing everyone else was using: "It's not
only generals that prefer to fight the last war. Most business and investment
analysis also comes from the rear-view mirror."

His forecast for the coming year needs no cycle theory at all: "underwriting
results in 1983 will not be a sight for the squeamish." And the reported 109.5
combined ratio is called a best case, because "In insurance, as elsewhere, the
reaction of weak managements to weak operations is often weak accounting."

See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Concepts/Float]].

### Issuance of equity: the letter's central essay

Berkshire and Blue Chip were considering a merger, which gives Buffett occasion
for the most systematic argument in the early letters about what a share issuance
actually is.

The rule: "we will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business
value as we give." Which sounds trivial — "Why, you might ask, would anyone issue
dollar bills in exchange for fifty-cent pieces? Unfortunately, many corporate
managers have been willing to do just that."

The mechanism is an asymmetry. A seller in a negotiated deal gets full business
value. A buyer paying in its own shares is making a partial sale of itself, and
"it can customarily get no higher value set on its shares than the market chooses
to grant it." So when the buyer's stock trades below intrinsic value:

- "The acquirer who nevertheless barges ahead ends up using an undervalued (market
  value) currency to pay for a fully valued (negotiated value) property. In effect,
  the acquirer must give up $2 of value to receive $1 of value. Under such
  circumstances, a marvelous business purchased at a fair sales price becomes a
  terrible buy."
- "For gold valued as gold cannot be purchased intelligently through the
  utilization of gold - or even silver - valued as lead."

Buffett then takes apart three standard rationalizations. The one worth
reproducing in full is the answer to *we have to grow*, because it makes the
dilution visible in a way per-share arithmetic does not:

> "If (1) your family owns a 120-acre farm and (2) you invite a neighbor with 60
> acres of comparable land to merge his farm into an equal partnership - with you
> to be managing partner, then (3) your managerial domain will have grown to 180
> acres but you will have permanently shrunk by 25% your family's ownership
> interest in both acreage and crops. Managers who want to expand their domain at
> the expense of owners might better consider a career in government."

On who supplies the reassurance: "Friendly investment bankers will reassure him as
to the soundness of his actions. (Don't ask the barber whether you need a
haircut.)" And on the projections that make it look sound: "While deals often fail
in practice, they never fail in projections - if the CEO is visibly panting over a
prospective acquisition, subordinates and consultants will supply the requisite
projections to rationalize any price."

Three ways to avoid destroying value are given: a true
business-value-for-business-value merger such as Berkshire–Blue Chip intends to
be; issuing stock only when it trades at or above intrinsic value; or buying back
an equal number of shares afterwards, which converts a stock deal into a cash
deal. The third is explicitly second-best — "Scoring touchdowns is more
exhilarating than recovering one's fumbles."

Two language traps are named. *Dilution* is computed on book value and near-term
earnings per share, which is the wrong test: "What really counts is whether a
merger is dilutive or anti-dilutive in terms of intrinsic business value". And
*A acquires B* obscures what happened; the accurate description is that part of A
was sold. "In a trade, what you are giving is just as important as what you are
getting."

The section closes on the durable cost, which is a reputation effect rather than
a one-time loss. A management with a record of value-destroying issuance is
marked down permanently: "Once management shows itself insensitive to the
interests of owners, shareholders will suffer a long time from the price/value
ratio afforded their stock".

Berkshire's own commitment: "we will issue shares only if our owners receive in
business value as much as we give. We will not equate activity with progress or
corporate size with owner-wealth."

See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### The acquisition advertisement

The letter contains the first full statement of the criteria Berkshire would
repeat for forty years: purchases of at least $5 million of after-tax earnings,
demonstrated consistent earning power rather than projections or turnarounds,
good returns on equity with little or no debt, management already in place —
Buffett notes plainly that Berkshire cannot supply it — simple businesses, and a
stated asking price.

- "We will not engage in unfriendly transactions. We can promise complete
  confidentiality and a very fast answer as to possible interest - customarily
  within five minutes."

See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### GEICO — 35% interest, $79 million of the year's $208 million gain

The largest single contributor again, and again Buffett separates market
performance from business performance rather than banking the difference:
"GEICO's increase in market value during the past two years has been considerably
greater than the gain in its intrinsic business value, impressive as the latter
has been."

Why he expects that to be fine anyway: "We expected such a favorable variation at
some point, as the perception of investors converged with business reality."

GEICO is also the worked example of the letter's own commodity framework — "a
magnificent illustration of the high-profit exception we described earlier in
discussing commodity industries with over-capacity - a company with a wide and
sustainable cost advantage." The 35% interest represented about $250 million of
premium volume, more than all the direct volume Berkshire wrote itself.

On the managers: "Jack Byrne and Bill Snyder are achieving the most elusive of
human goals - keeping things simple and remembering what you set out to do." Lou
Simpson is called the best investment manager in the property-casualty business.
See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### The abandoned major acquisition

Committed to, then blocked for reasons outside Berkshire's control. Buffett rates
it the year's best outcome. No name is given.

### Insurance underwriting — from above average to below it

Berkshire's underwriting deteriorated further than the industry's, moving from
well above average to modestly below. The largest swing was in National
Indemnity's traditional coverages: "Lines that have been highly profitable for us
in the past are now priced at levels that guarantee underwriting losses." The
guidance offered is unusually bleak: "In 1983 we expect our insurance group to
record an average performance in an industry in which average is very poor."

Milt Thornton at Cypress and Floyd Taylor at Kansas Fire and Casualty are named
for producing an underwriting profit every year since joining. Mike Goldberg took
over parent-company responsibility for insurance operations from Buffett himself.

### Berkshire–Blue Chip merger, under consideration

Proposed as a business-value-for-business-value exchange — the first of the three
non-destructive share issuances the letter defines, and the reason the essay on
equity issuance appears at all.

### Net worth and book value

The gain in net worth was $208 million on a beginning base of $519 million, or
40%. Over eighteen years book value had grown from $19.46 to $737.43 per share,
22.0% compounded — reported with the standing warning attached: "You can be
certain that this percentage will diminish in the future. Geometric progressions
eventually forge their own anchors."

### Shareholder-designated contributions

95.8% of eligible shares participated, on $1 per share, down from $2 in 1981.

### Retirements: Phil Liesche and Ben Rosner

Buffett's account of why they mattered is a statement about character rather than
incentives: "Both Ben and Phil ran their businesses for Berkshire with every bit
of the care and drive that they would have exhibited had they personally owned
100% of these businesses. No rules were necessary to enforce or even encourage
this attitude; it was embedded in the character of these men long before we came
on the scene. Their good character became our good fortune."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].
