# 1998 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 48.3% and Buffett spends the first page explaining why the number is
close to meaningless: most of it came from issuing shares above book value in the year's two
acquisitions, which raises book value per share without earning a dime. The year's real content
is the $22 billion General Re purchase, the $725 million Executive Jet purchase, the float table
jumping from $7 billion to $22.8 billion — and two long sections attacking accounting practice
that are among the most quotable in the whole corpus.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A 48.3% gain that is not what it looks like

Net worth rose $25.9 billion, lifting per-share book value 48.3%. Over 34 years book value per
share went from $19 to $37,801, 24.7% compounded annually.

> "Remember Wagner, whose music has been described as better than it sounds?"
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Berkshire's stock sells at a large premium over book value, so issuing shares — for cash or as
merger consideration — instantly raises per-share book value because more book value comes in
than goes out. Intrinsic value gets no such lift: what is given and what is received are roughly
equal. Buffett restates the priority that orders everything:

> "it's the per-share gain in intrinsic value that counts rather than the per-share gain in book
> value" — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Intrinsic value grew substantially in 1998 but well short of 48.3%, while still far exceeding
book value. This is the sharpest correction in the corpus of the habit of reading Berkshire's
headline number as performance. See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

The honest debits are stated in the same breath: several public companies in which Berkshire
holds major investments had significant operating shortfalls neither they nor Buffett
anticipated, so the equity portfolio did not perform nearly as well as the S&P 500. He judges
the problems temporary and the long-term prospects excellent.

### The two-column table, distorted by General Re

Including General Re on a pro-forma basis as if owned all year:

| Year | Investments per share | Pre-tax earnings per share with all income from investments excluded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1968 | $53 | $2.87 |
| 1978 | $465 | $12.85 |
| 1988 | $4,876 | $145.77 |
| 1998 | $47,647 | $474.45 |

Investments rose $9,604 per share (25.2%) while per-share operating earnings fell 33.9%. General
Re explains both: it carries very large investments, and it ran an underwriting loss in 1998.
Without the acquisition, per-share operating earnings would have shown a modest gain. Annual
growth 1968-1998 was 25.4% for investments and 18.6% for operating earnings.

### Size, and a 15% promise treated as a ceiling

Berkshire is compounding a net worth of $57.4 billion, the largest of any American corporation —
though Buffett notes it will be eclipsed if the Exxon/Mobil merger goes through, and that net
worth is not value: General Electric and Microsoft carry valuations more than three times
Berkshire's. Net worth measures the capital managers must deploy, and Berkshire's has become
huge.

> "Berkshire's capital base is now simply too large to allow us to earn truly outsized returns."
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

He commits to trying for 15% average growth in intrinsic value while calling it "at the very
peak of possible outcomes", with years above and years of negative return below. The arithmetic
is spelled out: 15% over five years means increasing net worth by $58 billion.

> "Earning this daunting 15% will require us to come up with big ideas: Popcorn stands just
> won't do." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Today's markets are unfriendly to the search for elephants; the hunt continues anyway. He also
renews the alignment promise — at least 99% of his net worth stays in Berkshire for as long as
he is around — and notes that headquarters staff went from 12 to 12.8 people against 47,566 on
the payroll, with after-tax overhead of about $3.5 million, well under one basis point of assets
managed. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Taxes

Berkshire and General Re paid or will soon pay $2.7 billion of federal income tax on 1998
earnings — enough to shoulder all of the U.S. Government's expenses for more than half a day. If
only 625 other taxpayers had matched them, no one else would have paid any federal tax at all.
Buffett says writing those cheques does not bother him: he and Berkshire prospered in America as
they would have nowhere else, and he counts it luck to be writing cheques to the government
rather than receiving them.

Two corrections of common misunderstandings: capital gains hold no special attraction, because a
corporation pays 35% whether income comes from gains or operations — making Berkshire's tax on a
long-term capital gain fully 75% higher than an individual's on the same gain. And the dividends-
received exclusion is 59.5% for stocks held in insurance subsidiaries, where almost all of
Berkshire's equities sit, not the 70% that applies to most corporations. A dividend dollar is
still worth more than an ordinary-income dollar, but by less than usually assumed.

The General Re merger also required a $30 million cheque to the SEC in fees on the new shares —
understood to be a record.

### GEICO: paying for growth that accounting punishes

The compensation design is the argument. Direct writing carries a substantial front-end cost, so
first-year business is unprofitable in a major way — and GEICO deliberately leaves that cost out
of the bonus formula so it cannot deter the pursuit of new business. Half of associates' bonuses
and profit sharing rest on the earnings of the *seasoned* book (policies held more than a year),
half on growth in policyholders.

> "Combine a great idea with a great manager and you're certain to obtain a great result."
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Marketing went from $33 million with 652 telephone counselors in 1995, the year before Berkshire
acquired the company, to $143 million and 2,162 counselors in 1998, with at least $190 million
budgeted for 1999. Buffett states there is no limit to what Berkshire will invest in new
business as long as the servicing infrastructure can keep pace, and names the reason others
would not: companies concerned about quarterly or annual earnings would shy away regardless of
long-term value. His own test is simply whether more than a dollar of value is created per dollar
spent.

Voluntary auto policies in force grew from 2,949,439 to 3,562,644. *(A caveat on this table: the
new-policy column in `Raw/1998ltr.md` reads 1,354,882 for 1993 where the 1997 letter gives
354,882 for the same year, so the leading digit appears to be an extraction artifact. The
in-force column agrees across both letters and is the one used here.)*

1998's profit margin was 6.7% — better than anticipated and better than wished, reflecting an
industry-wide fall in both frequency and severity of accidents. Rates were cut 3.3% in 1998 with
more to come, aiming margins back to the 4% target or lower, while still expecting to beat the
industry. Profit sharing of $103 million, 32.3% of salary, went to all 9,313 associates with
more than a year's service, possibly the highest percentage payment at any large company in the
country; 4,612 people were promoted. On service quality, the New York Insurance Department
reported GEICO's 1997 complaint ratio as the lowest of the five largest auto insurers and less
than half the average of the other four. Market share improved from 3% to 3½% — for every
policyholder, another ten should be customers. See [[Cases/GEICO]] and
[[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### The mandate given to managers

An aside addressed to CEOs of public companies, and the clearest statement of Berkshire's
operating model in the corpus. Berkshire eliminates the ritualistic and nonproductive parts of
the job — managers control their own schedules — and gives a three-part mission: run the business
as if you own 100% of it, as if it is the only asset your family will ever have, and as if you
cannot sell or merge it for at least a century. The corollary is that no decision should be
affected in the slightest by accounting considerations.

> "We want our managers to think about what counts, not how it will be counted."
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Few public-company CEOs operate under such a mandate because their owners focus on short-term
prospects and reported earnings. Berkshire's shareholder base has the longest horizon in the
public-company universe — a majority of shares are held by investors who expect to die still
holding them — which is what buys the freedom. Tony Nicely never has to think about financing,
credit ratings or Street expectations for earnings per share. Around 75% of Berkshire's managers
are independently wealthy and would not tolerate backseat driving.

### Executive Jet: buying an industry its founder invented

Rich Santulli created the fractional-ownership industry in 1986. A buyer purchases a fraction of
a jet — a 1/8th share entitles the owner to 100 hours of flying annually, with dead-head hours
excluded and hours averageable over five years — plus a monthly management fee and an hourly fee,
and on a few hours' notice EJA delivers that plane or a better one at any of 5,500 U.S. airports.

Buffett heard of the NetJets program about four years earlier from Frank Rooney of H.H. Brown;
Santulli took about 15 minutes to sell him a quarter share of a Hawker 1000, and his family flew
900 hours on 300 trips before any purchase was contemplated. He had already done a testimonial
ad. Santulli called in May and the $725 million deal — half cash, half stock — was made quickly.

EJA is by far the largest operator in its industry: more than 1,000 customers and 163 aircraft,
including 23 core aircraft owned or leased by EJA itself so service holds up at peak demand.
About 650 pilots train at least twice a year at [[Sources/1996ltr]]'s FlightSafety, another
Berkshire subsidiary. Buffett sold the Berkshire plane and moved all his flying to NetJets crews.

The competitive point is worth carrying: the two competitors are owned by manufacturers and can
offer only their own aircraft, while NetJets offers Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna and
Raytheon — "like a physician who can recommend whatever medicine best fits the needs of each
patient". Leadership also compounds, because an armada positioned across the country both
improves service and cuts dead-head costs. In 1997 EJA accounted for 31% of all corporate jets
ordered in the world, and Buffett argues the potential is barely scratched: whole-plane ownership
only makes economic sense above 350-400 hours a year, so the addressable population for
fractions must be a large multiple of it. The closing argument to a personal buyer is the one he
gave his Aunt Alice about a fur coat: "Alice, you aren't buying it; your heirs are."

See [[Concepts/Moat]] for the multi-manufacturer distinction and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]
for Santulli, who like most Berkshire managers has no economic need to work.

### General Re: buying a business to remove a constraint

Completed December 21 for $22 billion: 100% of General Reinsurance Corporation, the largest U.S.
property-casualty reinsurer, and 82% of Cologne Re, the oldest reinsurance company in the world.
Together they reinsure all lines and operate in 124 countries, under Ron Ferguson.

The reasoning is the most interesting part, and it is structural rather than financial. Demand
for reinsurance comes from primary insurers wanting to shed the earnings swings caused by large
and unusual losses — so a reinsurer is paid to absorb volatility. Yet a publicly held reinsurer
is itself graded by owners and credit raters on the *smoothness* of its results, and wide swings
damage credit ratings and p/e ratios even when the business has satisfactory expected profits.
That pressure drives costly moves: retroceding a significant portion of what it writes, or
turning down good business purely because it brings volatility.

Berkshire has no such constraint. It accepts volatility as long as expected profits rise with it,
and its capital position means volatile earnings cannot impair its credit ratings.

> "we prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Berkshire supplies the balance sheet and General Re supplies the distribution, technical
facilities and management to apply it across the whole industry, particularly internationally
where most industry growth will occur. Berkshire takes over General Re's investment portfolio but
not Cologne Re's, and will not be involved in underwriting — the ask is simply to keep the old
discipline while retaining more business, widening the product line and extending geographically.
Buffett notes that General Re played a key role in resuscitating GEICO from near-death in 1976.

### Float: the three questions, and a jump to $22.8 billion

With General Re acquired and GEICO mushrooming, Buffett says understanding insurance evaluation
matters more than ever, and reduces it to three determinants: the amount of float, its cost, and
— most important — the long-term outlook for both.

> "To begin with, float is money we hold but don't own." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

The definition and the reserving caution are repeated essentially verbatim from
[[Sources/1997ltr]]: because loss costs must be estimated, insurers have enormous latitude in
figuring underwriting results, errors are usually innocent but sometimes not, and he has been
amazed at numbers big-name auditors have implicitly blessed.

| Year | Average float ($m) |
| --- | --- |
| 1967 | 17 |
| 1972 | 70 |
| 1977 | 139 |
| 1982 | 221 |
| 1987 | 1,267 |
| 1992 | 2,290 |
| 1997 | 7,093 |
| 1998 | 22,762 (yearend) |

Float has compounded at 25.4% annually over 32 years, and average cost has been well under zero
— an aggregate underwriting profit, meaning Berkshire has been paid to hold a large and growing
sum. As long as underwriting stays profitable, float outranks net worth in value despite sitting
on the balance sheet as a liability. He warns that growth in float may be modest for a few years:
the reinsurance market is soft, relationships change slowly, and General Re's float — two-thirds
of the total — is unlikely to increase much near-term. See [[Concepts/Float]] and
[[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### Reported earnings, and the easy job

Pre-tax operating earnings were $1,899 million against $1,721 million in 1997; after taxes and
minority interests, $1,277 million against $1,197 million. Capital gains of $2,415 million pre-tax
brought total earnings to $4,314 million pre-tax and $2,830 million after — against $2,827 million
and $1,901 million in 1997. Look-through earnings are omitted this year: General Re closed near
yearend, so neither a historical nor a pro-forma figure would be meaningful, and the calculation
resumes next year.

Scott Fetzer supplies the standout: operating with no leverage beyond a conservative level of
debt in its finance subsidiary, Ralph Schey's company earned a record $96.5 million after tax on
$112 million of net worth.

> "Charlie and I have the easy jobs at Berkshire: We do very little except allocate capital."
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

The excuse offered is a principle:

> "In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

In investments and acquisitions, frenetic behaviour is often counterproductive — "Charlie and I
mainly just wait for the phone to ring." See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### The portfolio, a confession, and a disclosure policy

Holdings above $750 million at 12/31/98:

| Shares | Company | Cost ($m) | Market ($m) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50,536,900 | American Express | 1,470 | 5,180 |
| 200,000,000 | Coca-Cola | 1,299 | 13,400 |
| 51,202,242 | Walt Disney | 281 | 1,536 |
| 60,298,000 | Freddie Mac | 308 | 3,885 |
| 96,000,000 | Gillette | 600 | 4,590 |
| 1,727,765 | The Washington Post | 11 | 999 |
| 63,595,180 | Wells Fargo | 392 | 2,540 |
| | Others | 2,683 | 5,135 |
| | **Total** | **7,044** | **37,265** |

American Express was slightly increased, the other two of the three largest commitments left
unchanged, and many smaller positions trimmed or substantially cut. Then the confession:

> "you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off to the movies during
> market hours" — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Selling McDonald's is named as a very big mistake. More than $15 billion sat in cash equivalents
at yearend, and "Cash never makes us happy" — though better burning a hole in Berkshire's pocket
than resting in someone else's. Nothing is on the horizon large enough to absorb it.

Once the General Re merger was certain, Berkshire asked the company to dispose of its equities;
General Re eliminated positions in about 250 common stocks and incurred $935 million of taxes
doing it.

> "We don't back into decisions." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

On disclosure, Buffett explains why 1997's unconventional positions were disclosed at all
(regulatory questions about silver; the size of the zero-coupon position; a wish to signal that
such commitments happen) and closes the door: absent another particularly large position, there
will be no updates on unconventional holdings. Certain 1997 positions have been eliminated and
others added, without specifics. The never-comment-even-if-untrue policy may disappoint
piggybackers but protects owners — and media speculation about Berkshire's moves continues in
most cases to be incorrect.

### Accounting, Part 1: options

The General Re merger exposed the flaw. The proxy's pro-forma statement showed compensation
expense increasing by $63 million, purely because Berkshire replaced General Re's longstanding
stock option plan with a cash plan tying incentive compensation to operating achievement. The two
plans have matching economics — the same rewards for the same performance — yet produce vastly
different accounting, because existing principles ignore the cost of options entirely.

The choice this creates is the indictment: pay employees in one form and count the cost, or pay
them in another form and ignore it. No wonder option use has mushroomed. Buffett allows that
properly structured options can be an appropriate and even ideal way to compensate top managers,
but says they are more often capricious in distributing rewards, inefficient as motivators, and
inordinately expensive for shareholders.

The test he offers is the GEICO advertising budget: suppose the $190 million of 1999 advertising
were paid not in cash but in ten-year, at-the-market Berkshire options — would anyone argue no
cost had been borne? He answers the standard objections directly. Employee options are sometimes
forfeited and carry a tax deduction on exercise, which publicly traded options do not; but
employee options are also often repriced, which makes them more costly than the public variety.
And non-transferability reduces an option's value to the employee without reducing its cost to
the employer, exactly as restricting a company car does.

When evaluating an option-issuing company Buffett subtracts what the company could have realized
by publicly selling options of like quantity and structure. Those revisions have frequently cut
reported per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not uncommon, and have on occasion changed a portfolio
decision. The three unanswered questions are restated: if options aren't a form of compensation,
what are they; if compensation isn't an expense, what is it; and if expenses shouldn't go into
the calculation of earnings, "where in the world should they go?"

### Accounting, Part 2: restructurings, and the golf scorecard

Buffett judges managements' behaviour worse still on restructurings and merger accounting, where
numbers are purposefully manipulated to deceive investors. He quotes Michael Kinsley on
Washington: "The scandal isn't in what's done that's illegal but rather in what's legal."

The diagnosis is a decline in probity rather than the emergence of obvious villains. In the late
1960s the games were visible and most admired companies shunned deception; now a growing number
of otherwise high-grade managers — the kind you would happily have as trustees under your will —
believe manipulating earnings to satisfy Wall Street is acceptable, and some believe it is a duty.
The premise underneath is that a CEO's job is to encourage the highest possible stock price, with
which Buffett adamantly disagrees. Once an everybody's-doing-it attitude takes hold, ethical
misgivings vanish:

> "Call this behavior Son of Gresham: Bad accounting drives out good." — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

The restructuring charge is the distortion of the day: costs properly attributable to several
years dumped into a single quarter already fated to disappoint, either cleaning up past
misrepresentation or preparing future ones. The illustration is the letter's best-known passage —
a golfer who fills his first card with atrocious numbers for a score of 140, declares a
restructuring of his swing, and charges every subsequent bad hole to that reserve, ending with
140, 80, 80, 80, 80 instead of 91, 94, 89, 94, 92. Wall Street ignores the 140 as a discontinued
swing and classifies him an 80 shooter who never disappoints. The "big bath" variant defers the
bad holes instead, banking praise for four 80s before turning in a 140 — without returning the
winnings. And the habit sticks, because fiddling the scorecard is easier than the practice tee:
"Once a philosopher, twice a pervert."

The property-casualty application is specific and matters for [[Applications/RedFlags]]: an
acquirer of a p-c company often simultaneously increases loss reserves, which may genuinely
reflect prior inadequacy — though it is uncanny how often such an actuarial revelation coincides
with the inking of a deal — and sets up earnings to flow in later as reserves are released.

> "If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting."
> — [[Sources/1998ltr]]

Berkshire has left loss reserve figures exactly as found in every acquisition, on the reasoning
that if liabilities must be raised immediately and substantially, either the acquired managers
were not knowledgeable or not honest — or the acquirer is laying groundwork for future
"earnings". The anecdote that lands it: one bank CEO asked whether a merger would mean a huge
charge, perhaps as much as $1 billion, and the veteran acquirer answered that they would make it
bigger than that — that being why they were doing the deal.

The scale: R. G. Associates of Baltimore counted no fewer than 1,369 special charges taken or
announced during 1998, totalling $72.1 billion, against 1997 earnings of $324 billion for the
entire Fortune 500. Auditors have not helped, tending to kowtow to the managers who choose them
and pay them — "Whose bread I eat, his song I sing." Buffett endorses SEC chairman Arthur
Levitt's campaign against earnings management, quoting his indictment that "Managing may be
giving way to manipulating; integrity may be losing out to illusion", and urges shareholders to
read the speech. See [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Reporting, contributions and the meeting

- **Reports** — two goals: give shareholders the information Buffett would want if positions were
  reversed, and make it accessible to everyone simultaneously, which the Internet greatly helps.
  Quarterlies now go only to those who request them. Levitt's attack on selective disclosure is
  endorsed: at Berkshire the holder of one B share is the equal of a large institution, and
  anyone seeking earnings guidance is directed to the public documents. Quarterly postings are
  set for May 14, August 13 and November 12, with the 1999 annual report on 11 March 2000. GEICO,
  Borsheim's, See's and The Buffalo News are all doing substantial business online, and annuity
  products have been added — developed by Ajit Jain, who Buffett says has personally accounted
  for a significant portion of Berkshire's operating earnings over the past decade.
- **Shareholder-designated contributions** — 97.5% of eligible shares participated, contributing
  $16.9 million; $130 million cumulatively over 18 years. Subsidiaries gave $12.5 million,
  including $2.0 million in kind.
- **The annual meeting** — May 1-3. Attendance passed 10,000 in 1998 and the shareholder list has
  since doubled, partly through the 60,000 shareholders gained in the Gen Re merger, so both
  Aksarben (about 14,000) and the Holiday Convention Centre (5,000) are booked. The NetJets 737
  Boeing Business Jet will be on display — 79 feet long, nearly 12 feet wide, 14-hour range, 19
  passengers, bedroom, office and two showers, with deliveries to fractional owners from the first
  quarter of 2000. [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] did $300 million of business in 1998.
  Borsheim's doubled its meeting-weekend sales and wrote 2,501 tickets in eight hours on the
  Sunday — one every 11½ seconds — under Susan Jacques, who joined in 1983 as a $4-per-hour
  salesperson and became CEO in 1994, which Buffett ranks among his best managerial decisions.
- **The corporate office** — 11.8 people, itemised by name and task: 6,106 shareholders designating
  3,880 charities; Kelly Muchemore processing about 17,500 admission tickets and 3,200 baseball
  tickets; Debbie Bosanek handling 46 media organizations, 13 of them non-U.S.; 165,000 copies of
  the annual report produced, rising to 325,000; Marc Hamburg handling 95% of the details of two
  major mergers.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **General Re Corp** — completed 21 December 1998 for $22 billion in stock. Brings 100% of the
  largest U.S. property-casualty reinsurer and 82% of Cologne Re, operations in 124 countries, and
  roughly two-thirds of Berkshire's now-$22.8 billion float. Bought to remove a constraint rather
  than to add a skill: Berkshire's balance sheet lets General Re retain volatility that a listed
  reinsurer must lay off.
- **Executive Jet Aviation** — $725 million, half cash and half stock, agreed after Rich Santulli
  called in May. Largest operator in fractional jet ownership, 163 aircraft, 1,000+ customers.

**Portfolio**

- American Express slightly increased; Coca-Cola and Gillette unchanged; many smaller positions
  trimmed or substantially cut.
- **McDonald's sold — named as a very big mistake.** Buffett states plainly that his 1998
  portfolio actions decreased the year's gain.
- General Re's own equity book liquidated at Berkshire's instruction: about 250 common stocks
  eliminated, $935 million of taxes incurred.
- More than $15 billion held in cash equivalents at yearend, with nothing on the horizon large
  enough to absorb it.

**Not done, and why**

- No major business acquisition beyond the two above, and no large new equity commitment. The
  search continues for something big enough to absorb the cash; markets are unfriendly to it.
- Look-through earnings not reported for 1998, because General Re closed too near yearend for
  either a historical or a pro-forma figure to mean anything.
- General Re's loss reserves left exactly as found, as in every prior insurance acquisition —
  a deliberate refusal of the industry's standard earnings-smoothing device.
