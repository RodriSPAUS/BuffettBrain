# 2023 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share market value grew 15.8% in 2023 against the S&P 500's 26.3% total return, extending the compounded annual gain since 1965 to 19.8%. This is the first letter written after Charlie Munger's death (November 28, 2023, 33 days before his 100th birthday), and it opens with a dedicated one-and-a-half-page tribute, Charlie Munger — The Architect of Berkshire Hathaway, before the regular shareholder letter begins. The letter also introduces a new device: Buffett writes the rest of the letter addressing his sister Bertie as a stand-in for the intelligent, trusting, non-expert individual shareholder he says he's always trying to reach. Both BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy delivered earnings disappointments in 2023 — Buffett calls the BHE shortfall, tied to wildfire liability exposure, a mistake he didn't anticipate — while insurance had a record year.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Charlie Munger, the Architect
Buffett's opening tribute recounts meeting Munger in 1959 at age 35 (despite both having grown up in Omaha), Munger's 1965 verdict that buying control of Berkshire had been "a dumb decision," and the advice that followed once the deed was done:

> "Warren, forget about ever buying another company like Berkshire. But now that you control Berkshire, add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices." [[Sources/2023ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

Buffett frames the relationship architecturally: "In reality, Charlie was the 'architect' of the present Berkshire, and I acted as the 'general contractor' to carry out the day-by-day construction of his vision." [[Sources/2023ltr]] He notes Munger never sought credit for his role and, even when certain he was right, let Buffett keep the reins.

### Meet Bertie: The Shareholder Buffett Writes For
Buffett introduces his sister Bertie as his mental model for the ideal Berkshire reader — smart, skeptical of pundits, financially literate but not an accountant, and a decades-long holder of Berkshire stock alongside her three daughters. The device replaces the more abstract "shareholder buckets" language of prior letters with a single, concrete stand-in whose likely questions guide what Buffett chooses to explain.

### Operating Earnings vs. GAAP "Net Income": Fact and Fiction
Buffett contrasts headline GAAP "net earnings (loss)" — $90 billion (2021), a $23 billion loss (2022), $96 billion (2023) — with Berkshire's preferred "operating earnings" figure — $27.6 billion (2021), $30.9 billion (2022), $37.4 billion (2023) — which excludes unrealized equity swings that can exceed $5 billion in a single day. He notes dryly that this was the standard approach until a 2018 rule mandated the change: "Galileo's experience, several centuries ago, should have taught us not to mess with mandates from on high. But, at Berkshire, we can be stubborn." [[Sources/2023ltr]] He also quotes 19th-century Comptroller of the Currency Hugh McCulloch on the limits of trusting counterparties:

> "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you." [[Sources/2023ltr]]

### Berkshire's Scale: Nearly 6% of the S&P 500's Combined Net Worth
Berkshire's year-end 2023 GAAP net worth of $561 billion compares to the other 499 S&P 500 companies' combined net worth of $8.9 trillion in 2022 (2023's full figure not yet tallied, but unlikely to exceed $9.5 trillion), meaning Berkshire alone represents nearly 6% of the net worth of the index it belongs to — a scale Buffett says makes "eye-popping performance" impossible going forward, since there are only a handful of U.S. companies left that could meaningfully move Berkshire's needle.

### Coca-Cola and American Express: Two Decades of Doing Nothing
Berkshire bought neither more nor fewer shares of Coca-Cola or American Express in 2023, extending what Buffett calls "our own Rip Van Winkle slumber that has now lasted well over two decades." [[Sources/2023ltr]] Berkshire's 2023 share of American Express earnings alone exceeded the original $1.3 billion purchase cost. His lesson: "When you find a truly wonderful business, stick with it." [[Sources/2023ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

### Occidental Petroleum and Five Japanese Trading Houses
Berkshire's stake in Occidental Petroleum grew to 27.8% of common shares by year-end, plus warrants allowing further increases at a fixed price for over five years; Buffett credits CEO Vicki Hollub's operating skill and frames Occidental's oil and carbon-capture position as being in the U.S. national interest, tracing America's shale-driven recovery from 5 million barrels of oil-equivalent per day in 2007 to over 13 million by 2023. Separately, Berkshire increased its stakes in all five of its Japanese trading-house holdings (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo) to about 9% each after Buffett and Greg Abel traveled to Tokyo to meet management; the position (cost ¥1.6 trillion, year-end market value ¥2.9 trillion) is financed mostly with ¥1.3 trillion of yen-denominated bonds — Buffett believes more than any other American company holds — producing a currency-driven GAAP gain of $1.9 billion recognized over 2020–2023, on top of an $8 billion unrealized dollar gain on the equity position itself.

### The Scorecard in 2023: BNSF and BHE Both Disappoint
Buffett's full-year operating-earnings breakdown (in millions) showed insurance underwriting $5,428 (2023) vs. -$30 (2022), insurance investment income $9,567 vs. $6,484, railroad $5,087 vs. $5,946, utilities and energy $2,331 vs. $3,904, other businesses $14,937 vs. $14,549, for total operating earnings of $37,350 vs. $30,853. He admits erring in his expectations for both major non-insurance businesses. At BNSF, capital spending in excess of GAAP depreciation has totaled $22 billion over 14 years of Berkshire ownership (more than $1.5 billion annually), meaning dividends to Berkshire will structurally lag reported earnings unless the railroad takes on more debt, which Buffett says it will not do. At BHE, wildfire liability exposure in a handful of states (with an actual bankruptcy at California's largest utility and a live threat in Hawaii) has broken the century-old regulatory compact of a fixed, dependable return on utility investment:

> "I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns and, along with Berkshire's two partners at BHE, I made a costly mistake in not doing so." [[Sources/2023ltr]]

### Insurance: A Record Year
Property-casualty insurance set records in sales, float, and underwriting profit in 2023, growing nearly 5,000-fold in volume since Berkshire's 1967 entry into the business (from $17 million to $83 billion). Buffett again credits Ajit Jain's 1986 arrival as pivotal, alongside the earlier "almost unbelievably wonderful experience with GEICO" dating to 1951.

### The Omaha Effect
Buffett closes with a lighthearted reflection on how many of Berkshire's central figures — himself, Munger, Ajit Jain (who lived a mile from Buffett's home before moving to New York), Greg Abel (who lived in Omaha in the 1990s without meeting Buffett), and Bertie herself — trace back to Omaha, alongside the fact that Berkshire itself relocated there from New England in 1970. He notes Bertie's own investing history: two decades of conventional bonds, a mutual fund, and active stock trading before, in 1980 at age 46, deciding independently to hold only Berkshire and her mutual fund and make no further trades for the next 43 years, becoming very wealthy despite substantial philanthropic giving along the way.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Per-share market value +15.8% versus the S&P 500's +26.3%
- **Operating earnings**: $37.4 billion, up from $30.9 billion in 2022
- **BNSF and BHE**: Both underperformed Buffett's own expectations; BHE wildfire liability exposure explicitly called a mistake in risk assessment
- **Insurance**: Record year in sales, float, and underwriting profit
- **Occidental Petroleum**: Stake grown to 27.8% of common shares plus long-dated warrants
- **Japanese trading houses**: All five holdings (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo) increased to about 9% each, financed mostly with yen-denominated debt
- **Coca-Cola and American Express**: No shares bought or sold for the second decade running
