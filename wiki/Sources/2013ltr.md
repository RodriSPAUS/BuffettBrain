# 2013 Annual Letter Summary

Book value per share gained 18.2% in 2013, and net worth rose $34.2 billion despite a $1.8 billion charge — meaningless economically, as Buffett explains — triggered by GAAP accounting rules when Berkshire bought out the minority interests in Marmon and Iscar. Berkshire's operating businesses (the Powerhouse Five: BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon and MidAmerican) earned $10.8 billion pre-tax, up $758 million from 2012, and the year's headline moves were the H. J. Heinz acquisition alongside 3G Capital and the NV Energy purchase. The letter's long essay on Some Thoughts About Investing lays out Buffett's case for productive assets, the low-cost S&P 500 index fund for non-professionals, and two small personal investments — a Nebraska farm and a New York retail property — used to illustrate his method.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Year at Berkshire: Heinz, NV Energy and the Powerhouse Five
Berkshire and 3G Capital (led by Jorge Paulo Lemann) jointly acquired H. J. Heinz — combined with the earlier NV Energy purchase (roughly $5.6 billion, via MidAmerican), the two deals put about $18 billion of Berkshire funds to work. Berkshire holds $8 billion of 9%-coupon Heinz preferred stock plus half of the $4.25 billion of common equity, with Bernardo Hees as CEO and Alex Behring as Chairman. Twenty-five bolt-on acquisitions for the existing businesses added a further $3.1 billion.

> "Our flexibility in capital allocation – our willingness to invest large sums passively in non-controlled businesses–gives us a significant advantage over companies that limit themselves to acquisitions they can operate. Woody Allen stated the general idea when he said: 'The advantage of being bi-sexual is that it doubles your chances for a date on Saturday night.'" [[Sources/2013ltr]]

The "Powerhouse Five" — BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon and MidAmerican — earned $10.8 billion pre-tax in 2013, and Buffett expects the group's earnings to grow further in 2014.

### The Marmon and Iscar Minority Buyouts (and the Recurring GAAP Charge)
Berkshire completed its purchase of the remaining minority stakes in both Marmon and Iscar during 2013 — Iscar via the Wertheimer family's exercise of a put option on their remaining 20%. As with the 2012 Marmon step-up, GAAP required Berkshire to record a paper write-off on the purchase even though the businesses' economics were unaffected, producing the year's $1.8 billion accounting charge.

### Insurance: An Eleventh Consecutive Underwriting Profit
Berkshire's insurers posted an underwriting profit for the eleventh straight year, and float grew to $77.24 billion at year-end 2013 from $41 billion eleven years earlier, with $22 billion of cumulative underwriting profit generated along the way.

> "Our investment in the insurance companies reflects a first major step in our efforts to achieve a more diversified base of earning power." — 1967 Annual Report, quoted as the section's epigraph [[Sources/2013ltr]]

[[Cases/GEICO]] passed Allstate to become the country's #2 auto insurer in 2013, in Tony Nicely's 52nd year running the company. Its 1995 acquisition goodwill of $1.4 billion has grown into economic goodwill Buffett estimates as:

> "As GEICO’s business grows, however, so does its true economic goodwill." Buffett estimates that figure now approaches $20 billion, up from the $1.4 billion of goodwill recorded at the 1995 acquisition. [[Sources/2013ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

Ajit Jain's reinsurance operation grew float toward $37 billion and launched Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance under Peter Eastwood. On the 2007 Lloyd's retroactive reinsurance deal (a $15 billion policy limit), Buffett quotes the client's own assessment:

> "No other insurer's promise would have given Lloyd's the comfort provided by its agreement with Berkshire. The CEO of the entity then handling Lloyd's claims said it best: 'Names [the original insurers at Lloyd's] wanted to sleep easy at night, and we think we've just bought them the world's best mattress.'" [[Sources/2013ltr]]

General Re, under Tad Montross, is restated as run on the same four insurance disciplines as Berkshire's other insurers; Buffett notes it briefly looked like "a huge mistake" after purchase but calls it "now a gem."

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses: BNSF and MidAmerican
The section opens with a 1999 epigraph, on the theme of regulatory restraints in the utility industry, anticipating exactly this kind of large regulated commitment:

> "It’s possible that we will make additional commitments in the field. If we do, the amounts involved could be large." — 1999 Annual Report [[Sources/2013ltr]]

BNSF and MidAmerican share long-lived, regulated assets funded partly by debt not guaranteed by Berkshire. MidAmerican's renewables build-out reached about 7% of U.S. wind generating capacity, with solar mostly still under construction, for a total renewables cost of $15 billion. Berkshire's utilities ranked #1 in customer satisfaction among 52 holding companies covering 101 utilities surveyed, with 95.3% of customers "very satisfied." BNSF's 2013 capital expenditures hit a single-year record of $4 billion, roughly double its depreciation charge. [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]] and [[Cases/BNSF]] are both discussed.

HomeServices, under Ron Peltier, grew agent count 38% to 22,114 following the Fox & Roach acquisition.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: Nebraska Furniture Mart's Texas Expansion
This section opens with an Adam Smith epigraph about Nebraska Furniture Mart, then reports the segment's summary balance sheet and earnings ($95.3 billion revenue, $6.7 billion pre-tax earnings in 2013, up from $6.1 billion in 2012). Buffett explains that the segment's non-GAAP operating-expense figures exclude purchase-accounting amortization he considers not a real economic cost:

> "Every dime of depreciation expense we report, however, is a real cost. And that's true at almost all other companies as well." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

Viewed as a single unit, this group earned 16.7% after-tax on the roughly $25 billion of net tangible assets it employed during 2013.

The bulk of the section is a tribute to [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]]'s new Texas store — 1.8 million square feet on a 433-acre site in The Colony, near Dallas, Berkshire's largest single retail commitment in the segment. Buffett retells the 1983 handshake purchase from Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B"), unaudited financials and all:

> "Mrs. B was 89 at the time and worked until 103 – definitely my kind of woman. Take a look at NFM's financial statements from 1946 on pages 116-117." [[Sources/2013ltr]] [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]]

Everything NFM now owns traces to $72,264 of 1946 net worth and $50 of cash, plus "the incredible talents of Mrs. B, her son, Louie, and his sons Ron and Irv" — a story Buffett has new students from 40 visiting universities begin their Omaha day with.

### Finance and Financial Products
Berkshire's smallest reporting segment: Clayton Homes (326,569 mortgages totaling $13.6 billion, and America's #1 homebuilder at 29,547 homes in 2013, about 4.7% of all U.S. single-family starts), the XTRA and CORT rental businesses, and the 50%-owned Berkadia Commercial Mortgage. Pre-tax earnings by unit (in millions): Berkadia $80, Clayton $416, CORT $40, XTRA $125, net financial income $324, for a segment total of $985 versus $848 in 2012.

### Investments: The Big Four, Bank of America, and a $873 Million Bond Mistake
The top-15 common stock table at year-end 2013 (cost / market value, in millions) included American Express ($1,287 / $13,756), Coca-Cola ($1,299 / $16,524), IBM ($11,681 / $12,778), Moody's ($248 / $1,936), Munich Re ($2,990 / $4,415), Procter & Gamble ($336 / $4,272), U.S. Bancorp ($3,002 / $3,883), Wal-Mart ($2,976 / $4,470) and Wells Fargo ($11,871 / $21,950), among others, plus an Others line of $11,281 / $19,894, for total common stocks carried at market of $56,581 / $117,505. The Bank of America warrants (700 million shares purchasable for $5 billion through September 2021, worth $10.9 billion at year-end) are described as, in effect, Berkshire's fifth-largest equity position.

Buffett recounts a rare bond loss on Energy Future Holdings, a 2007 leveraged buyout of Texas utility assets:

> "About $2 billion of the debt was purchased by Berkshire, pursuant to a decision I made without consultingwith Charlie. That was a big mistake." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

Berkshire sold the position in 2013 for $259 million after collecting $837 million in cash interest, for an overall pre-tax loss of $873 million; Buffett expected EFH to file for bankruptcy in 2014 absent a natural-gas price surge.

### Some Thoughts About Investing: The Farm, the NYU Property, and Ben Graham
The letter's major essay opens with two small personal investments used to illustrate business-like investing. In 1986 Buffett bought a 400-acre Nebraska farm from the FDIC for $280,000:

> "I needed no unusual knowledge or intelligence to conclude that the investment had no downside and potentially had substantial upside." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

In 1993 he joined a small group (including Larry Silverstein and Fred Rose) buying a retail property next to NYU from the Resolution Trust Corporation; annual distributions have since exceeded 35% of the original equity investment, with refinancings in 1996 and 1999 returning over 150% more. From these two examples Buffett draws his investing principles:

> "You don't need to be an expert in order to achieve satisfactory investment returns. But if you aren't, you must recognize your limitations and follow a course certain to work reasonably well." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

> "Focus on the future productivity of the asset you are considering. If youdon'tfeel comfortable making a rough estimate of the asset's future earnings, just forget it and move on." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

For non-professional investors, Buffett's advice is explicit and matches instructions in his own will (90% low-cost S&P 500 index fund, 10% short-term government bonds, naming Vanguard). He also credits Benjamin Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*, bought in 1949, as the book that changed his financial life — including its postscript revealing Graham's unnamed 1948 investment bonanza:

> "Ben made the purchase in 1948 when he was writing the first editionand–brace yourself–the mystery company was GEICO." [[Sources/2013ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

And Graham's famous closing formulation, quoted directly:

> "Price is what you pay, value is what you get." [[Sources/2013ltr]]

The essay also references a 1975 memo Buffett wrote to Katharine Graham of The Washington Post Company on the dangers of pension promises and investment policy, reproduced as an attachment to the letter, as a warning about looming public-pension problems.

### The Annual Meeting
Held Saturday, May 3, 2014 at the CenturyLink Center, with the usual newspaper-tossing contest, a new Berkshire movie, the question-and-answer session with journalists Carol Loomis, Becky Quick and Andrew Ross Sorkin plus analysts including Jay Gelb, shopping at Nebraska Furniture Mart and Borsheims, and side events with chess (Patrick Wolff), bridge (Bob Hamman and Sharon Osberg) and table tennis (Ariel Hsing). Buffett notes next year's letter (Berkshire's 50th) will review the company's history and look ahead to the next 50 years.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Book value per share +18.2%; net worth +$34.2 billion net of a $1.8 billion GAAP charge from the Marmon/Iscar minority buyouts
- **Acquisitions**: H. J. Heinz (with 3G Capital, ~$8B preferred + shared $4.25B common) and NV Energy (~$5.6B via MidAmerican), combined ~$18 billion deployed; 25 bolt-ons totaling $3.1 billion
- **Minority buyouts**: Full ownership of Marmon and Iscar completed, each triggering a GAAP write-off despite unchanged economics
- **Insurance**: Underwriting profit for the 11th consecutive year; float grew to $77.24 billion; GEICO passed Allstate to become the #2 U.S. auto insurer
- **Capital expenditure**: Record $11 billion companywide; BNSF alone spent a record $4 billion, about double depreciation
- **Big Four stakes increased**: Wells Fargo 9.2%, IBM 6.3%, Coca-Cola 9.1%, American Express 14.2%
- **Fixed income mistake**: Energy Future Holdings bonds sold for a net $873 million pre-tax loss
- **Personal investments as teaching examples**: 1986 Nebraska farm and 1993 NYU-adjacent retail property, both used to illustrate business-like, non-speculative investing
