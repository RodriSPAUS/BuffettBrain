# 1963 Partnership Letter Summary

`Raw/1963ltr.md` holds three documents: the **January 18, 1963 annual letter**
(full-year 1962 results, opening for the first time with "The Ground Rules"),
the **July 10, 1963 mid-year letter**, and the **November 6, 1963 letter to
partners for 1964**, which reports the sale of [[Cases/DempsterMill]]'s
operating assets. Together they close the Dempster story and put in writing,
for the first time, the seven axioms Buffett asks every partner to actually
believe before signing the agreement.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Ground Rules — axioms stated before the year, not after

Buffett opens with seven numbered points partners must accept "mentally and
viscerally," because retrospective judgment is worthless:

> "To most of you this material will seem unduly repetitious, but I would rather have nine partners out of ten mildly bored than have one out of ten with any basic misconceptions." [[Sources/1963ltr]]

> "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is not to be measured by whether we are plus or minus for the year. It is instead to be measured against the general experience in securities as measured by the Dow-Jones Industrial Average, leading investment companies, etc. If our record is better than that of these yardsticks, we consider it a good year whether we are plus or minus. If we do poorer, we deserve the tomatoes." [[Sources/1963ltr]]

Two more points fix the terms for good: no guaranteed return (monthly payments
are withdrawals, covered by earnings only above 6%), and a minimum three-year
test — "an exception to the latter statement would be three years covering a
speculative explosion in a bull market."

### 1962 in the numbers — the ceiling on the goal itself

1962: Dow −7.6%, partnership +13.9%, limited partners +11.9%. Compounded
1957–1962: Dow 61.6%, partnership 299.8%, limited partners 215.1% (annual
rates 8.3% / 26.0% / 21.1%). Buffett immediately discounts his own record:

> "My (unscientific) opinion is that a margin of ten percentage points per annum over the Dow is the very maximum that can be achieved with invested funds over any long period of years, so it may be well to mentally modify some of the above figures." [[Sources/1963ltr]]


Combined limited-partnership assets had grown from $303,726 at the start of
1957 to $7,178,500 at the start of 1962 with "no tendency to narrow" in the
margin over the Dow as funds increased.

### The Joys of Compounding — Columbus and Isabella

The first of the recurring compounding essays: $30,000 backing Columbus's
voyage, compounded at 4% since 1492, "would have amounted to something like
$2,000,000,000,000 (that's $2 trillion...) by 1962." The point is the same one
made with a table of $100,000 compounded at 5%, 10% and 15% over 10, 20 and 30
years — small rate differences compound into very large sums.

### Coattail riding, and the year the generals lost money

The three-category method (generals / work-outs / controls) is restated, with
a new admission of where "generals" sometimes comes from:

> "Many times generals represent a form of 'coattail riding' where we feel the dominating stockholder group has plans for the conversion of unprofitable or under-utilized assets to a better use." [[Sources/1963ltr]]

He names the practice in his own portfolio directly afterward, citing Sanborn
and Dempster as cases where the partnership itself rode a dominating
stockholder's coattails — while adding that, everything else equal, he would
rather let someone else do the work. [[Cases/SanbornMap]] [[Cases/DempsterMill]]

1962 itself inverted the usual pattern: generals lost money outright while
work-outs and Dempster carried the year — "not only did we not make any money
out of our general category, but I am even doubtful if it did better than the
Dow." Short sales of about $340,000, tied to one late-year work-out, were used
specifically to strip out general-market risk from that position.

### Dempster Mill — the cornerstone stated as a rule

The full case is told as "The high point of 1962 from a performance standpoint
was our present control situation --73% owned Dempster Mill." Harry
Bottle's turnaround (inventory cut, overhead cut, branches closed, pricing
fixed) is laid out point by point against comparative
balance sheets for November 1961 and November 1962. The lesson is generalized
into the philosophy's defining sentence:

> "This is the cornerstone of our investment philosophy: 'Never count on making a good sale. Have the purchase price be so attractive that even a mediocre sale gives good results. The better sales will be the frosting on the cake.'" [[Sources/1963ltr]] [[Cases/DempsterMill]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

On January 2, 1963, Dempster received a $1,250,000 unsecured term loan,
freeing up a securities portfolio worth more than the whole company had
originally cost.

### Conservatism — the honest exception

Repeating the conservatism defense from 1961–62, but with 1962's own numbers
left in rather than smoothed over:

> "In 1962, we did realize a loss on one commitment or 1.0% and our ratio or realized gains to losses was only slightly over 3 to 1." [[Sources/1963ltr]]
>
> ("or" is `Raw/1963ltr.md`'s own extraction artifact for "of," quoted verbatim)

### The Usual Prediction — the Dow's expected return revised down

The ten-year framework returns with the Dow's expected compounding trimmed
from "5% to 7%" to a flat "5% per year," the ten-point margin unchanged, and
the implied hope trimmed correspondingly from 15–17% to "15% per year."

### Mid-year and Dempster's sale — the story closes

The July 10 letter reports a first half of +14% against the Dow's +10.0%,
weaker in relative terms than the −7.5%-vs−21.7% first half of 1962 — the
point being that the advantage is structurally a down-market advantage, not a
constant one. Generals alone gained roughly 21% on an average net investment of
about $5,275,000. Dempster's per-share value climbed again, to $64.81 by June
30, 1963, and Buffett offers the plainest possible accounting of the profit
already locked in:

> "B.P.L. owns 71.7% of Dempster acquired at a cost of $1,262,577.27. On June 30, 1963 Dempster had a small safe deposit box at the Omaha National Bank containing securities worth $2,028,415.25. Our 71.7% share of $2,028,415.25 amounts to $1,454,373.70. Thus, everything above ground (and part of it underground) is profit." [[Sources/1963ltr]] [[Cases/DempsterMill]]

The November 6 letter reports the ending directly: "A satisfactory sale on a
going concern basis of Dempster Mill Manufacturing operating assets was made
about a month ago" — the company, later renamed First Beatrice Corp., left
holding cash and marketable securities (the harder-fought story behind that
one sentence — deals falling through at advanced stages before this one
closed — is told in full in the next annual letter [[Sources/1964ltr]]).
Year-to-October results (partnership +32%, limited partners about +25.5%) are
explicitly flagged as abnormal: "I do not consider a 13.2 percentage point
margin to be in the cards on a long term basis."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Dempster Mill Manufacturing — sold.** Operating assets sold on a going-concern basis around October 1963, close to book value; combined with the securities portfolio gain, realization of about $80 per share. Renamed First Beatrice Corp., left holding mostly cash and marketable securities. See [[Cases/DempsterMill]].
- **Results (full year 1962):** partnership +13.9%, limited partners +11.9%, against the Dow's −7.6%. Compounded 1957–62: 299.8% / 215.1% vs 61.6%.
- **Results (H1 1963):** partnership +14% (excluding Dempster revaluation) against the Dow's +10.0%.
- **Structure:** net assets at the start of 1963 were $9,405,400; Dempster received a $1,250,000 unsecured term loan on January 2, 1963; office moved that year "from the office off the bedroom to one a bit... more conventional."
