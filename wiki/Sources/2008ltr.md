# 2008 Annual Letter

Book value per share fell 9.6% — the worst year in the 44-year table for both
Berkshire and the S&P 500, which fell 37.0%. Buffett narrates the crisis
directly: a "paralyzing fear" gripping the country, the Treasury and Fed going
"all in," and the watchword becoming a creed he remembers from restaurant
walls in his youth, "In God we trust; all others pay cash." Insurance and the
utility business, uncorrelated to the general economy, carried Berkshire
through; Buffett's own investment decisions did not — he confesses a "major
mistake of commission" in ConocoPhillips and smaller ones in two Irish banks,
both told without blaming anyone else. The letter's longest technical section
is derivatives: 251 personally-managed contracts, a rebuttal of Black-Scholes
at long duration, and the Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac regulatory failure told as a
case study in why disclosure alone cannot fix complexity.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The worst year, narrated without euphemism

By the fourth quarter, Buffett writes, the credit crisis and tumbling home and
stock prices had produced a paralyzing fear that gripped the country, and the
Treasury and the Fed had gone "all in." The Depression-era creed he remembers
from restaurant walls in his youth became the watchword:

> "In God we trust; all others pay cash." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Even inside that narrative, the letter restates the long-run case for
American business, citing the 20th century's near-sevenfold rise in living
standards through two world wars, a dozen recessions and the Depression, and
concludes that the economic system has "worked extraordinarily" well over
time — [[Sources/2008ltr]]. Four standing goals are restated as what guide
Berkshire in good years and bad: a Gibraltar-like financial position, widening
the moats around operating businesses, acquiring new earnings streams, and
expanding the roster of outstanding managers.

### Yardsticks: both buckets fell

Per-share investments fell from $90,343 to $77,793 — a decline from market
prices, not net selling. Non-insurance pre-tax earnings per share fell more
modestly, from $4,093 to $3,921. Buffett does not soften the verdict: both
results, he writes, were unsatisfactory.

He restates that the earnings segment, not the investment segment, has been
Berkshire's real focus for decades:

> "We like buying underpriced securities," — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

but he likes buying fairly-priced operating businesses even more.

### Capital allocation: the tailwind of disarray

Pessimism, Buffett writes, is the investor's friend; euphoria is the enemy.
Three large insurance-portfolio investments made on crisis terms were
expected to add about $1.5 billion pre-tax annually. Marmon's ownership
reached 64%, en route to full ownership within six years per the formula from
[[Sources/2007ltr]]. Buffett restates Ben Graham's foundational distinction as
the frame for buying into the downturn:

> "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what
> you get.'" — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Whether talking about socks or stocks, Buffett writes, he likes buying quality
merchandise when it is marked down.

### Regulated Utility Business: wind, pipelines, and a satire on private equity

MidAmerican's diluted interest reached 87.4%. Two operating results stand
out: Kern River and Northern Natural pipelines rose from 9th and 39th (of 44
rated) in Mastio's customer-satisfaction rankings to 1st and 3rd, and Iowa
became the top state in the country for wind as a share of generation
capacity, rising from zero to nearly 20% since MidAmerican's purchase; at
PacifiCorp, wind capacity grew from 33 to 794 megawatts. MidAmerican has paid
no dividend to Berkshire since the 2000 purchase, reinvesting instead:

> "In our utility business, we spend all we earn, and then some," — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

in order to meet the needs of Berkshire's service areas.

Buffett contrasts Berkshire's approach to being a "buyer of choice" with
private-equity practice, arguing the industry rebranded itself without
changing its substance:

> "Some years back our competitors were known as 'leveraged-buyout
> operators.' But LBO became a bad name." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

In Buffett's telling, the buyout firms simply renamed themselves without
changing their practices:

> "Their new label became 'private equity,' a name that turns the facts
> upside-down: A purchase of a business by these firms almost invariably
> results in dramatic reductions in the equity portion of the acquiree's
> capital structure compared to that previously existing." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

He extends the "buyer of choice" idea to a category with no private owners at
all — utility regulators, who check a prospective buyer's record with
regulators in every other state it already operates in. See
[[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Insurance: outperforming an industry with the wind against it

> "During the 25 years ending in 2007, return on net worth for insurers
> averaged 8.5% versus 14.0% for the Fortune 500." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

GEICO's market share rose to 7.7% from 2.0% when Tony Nicely became CEO in
1993, moving GEICO into third place among auto insurers, trailing only State
Farm and Allstate. Productivity nearly doubled in five years — 299 policies
per employee to 439 — and January 2009 set a monthly policyholder-growth
record that Buffett expected February to beat before the letter even went to
press. The opportunity is described without restraint:

> "As we view GEICO's current opportunities, Tony and I feel like two hungry
> mosquitoes in a nudist camp." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Motorcycle share rose from about 6% to over 7% on 23.4% growth in policies,
with RV, ATV and, as of the prior year, commercial auto lines also expanding.
See [[Cases/GEICO]].

Leadership changed at General Re: Joe Brandon, who had spent six years
restoring discipline after the 1998 merger's undetected problems, stepped
down as CEO in spring 2008, with Tad Montross taking over. Buffett credits
Ben Franklin for the line that captures why an AAA-backed reinsurer matters
in a crisis year: "It's difficult for an empty sack to stand upright."

Ajit Jain's 31-person Stamford operation is described as one of the most
remarkable and hardest-to-characterize businesses Buffett knows, and its
first-half year work included activating **Berkshire Hathaway Assurance
Company (BHAC)**, a new tax-exempt bond insurer. Early in 2008 Berkshire
offered to assume all tax-exempt guarantees from the three largest troubled
monoline insurers at a 1.5% rate on about $822 billion of bonds; the offer
was rejected — Buffett later concluded he had "severely underpriced" it —
after which Berkshire instead wrote about $15.6 billion of secondary-market
"second-to-pay" insurance at rates averaging 3.3%, more than double what the
rejected first-to-pay offer would have earned. Buffett explains the mechanism
by which a formerly cautious, low-risk insurance line degenerates: rate
competition pushed monoline managers "to ever-riskier propositions,"
eventually including residential mortgage guarantees.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: Iscar buys Tungaloy

Iscar's late-November purchase of Tungaloy, a leading Japanese toolmaker, is
the year's notable tuck-in. Buffett's tribute to Iscar's three-manager team —
Eitan Wertheimer, Jacob Harpaz and Danny Goldman — measures the acquisition's
real scarcity value:

Securing even one manager of that caliber in an acquisition is a blessing, he
writes:

> "Getting three is like winning the Triple Crown." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

CTB, bought for $140 million in 2002, earned $89 million pre-tax in 2008
after six further tuck-in acquisitions under CEO Vic Mancinelli, whom Buffett
credits with "blocking and tackling" fundamentals rather than any single
dramatic move.

### Finance: the Clayton mortgage lesson, told for public-policy purposes

Buffett states his purpose directly — this section exists for the housing
policy debate, not just the shareholder report. Manufactured-housing
"borrowers who shouldn't have borrowed" being financed by "lenders who
shouldn't have lent" in the late 1990s is presented as the exact rehearsal for
what happened to conventional housing in 2004-07, down to the same excuse:

> "It was Scarlett O'Hara all over again: 'I'll think about it tomorrow.'"
> — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Clayton's own borrowers — median FICO score 644 against a 723 national
median, 35% in the "sub-prime" band — nonetheless kept paying at rates far
better than the broader market, with foreclosures actually falling from 5.3%
(2004) to 3.0% (2008). Buffett's explanation is behavioral rather than
statistical:

> "Simply put, they took out a mortgage with the intention of paying it off,
> whatever the course of home prices." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

> "They did not sign up for 'teaser' rates that upon reset were outsized
> relative to their income. And they did not assume that they could always
> sell their home at a profit if their mortgage payments became onerous."
> — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

The policy lesson he draws is a floor, not a ceiling: "Home purchases should
involve an honest-to-God down payment of at least 10% and monthly payments
that can be comfortably handled by the borrower's income." A separate and
unrelated threat to Clayton's lending is named too — government-guaranteed
competitors now borrow at costs even AAA-rated Berkshire cannot match,
inverting who can lend profitably. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]] for
the parallel argument about earned trust with regulators.

Moody's and Burlington Northern Santa Fe crossed the 20% ownership threshold
during the year and moved to equity-method accounting instead.

> "I told you in an earlier part of this report that last year I made a major
> mistake of commission (and maybe more; this one sticks out). Without urging
> from Charlie or anyone else, I bought a large amount of ConocoPhillips stock
> when oil and gas prices were near their peak." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

> "So far I have been dead wrong." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

A smaller, equally unforced pair of errors: $244 million spent on two Irish
banks that "appeared cheap," written down to $27 million by yearend — an 89%
loss, and one that deepened afterward. On the other side of the ledger,
Berkshire committed $14.5 billion to Wrigley, Goldman Sachs and General
Electric fixed-income securities carrying high current yields plus equity
participation, funded partly by selling holdings Buffett would have preferred
to keep (Johnson & Johnson, Procter & Gamble, ConocoPhillips) rather than let
cash run thin:

> "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet
> tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a
> night's sleep for the chance of extra profits." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

He closes the investment section with a warning about the crowd seeking
safety in cash at the bottom:

> "They regard their judgment confirmed when they hear commentators proclaim
> 'cash is king,'" — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

even though that cash was earning close to nothing and would surely see its
purchasing power eroded over time.

### Derivatives: 251 contracts, and a rebuttal of Black-Scholes

Buffett restates the derivatives danger in the starkest terms yet, tying
Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's accounting failures directly to instrument
complexity:

Derivatives, he writes, allowed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to engage in
massive misstatements of earnings for years.

The OFHEO case study is damning by its own paper trail: created by Congress
in 1992 specifically to oversee the two companies, its 2003 annual report —
filed nine days after Freddie's CEO and CFO resigned in disgrace — still
concluded "Both Enterprises were financially sound and well managed." Buffett
draws the general lesson for regulation of complexity directly:

> "Auditors can't audit these contracts, and regulators can't regulate"
> such instruments. — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Bear Stearns is cited as the live counterparty-risk case, quoting Fed
official Tim Geithner's justification for the rescue — that the Fed had
stepped in to avoid a financial chain reaction of unpredictable magnitude —
and endorsing it: "In my opinion, the Fed was right to do so."

The structural warning generalizes past any one firm: ambitious CEOs who pile
on leverage and run large, unfathomable derivatives books face what Buffett
calls The First Law of Corporate Survival —

> "Modest incompetence simply won't do..." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Berkshire's own 251 personally-managed derivative contracts (excluding
MidAmerican's operational ones and Gen Re's remaining runoff) fall into four
categories: long-dated equity index puts across four indices ($37.1 billion
notional, $4.9 billion of premiums received, expirations 2019-2028); five-year
high-yield credit-default contracts ($3.4 billion of premiums, $542 million
of losses paid by yearend); newly-written single-company credit default
swaps ($4 billion covering 42 corporations); and tax-exempt bond derivatives
structured like BHAC's insurance but subject to mark-to-market rather than
accrual accounting. Every category shares one structural advantage:

> "Berkshire therefore always holds the money, which leaves us assuming no
> meaningful counterparty risk." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Buffett then devotes several pages to a rebuttal of Black-Scholes at extreme
duration, working a hypothetical 100-year, $1 billion S&P put priced by the
formula at $2.5 million:

> "Considering everything, I believe the probability of a decline in the
> index over a one-hundred-year period to be far less than 1%." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

> "Would you like to borrow money for 100 years at a 0.7% rate?" — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

The diagnosis is that historical volatility, the formula's key input, is a
reasonable proxy for short-dated options but meaningless over a century, when
retained earnings alone should push valuations far higher regardless of past
price swings. Buffett nonetheless commits to using the formula for financial
reporting, distrusting any self-serving alternative more:

> "Our advice: Beware of geeks bearing formulas." — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

> "CEOs who have concocted their own valuations for esoteric financial
> instruments have seldom erred" toward conservatism, he adds.
> — [[Sources/2008ltr]]

Buffett states plainly that he and Charlie have no desire to join that club
of optimists.

warnings this letter's crisis narrative confirms.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Tungaloy** (via Iscar) — leading Japanese toolmaker, closed late November.
- Marmon ownership increased to 64%, en route to full ownership by formula.
- Tuck-ins at MiTek, Benjamin Moore, Acme Brick, Forest River, Marmon and CTB.
- Three large opportunistic insurance-portfolio investments expected to add
  about $1.5 billion pre-tax annually.
- $14.5 billion in Wrigley, Goldman Sachs and General Electric fixed-income
  securities with equity participation, funded partly by selling Johnson &
  Johnson, Procter & Gamble and ConocoPhillips shares.

**Mistakes confessed**

- ConocoPhillips — bought heavily near the energy-price peak; named as a
  "major mistake of commission."
- Two Irish bank stocks — $244 million invested, written down to $27 million
  (89% loss) by yearend.

**Insurance**

- Activated Berkshire Hathaway Assurance Company (BHAC); wrote about $15.6
  billion of secondary tax-exempt bond insurance at average 3.3% rates after
  a rejected $822 billion primary offer at 1.5%.
- Float grew slightly to $58.488 billion; underwriting profit of $2.792
  billion, the sixth consecutive profitable year.
- Wrote $4 billion of new single-company credit-default swaps across 42
  corporations.

**Utilities**

- MidAmerican spent $1.8 billion on wind generation in 2008 alone, becoming
  the largest owner of regulated-utility wind capacity in the country.
