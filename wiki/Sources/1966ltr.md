# 1966 Annual Letter Summary

`Raw/1966ltr.md` holds two documents: the **January 20, 1966 annual letter**
(full-year 1965 results, and the fullest statement yet of concentration and
scale) and the **July 12, 1966 mid-year letter** (the Hochschild, Kohn & Co.
acquisition — the partnership's first negotiated purchase of a whole private
business). Between them they give the full origin story of Berkshire
Hathaway, close the partnership to new outside capital, and write out the
40%-concentration Ground Rule in full for the first time.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### 1965 — the widest margin in the partnership's history, and a joke at his own expense

> "Our War on Poverty was successful in 1965." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Partnership +47.2% against the Dow's +14.2% — the widest margin yet, against
a forecast made a year earlier ("10 percentage points worse... to 25
percentage points better") that this result blew straight through:

> "Naturally, no writer likes to be publicly humiliated by such a mistake." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Compounded 1957–1965: Dow 164.1%, partnership 943.2%, limited partners 588.5%
(annual rates 11.4% / 29.8% / 23.9%).

### Berkshire Hathaway — the full story, told for the first time

Buffett gives the complete account promised in the November 1965 letter.
Purchases began in 1962 as a "Generals-Private Owner" position:

> "Our purchases of Berkshire started at a price of $7.60 per share in 1962." [[Sources/1966ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

The average cost across accumulation was $14.86, reflecting heavy buying in
early 1965 when control was actually assumed. The company had shrunk from 11
textile mills and about $29.5 million pre-tax earnings at its 1948 peak to
two mills and about 2,300 employees by the time BPL took control — but the
remaining management was, to Buffett's pleasant surprise, first-class, with
Ken Chace running the operation without a single outside hire needed. Because
it is a controlled business, not a stock, valuation follows the same
fiduciary principle already fixed for [[Cases/DempsterMill]]:

> "We would not be a seller of our holdings at such a figure, but neither would we be a seller of the other items in our portfolio at yearend valuations" — the reason being, plainly, that otherwise they would already have sold them. [[Sources/1966ltr]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

> "Berkshire is a delight to own." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

### The Sorrows of Compounding — closing the partnership to new money

For the first time, more capital is treated as a threat rather than an
opportunity. Beginning capital had reached $43,645,000, and Buffett judges
the partnership close to the point of diminishing returns:

> "I now feel that we are much closer to the point where increased size may prove disadvantageous." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

> "Therefore, unless it appears that circumstances have changed (under some conditions added capital would improve results) or unless new partners can bring some asset to the Partnership other than simply capital, I intend to admit no additional partners to BPL." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Existing partners may still add capital; the restriction is on new outside
money only.

### Diversification — Ground Rule 7, written out in full

The concentration policy flagged but not quoted in the November 1965 letter
is now given verbatim, with its own justification:

> "We diversify substantially less than most investment operations." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

> "We might invest up to 40% of our net worth in a single security under conditions coupling an extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct with a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value of the investment." [[Sources/1966ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

Buffett explains the reasoning as trading a smoother year-to-year ride for a
better long-term average:

> "Our margin versus the Dow has ranged from 2.4 percentage points in 1958 to 33.0 points in 1965." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Only five or six positions had ever exceeded 25% of assets. The summary
line for the whole argument against the conventional wisdom of broad
diversification:

> "Hence, for our summation on overdiversification, we turn to that eminent academician Billy Rose, who says, 'You've got a harem of seventy girls; you don't get to know any of them very well.'" [[Sources/1966ltr]]

### Hochschild, Kohn & Co. — the first negotiated whole-business purchase

The July letter reports the first time the partnership bought an entire
private business by direct negotiation rather than through the market:

> "During the first half we, and two 10% partners, purchased all of the stock of Hochschild, Kohn & Co., a privately owned Baltimore department store." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Buffett is explicit the underlying discipline did not change — quantitative
and qualitative factors weighed against price, same as any marketable
security — and downplays the deal's news value relative to unnamed larger
holdings already in the portfolio:

> "I still prefer the iceberg approach toward investment disclosure." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

### Market forecasting — the discipline restated against real pressure

After the Dow fell from 995 to about 865 between February and May 1966,
partners called asking whether to get out. Buffett's answer, echoing Ground
Rule 6, points to Benjamin Graham directly:

> "A marvelous articulation of this idea is contained in chapter two (The Investor and Stock Market Fluctuations) of Benjamin Graham's 'The Intelligent Investor.'" [[Sources/1966ltr]] [[People/BenGraham]]

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Berkshire Hathaway — full origin story.** Bought from $7.60/share (1962), average cost $14.86 (heavy 1965 buying), control assumed spring 1965. Two mills, ~2,300 employees remaining from an 11-mill, ~$29.5M pre-tax-earnings 1948 peak. Valued as a business, not a stock.
- **Hochschild, Kohn & Co. — acquired.** Baltimore department store, entire stock purchased by negotiation with two 10% co-investors — the first whole-business negotiated deal in partnership history.
- **New partners — closed.** No new outside capital accepted from this letter forward; existing partners may still add.
- **Results (full year 1965):** partnership +47.2%, limited partners +36.9%, against the Dow's +14.2% — the widest margin in the partnership's history. Compounded 1957–65: 943.2% / 588.5% vs 164.1%.
- **Results (H1 1966):** partnership +8.2% against the Dow's −8.7%.
