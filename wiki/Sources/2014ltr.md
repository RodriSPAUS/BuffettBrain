# 2014 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share book value grew 8.3% in 2014 (net worth +$18.3 billion), trailing the S&P 500's 13.7% total return, and per-share book value has compounded at 19.4% annually over the full 50 years since the Buffett/Munger management took over in 1965. This is the Golden Anniversary letter: after the regular business report, Buffett contributes a long essay, Berkshire — Past, Present and Future, covering the textile-mill origins, his early mistakes, Charlie Munger's influence, the case for the conglomerate structure, and his outlook for Berkshire's next 50 years — and Munger, for the first time, adds his own companion essay, Vice Chairman's Thoughts — Past and Future, analyzing why the Berkshire system worked. Buffett states neither read the other's essay before publication.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Year at Berkshire: Powerhouse Five, BNSF Problems, and Van Tuyl
The "Powerhouse Five" (Berkshire Hathaway Energy, BNSF, IMC/Iscar, Lubrizol and Marmon) earned a record $12.4 billion pre-tax, up $1.6 billion from 2013. The year's one piece of bad news was BNSF: the railroad disappointed many customers on service, prompting a record $6 billion 2015 capital-expenditure plan (about 26% of revenues, far above the 2009–2013 average of 18%). Insurance operated at an underwriting profit for a 12th consecutive year, with float growing from $41 billion to $84 billion over that stretch and $24 billion of cumulative underwriting profit. Thirty-one bolt-on acquisitions were contracted for $7.8 billion, the largest being Duracell (to close later in 2015, placed under Marmon). Berkshire agreed to buy Van Tuyl Automotive, a 78-dealership group and the country's fifth-largest automotive group, described by Buffett as making Berkshire "car guys." With Van Tuyl, Berkshire owned 9½ Fortune 500-equivalent companies (Heinz counted as the ½, reflecting the 3G Capital partnership).

> "Our flexibility in capital allocation – our willingness to invest large sums passively in non-controlled businesses–gives us a significant advantage over companies that limit themselves to acquisitions they can operate." [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]

Berkshire increased its stakes in all of the "Big Four" — American Express (to 14.8%), Coca-Cola (9.2%), IBM (7.8%) and Wells Fargo (9.4%) — with unrealized gains on the four totaling $42 billion at year-end.

### Intrinsic Business Value
Per-share investments rose 8.4% to $140,123, and per-share pre-tax earnings from businesses other than insurance and investments rose 19% to $10,847. Since 1970, per-share investments have compounded at 19% annually and the earnings figure at 20.6%, tracking closely with Berkshire's long-run stock-price appreciation.

### Insurance: Twelve Years of Underwriting Profit and the Nature of Float
Berkshire's origin in insurance dates to the 1967 purchase of National Indemnity and National Fire & Marine for $8.6 million from Jack Ringwalt — a deal closed in fifteen minutes with no audit and no lawyers. Float grew from $39 million in 1970 to $83.921 billion in 2014:

| Year | Float ($M) |
| --- | --- |
| 1970 | $39 |
| 1980 | 237 |
| 1990 | 1,632 |
| 2000 | 27,871 |
| 2010 | 65,832 |
| 2014 | 83,921 |

Buffett explains why float is not simply a liability:

> "If our revolving float is both costless and long-enduring, which I believe it will be, the true value of this liability is dramatically less than the accounting liability." [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[Concepts/Float]]

The 2014 underwriting-profit and float table by segment (in millions):

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BH Reinsurance | $606 | $1,294 | $42,454 | $37,231 |
| General Re | 277 | 283 | 19,280 | 20,013 |
| GEICO | 1,159 | 1,127 | 13,569 | 12,566 |
| Other Primary | 626 | 385 | 8,618 | 7,430 |

Ajit Jain's reinsurance operation grew to the largest float pool and launched Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance under Peter Eastwood; General Re, under Tad Montross, is described as observing all "four insurance commandments" Buffett lists (understand exposures, price them conservatively, charge an adequate premium, and be willing to walk away). [[Cases/GEICO]], run by Tony Nicely for 53 years by 2014, grew its market share from 2.5% in 1995 to 10.8% by 2014.

Buffett credits GEICO's low-cost structure as the factor letting it "gobble up market share year after year". He calls it a moat competitors cannot cross. [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]] [[Concepts/Moat]]

Berkshire wrote a $3 billion single premium policy in 2014, second in size only to its $7.1 billion premium 2007 Lloyd's transaction; Buffett notes all eight P/C policies in history carrying a premium above $1 billion were written by Berkshire.

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses
BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (renamed from MidAmerican) share heavy investment in long-lived regulated assets funded by debt not guaranteed by Berkshire. BHE completed a $3 billion (Canadian) acquisition of AltaLink, an Alberta electric transmission system, and by 2014 accounted for 6% of the country's wind generation capacity and 7% of its solar capacity, alongside pipelines carrying 8% of U.S. natural gas consumption and utility operations in the U.K. and Philippines. BNSF's 2014 revenue was $23.239 billion versus $22.014 billion in 2013, but service problems caused it to lose market share to Union Pacific and post lower earnings growth, prompting the record 2015 capex plan discussed above. [[Cases/BNSF]] and [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]] are both covered.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing
This segment's non-GAAP earnings statement showed $97.689 billion of revenue and $6.792 billion pre-tax earnings for 2014 (up from $6.16 billion in 2013, restated to exclude Marmon's leasing operations, which moved to Finance and Financial Products). As in prior letters, Buffett distinguishes real depreciation from non-cash purchase-accounting amortization:

> "Depreciation charges, we want to emphasize, are different: Every dime of depreciation expense we report is a real cost." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

Viewed as a single unit, the segment earned 18.7% after-tax on about $24 billion of average net tangible assets employed during 2014.

### Finance and Financial Products
Marmon's railcar and container/crane leasing operations were folded into this section for the first time in 2014 (with 2012–2013 restated to match), alongside CORT, XTRA, and Clayton Homes. Clayton, under Kevin Clayton, sold 30,871 homes in 2014 — about 45% of all manufactured homes bought by Americans, up from a 14% share when Berkshire bought Clayton in 2003 for $1.7 billion. Marmon's 105,000-car railcar fleet is carried below its economic value because Berkshire manufactures its own tank cars and books no profit on the internal transfer. Segment pre-tax earnings recap (in millions): Berkadia $122, Clayton $558, CORT $36, Marmon Containers and Cranes $238, Marmon Railcars $442, XTRA $147, net financial income $296, for a total of $1,839 versus $1,564 in 2013.

### Investments: The Big Four, Bank of America, and the Tesco Mistake
The top-15 common stock table at year-end 2014 (cost / market value, in millions) included American Express ($1,287 / $14,106), Coca-Cola ($1,299 / $16,888), IBM ($13,157 / $12,349), Moody's ($248 / $2,364), Munich Re ($2,990 / $4,023), U.S. Bancorp ($3,033 / $4,355), Wal-Mart ($3,798 / $5,815) and Wells Fargo ($11,871 / $26,504), among others, plus Others of $10,180 / $15,704, for a total of $55,056 / $117,470. The Bank of America warrants (700 million shares purchasable for $5 billion through September 2021) were worth $12.5 billion at year-end, described as Berkshire's fourth-largest equity position.

Buffett candidly discusses a mistake selling too slowly out of Tesco, the U.K. grocer:

> "Attentive readers will notice that Tesco, which last year appeared in the list of our largest common stock investments, is now absent." [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

Berkshire had owned 415 million Tesco shares (cost $2.3 billion) at the end of 2012, sold some in 2013 as management concerns grew, then sold the rest during 2014 as Tesco's problems worsened, for an overall after-tax loss of $444 million — about 1/5 of 1% of net worth, and one of only a handful of investment losses in 50 years to cost Berkshire more than 1% of net worth at time of sale.

### Volatility Is Not Risk
Discussing the 1964–2014 record (the S&P 500 rising from 84 to 2,059 while the dollar's purchasing power fell 87%), Buffett argues that a diversified portfolio of American businesses has been safer over time than currency-denominated instruments, and that business schools mis-teach volatility as a proxy for risk:

> "Though this pedagogic assumption makes for easy teaching, it is dead wrong" [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

He closes the section citing Ben Graham quoting Shakespeare on the cause of investment failure — that the fault lies not in the stars but in ourselves. [[Sources/2014ltr]]

### The Annual Meeting
Held Saturday, May 2, 2015, at the CenturyLink Center as part of the 50th-anniversary celebrations, with record attendance expected, an expanded Friday shopping session, a longhorn-and-stagecoach procession, the newspaper-tossing contest, and the usual journalist/analyst Q&A panel (Carol Loomis, Becky Quick, Andrew Ross Sorkin; analysts including Gary Ransom, Jonathan Brandt and Gregg Warren).

### Golden Anniversary Essay: Berkshire — Past, Present and Future

Buffett traces Berkshire's origin to a 1964 tender-offer dispute with Seabury Stanton over an eighth of a point per share, which led him instead to buy control of the dying textile business — a decision he calls, in substance, foolish, comparing the original 1962 purchase to:

> "Buying the stock at that price was like picking up a discarded cigar butt that had one puff remaining in it. Though the stub might be ugly and soggy, the puff would be free." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

He describes the 1967 purchase of National Indemnity as, in hindsight, a "colossal mistake" of structure — the deal was personally offered to him by Jack Ringwalt but he bought it through Berkshire rather than through Buffett Partnership Ltd., diluting his partners' eventual ownership of everything Berkshire later became. Charlie Munger is credited with redirecting Berkshire's strategy away from "cigar-butt" investing toward paying fair prices for wonderful businesses:

> "The blueprint he gave me was simple: Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

The 1972 See's Candy acquisition (bought for $25 million against a $30 million ask, on about $8 million of net tangible assets) is presented as the turning point that taught Buffett the value of durable brands and pricing power, having since earned $1.9 billion pre-tax on only $40 million of incremental investment. Buffett also owns his worst mistake, the 1993 Dexter Shoe acquisition, paid for in Berkshire stock that would later be worth about $5.7 billion — a loss he calls deserving "a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records" [[Sources/2014ltr]] since Dexter's value went to zero.

The essay makes the case for Berkshire's conglomerate structure as a rational capital allocator, contrasting it with the 1960s conglomerate "hoc us-pocus" of pooling accounting and share-price arbitrage (citing ITT, Litton Industries, Gulf & Western and Jimmy Ling's LTV as cautionary examples) and arguing that Berkshire's tax-free internal capital movement, absence of a "control premium," and status as a permanent home for sellers make spin-offs and forced divestitures value-destroying. On succession, Buffett states the board has already identified his successor as CEO, proposes his son Howard as a non-executive Chairman purely as a "safety valve" against a bad CEO hire, and lays out the qualities his successor and the company will need, including guarding against:

> "the ABCs of business decay, which are arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

### Munger's Vice Chairman's Thoughts — Past and Future

For the first time, Munger contributes his own signed essay analyzing what he calls "the Berkshire system": fifteen enumerated management policies (subsidiary autonomy, a minimal head-office staff, always including casualty insurers for float, cash-only acquisitions, near-permanent ownership of subsidiaries and their CEOs, minimal debt, and confidentiality toward prospective sellers). He attributes Berkshire's results to Buffett's concentration of decision-making in himself over five decades, comparing him to basketball coach John Wooden, who won most regularly after he had learned to "assign virtually all playing time to his seven best players" — and states plainly that Berkshire's future leadership is strong:

> "Ajit Jain and Greg Abel are proven performers who would probably be under-described as 'world-class.'" [[Sources/2014ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

Munger judges that Berkshire's biggest historical mistakes were errors of omission, not commission — citing the failure to buy Walmart stock as an example — and concludes that the Berkshire system's lessons (avoiding headquarters bureaucracy, empowering one accountable decision-maker over long tenures) have use beyond Berkshire itself.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Book value per share +8.3%; 50-year compounded annual gain of 19.4% since 1965
- **Acquisitions**: Duracell (from Procter & Gamble, via stock exchange, closing 2015) and Van Tuyl Automotive (78 dealerships) agreed; 31 bolt-ons totaling $7.8 billion
- **Insurance**: 12th consecutive year of underwriting profit; float grew to $83.921 billion
- **Big Four stakes increased**: American Express 14.8%, Coca-Cola 9.2%, IBM 7.8%, Wells Fargo 9.4%
- **Tesco exit**: Full divestiture completed in 2014 for an after-tax loss of $444 million after a slow, costly exit
- **Capital expenditure**: Record $15 billion companywide; BNSF committed to a record $6 billion 2015 capex plan after a year of service problems
- **Succession**: Board has identified Buffett's CEO successor; Howard Buffett proposed as non-executive Chairman
