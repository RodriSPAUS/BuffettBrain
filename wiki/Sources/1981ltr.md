# 1981 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1981: operating earnings of $39.7 million, 15.2% of beginning equity.
The letter is built around two arguments — why most corporate acquisitions
destroy value, and why inflation had raised the return on doing nothing above the
return on running a business. It also contains the first shareholder-designated
contributions programme and a forecast that 1982 would be the worst underwriting
year in living memory.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The toad, the princess, and the three motives nobody states

The most quoted passage in the early letters is also the most carefully
constructed. Buffett begins with the arithmetic rather than the fable:

- "Regardless of the impact upon immediately reportable earnings, we would rather
  buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X per share.
  Most corporate managers prefer just the reverse, and have no shortage of stated
  rationales for their behavior."

Stated rationales are not the real ones. He names three motives he suspects are
doing the actual work:

1. Appetite. "Leaders, business or otherwise, seldom are deficient in animal
   spirits and often relish increased activity and challenge. At Berkshire, the
   corporate pulse never beats faster than when an acquisition is in prospect."
   Note that he includes himself.
2. The wrong scoreboard. "Most organizations, business or otherwise, measure
   themselves, are measured by others, and compensate their managers far more by
   the yardstick of size than by any other yardstick." The Fortune 500 example
   that follows — every manager knows the sales rank, few know the profitability
   rank — is the whole argument in one sentence.
3. Childhood reading. "Many managements apparently were overexposed in
   impressionable childhood years to the story in which the imprisoned handsome
   prince is released from a toad's body by a kiss from a beautiful princess."

The third motive gets the demolition, and it is an argument about *alternatives*
rather than about optimism: the buyer's shareholders could have bought the target
themselves at the market price.

- "investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors
  instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the
  toad, those kisses had better pack some real dynamite. We've observed many
  kisses but very few miracles."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

### The two acquirers who did succeed

Buffett does not leave the criticism unqualified. Two categories of acquirer
earned their records, and the description of the first is an early definition of
the inflation-resistant franchise that later becomes the moat test:

- "(1) an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is
  flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of
  either market share or unit volume, and (2) an ability to accommodate large
  dollar volume increases in business (often produced more by inflation than by
  real growth) with only minor additional investment of capital."

Pricing power without volume loss, and growth without capital. That pair of
tests survives unchanged into every later letter — see [[Concepts/Moat]] and
[[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]]. The catch is supply: "very few enterprises possess
both characteristics, and competition to buy those that do has now become fierce
to the point of being self-defeating."

The second category is people rather than businesses — "the managerial superstars
- men who can recognize that rare prince who is disguised as a toad, and who have
managerial abilities that enable them to peel away the disguise." He names Ben
Heineman at Northwest Industries, [[People/HenrySingleton]] at Teledyne, Erwin
Zaban at National Service Industries, and [[People/TomMurphy]] at Capital Cities,
whom he calls a twofer for qualifying under both headings. The parenthetical
matters as much as the list: these managers had made very few deals recently and
often found buying their own shares the most sensible use of capital.

Then the self-assessment, which is why the section is credible:

- "Your Chairman, unfortunately, does not qualify for Category 2."
- "Our preaching was better than our performance."
- "We neglected the Noah principle: predicting rain doesn't count, building arks
  does."

### What Berkshire will and will not pay for

The acquisition policy that follows is stated as a subtraction. Berkshire will
pay for what a business already is, not for what a new owner might add:

- "We will continue to seek the acquisition of businesses in their entirety at
  prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise
  develop much along the lines of its past."
- "we will not normally pay a lot in any purchase for what we are supposed to
  bring to the party - for we find that we ordinarily don't bring a lot."

A deal was walked away from in 1981 on exactly that basis — a business and a
manager Buffett liked, at a price that would have left owners worse off. The
sentence recording it is the cleanest statement in the corpus of whose interests
an acquisition is meant to serve: "The empire would have been larger, but the
citizenry would have been poorer."

What remained was the open market. "Currently, we find values most easily
obtained through the open-market purchase of fractional positions in companies
with excellent business franchises and competent, honest managements. We never
expect to run these companies, but we do expect to profit from them." Three ways
that can go wrong are listed without hedging: "(1) the management we have elected
to join; (2) the future economics of the business; or (3) the price we have
paid."

An underrated observation closes the section. Mistakes in marketable securities
get corrected faster than mistakes in wholly-owned subsidiaries, for personal as
much as economic reasons — "Lack of control, in effect, often has turned out to
be an economic plus." Compare the textile decision four years later in
[[Sources/1985ltr]], where control made quitting harder rather than easier.

### Look-through earnings, one year on

The 1980 letter introduced non-controlled ownership earnings; 1981 reports on the
premise. Berkshire's share of the undistributed earnings of GEICO, General Foods,
R. J. Reynolds and The Washington Post was expected to exceed $35 million in
1982 — more than reported operating earnings.

- "our belief is that, in aggregate, those undistributed and, therefore,
  unrecorded earnings will be translated into tangible value for Berkshire
  shareholders just as surely as if subsidiaries we control had earned, retained -
  and reported - similar earnings."

With a timing warning attached: "While market values track business values quite
well over long periods, in any given year the relationship can gyrate
capriciously."

He also explains why the non-controlled portfolio is the better collection of
businesses, and it is not a claim about skill: "In aggregate, our non-controlled
business interests have more favorable underlying economic characteristics than
our controlled businesses. That's understandable; the area of choice has been far
wider." Whole businesses of that quality are "available for purchase in their
entirety only rarely, and then almost always at high prices."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and [[Sources/1980ltr]].

### Equity value-added: the crossbar has been raised

This is the letter's analytical centre and it argues something close to heresy —
that owning American business had, in aggregate, stopped paying.

The old world: a 10% return on equity was a good business when long-term taxable
bonds yielded 5% and tax-exempts 3%, and stocks accordingly sold at over 150
cents on the dollar of book value. The 1981 world: long-term taxable bond yields
above 16%, tax-exempts above 14%, and American business earning about 14% on
equity — before the shareholder's tax.

- "Unless passive rates fall, companies achieving 14% per year gains in earnings
  per share while paying no cash dividend are an economic failure for their
  individual shareholders. The returns from passive capital outstrip the returns
  from active capital."
- The conclusion, stated flatly: "American equity capital, in aggregate, produces
  no value-added for individual investors."

The metaphor is worth keeping because it locates the fault correctly: "It should
be stressed that this depressing situation does not occur because corporations
are jumping, economically, less high than previously. In fact, they are jumping
somewhat higher: return on equity has improved a few points in the past decade.
But the crossbar of passive return has been elevated much faster."

Two consequences follow. The first is a dividend rule that runs directly against
practice: "Logically, a company with historic and prospective high returns on
equity should retain much or all of its earnings so that shareholders can earn
premium returns on enhanced capital. Conversely, low returns on corporate equity
would suggest a very high dividend payout so that owners could direct capital
toward more attractive areas." Buffett cites the parable of the talents in
support, which is the earliest version of the retention test formalised in
[[Sources/1984ltr]].

The second is the trap inflation sets for the low-return business, which must
retain earnings precisely because it cannot afford to distribute them:

- "For inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively
  consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health
  of the host organism."
- "The less prosperous the enterprise, the greater the proportion of available
  sustenance claimed by the tapeworm."
- On the disguises: "Beware of "dividends" that can be paid out only if someone
  promises to replace the capital distributed."

Berkshire is not exempted. "Berkshire continues to retain its earnings for
offensive, not defensive or obligatory, reasons" — but the 21% historic return
clears the passive after-tax bar "barely", and "It would be a bit humiliating to
have our corporate value-added turn negative."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]].

### Inflation, and the limit of good performance

The long-run view had not softened despite Volcker's progress. "Like virginity, a
stable price level seems capable of maintenance, but not of restoration." And the
line that states what a nominally excellent record is actually worth: "because of
the unrelenting destruction of currency values, our corporate efforts will
continue to do a much better job of filling your wallet than of filling your
stomach."

Seventeen years of the present management had taken book value "from $19.46 per
share to $526.02 per share, or 21.1% compounded annually" — reported alongside
the warning that the rate "is highly likely to drift downward in future years."

### Insurance: a forecast with the mechanism attached

The insurance section is not a prediction so much as an accounting identity.
Policies average a little under twelve months, and prices are frozen for the life
of the contract, so this year's written premiums determine roughly half of next
year's earned revenue. Given 1981 volume growth of 3.6% against incurred losses
rising at 10%+, the result was already fixed:

- "There is no danger, however, in forecasting that 1982 will be the worst year in
  recent history for insurance underwriting."
- "The profitability consequences are automatic: if you make a mistake in pricing,
  you have to live with it for an uncomfortable period of time."

Two observations distinguish this from ordinary industry commentary. First, the
recent results had been flattered, not hurt, by luck: "underwriting experience has
been getting worse in spite of good luck, not because of bad luck. In recent years
hurricanes have stayed at sea and motorists have reduced their driving. They
won't always be so obliging." Second, the cycle framing itself is rejected —
Buffett expects large underwriting losses to be the norm, with the best years of
the coming decade looking substandard against the average year of the last one.

Why no individual insurer can opt out: "it's difficult to price much differently
than your most threatened competitor", and the motives pushing volume are the
same ones from the acquisition section — "worship of size over profitability, and
the fear that market share surrendered never can be regained."

Berkshire's answer was to shrink. "We have sacrificed much volume, but have
maintained a substantial underwriting superiority in relation to industry-wide
results." The reserve of optionality this creates is stated explicitly, four
years before it pays off in [[Sources/1985ltr]]: "at some point, should fear ever
prevail throughout the industry, our financial strength could become an
operational asset of immense value."

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Shareholder-designated contributions

The programme let each registered shareholder direct Berkshire's charitable
giving pro rata. Of 932,206 eligible shares, 95.6% responded; excluding
Buffett-related shares, over 90%. Some $1,783,655 went to about 675 charities,
reducing earnings by roughly $900,000.

Buffett reads the response as evidence about ownership rather than about charity:
"Apparently the owners of our corporation like both possessing and exercising the
ability to determine where gifts of their funds shall be made." No shareholder
asked management to decide for them, and none proposed matching directors' own
gifts — a practice he notes is popular, proliferating and unpublicised elsewhere.
The credit goes to [[People/CharlieMunger]], who conceived it.

The failure mode is reported at equal length: brokers holding shares in nominee
name failed to forward the October letter, and one large house transmitted it
about three weeks after receipt, too late for any of its sixty clients — while
billing Berkshire for mailing services within six days. "The results from our
urgings would not strengthen the case for private ownership of the U.S. Postal
Service."

See [[Principles/Fidelity]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### GEICO — 7,200,000 shares, cost $47.1 million, market $199.8 million

The single largest contributor to the year. "Over half of the large gain in
Berkshire's net worth during 1981 - it totaled $124 million, or about 31% -
resulted from the market performance of a single investment, GEICO Corporation."

Buffett is careful not to claim that as business performance: "In aggregate, our
market gain from securities during the year considerably outstripped the gain in
underlying business values. Such market variations will not always be on the
pleasant side."

The reason for holding is operational, not statistical: "we believe that GEICO
Corporation, our major non-controlled business operating in this field, is, by
virtue of its extreme and improving operating efficiency, in a considerably more
protected position than almost any other major insurer. GEICO is a brilliantly
run implementation of a very important business idea." See [[Cases/GEICO]].

### The acquisition that did not happen

A business and a manager Buffett liked, at a price he would not pay. No name is
given. It is reported as a decision rather than a disappointment, and is the
reference point for the "empire … citizenry" line.

### The portfolio, $351.7 million at cost against $639.2 million at market

Fifteen named positions. The largest by market value: GEICO ($199.8m), R. J.
Reynolds ($83.1m), General Foods ($66.7m), The Washington Post ($58.2m, against a
$10.6m cost), Handy & Harman ($36.3m), SAFECO ($31.0m). Four of these — GEICO,
General Foods, R. J. Reynolds and the Post — were expected to contribute over $35
million of unrecorded look-through earnings in 1982.

### Aluminum, corrected

Alcoa is one of only two positions carried below cost ($19.4m against $18.0m).
Buffett flags it himself: "last year your Chairman volunteered his expert opinion
on the rosy future of the aluminum business. Several minor adjustments to that
opinion - now aggregating approximately 180 degrees - have since been required."
A worked example of why he treats errors in marketable securities as cheaper than
errors in subsidiaries.

### Insurance underwriting — volume deliberately surrendered

Underwriting profit fell to $1.5 million pre-tax from $6.7 million, against
industry combined ratios of 105.7 and worsening. Managers named for holding the
line: Phil Liesche, Bill Lyons, Roland Miller, Floyd Taylor and Milt Thornton.
