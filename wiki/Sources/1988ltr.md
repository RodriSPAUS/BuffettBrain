# 1988 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1988: a 20.0% gain in net worth, the first Coca-Cola purchase, the NYSE
listing, and Buffett's most sustained attack on efficient market theory — argued
from a 63-year arbitrage record rather than from principle.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The capital base as the governing problem

- "The major problem we face, however, is a growing capital base. You've heard that
  from us before, but this problem, like age, grows in significance each year."
- With the arithmetic attached: "Four years ago I told you that we needed profits
  of $3.9 billion to achieve a 15% annual return over the decade then ahead. Today,
  for the next decade, a 15% return demands profits of $10.3 billion."
- On why the historical record is a poor guide: "Anyone ignoring these differences
  makes the same mistake that a baseball manager would were he to judge the future
  prospects of a 42-year-old center fielder on the basis of his lifetime batting
  average."
- Four named negatives, including this one about the permanent holdings themselves:
  "All of these companies have superb management and strong properties. But, at
  current prices, their upside potential looks considerably less exciting to us
  today than it did some years ago."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

### Efficient market theory, refuted with a track record

The letter's centrepiece, and unusual in that Buffett argues from evidence rather
than assertion.

- The theory as he states it: "Essentially, it said that analyzing stocks was
  useless because all public information about them was appropriately reflected in
  their prices. In other words, the market always knew everything."
- The error he identifies is precise, and it is not that markets are inefficient:
  "Observing correctly that the market was frequently efficient, they went on to
  conclude incorrectly that it was always efficient. The difference between these
  propositions is night and day."
- The evidence: "In my opinion, the continuous 63-year arbitrage experience of
  Graham-Newman Corp. Buffett Partnership, and Berkshire illustrates just how
  foolish EMT is."
- "While at Graham-Newman, I made a study of its earnings from arbitrage during the
  entire 1926-1956 lifespan of the company. Unleveraged returns averaged 20% per
  year."
- Why he considers it a fair test, in four numbered conditions including: "the
  results are not skewed by a few fortunate experiences" and "our arbitrage
  positions were a clearly identified universe - they have not been selected by
  hindsight."
- The magnitude: "Over the 63 years, the general market delivered just under a 10%
  annual return, including dividends. That means $1,000 would have grown to
  $405,000 if all income had been reinvested. A 20% rate of return, however, would
  have produced $97 million."
- On why the theory persists: "no one, to my knowledge, has ever said he was wrong,
  no matter how many thousands of students he has sent forth misinstructed."
- And the self-interested conclusion: "Naturally the disservice done students and
  gullible investment professionals who have swallowed EMT has been an
  extraordinary service to us and other followers of Graham."

Crucially, he then argues against his own case: "As noted, the market is
reasonably efficient much of the time: For every arbitrage opportunity we seized
in that 63-year period, many more were foregone because they seemed
properly-priced."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]] — this is the empirical companion to the 1987 allegory.

### Arbitrage as a discipline, not a strategy

- Its role: "our insurance subsidiaries sometimes engage in arbitrage as an
  alternative to holding short-term cash equivalents."
- And the second, less obvious reason: "arbitrage sometimes promises much greater
  returns than Treasury Bills and, equally important, cools any temptation we may
  have to relax our standards for long-term investments."
- 1988's result: "Our pre-tax gain was about $78 million on average invested funds
  of about $147 million."
- What arbitrage does and does not insulate you from: "In most cases the
  arbitrageur expects to profit regardless of the behavior of the stock market. The
  major risk he usually faces instead is that the announced event won't happen."
- The closing warning, which applies to every style: "An investor cannot obtain
  superior profits from stocks by simply committing to a specific investment
  category or style. He can earn them only by carefully evaluating facts and
  continuously exercising discipline."
- "Investing in arbitrage situations, per se, is no better a strategy than
  selecting a portfolio by throwing darts."

See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### Why bad CEOs keep their jobs

One of the sharpest passages on governance in the letters, and the origin of a
much-repeated image.

- "The supreme irony of business management is that it is far easier for an
  inadequate CEO to keep his job than it is for an inadequate subordinate."
- The comparison: a secretary who types 50 words a minute against a required 80
  "will lose her job in no time. There is a logical standard for this job;
  performance is easily measured; and if you can't make the grade, you're out."
- The CEO's position: "However, a CEO who doesn't perform is frequently carried
  indefinitely. One reason is that performance standards for his job seldom exist."
- The image: "At too many companies, the boss shoots the arrow of managerial
  performance and then hastily paints the bullseye around the spot where it lands."
- The structural cause: "Another important, but seldom recognized, distinction
  between the boss and the foot soldier is that the CEO has no immediate superior
  whose performance is itself getting measured."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Insurance: unprofitable and unpopular

- "The property-casualty insurance industry is not only subnormally profitable, it
  is subnormally popular."
- The comparison that makes the point: "Take the breakfast cereal industry, whose
  return on invested capital is more than double that of the auto insurance
  industry (which is why companies like Kellogg and General Mills sell at five
  times book value and most large insurers sell close to book)."
- "The cereal companies regularly impose price increases, few of them related to a
  significant jump in their costs. Yet not a peep is heard from consumers. But when
  auto insurers raise prices by amounts that do not even match cost increases,
  customers are outraged."
- The forecast, again made explicitly: "As the table shows, the industry's
  underwriting loss grew in 1988. This trend is almost certain to continue - and
  probably will accelerate - for at least two more years."

Note that pricing power — the ability to raise prices without losing customers —
is exactly the franchise test Buffett will formalise three years later. See
[[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Concepts/Float]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Coca-Cola — the first appearance

The year-end table lists "14,172,500 The Coca-Cola Company" at a cost of
$592,540 thousand against a market value of $632,448 thousand — a position built
during 1988 and expanded the following year. It becomes the largest single
holding in the letters and the standing example of a brand franchise.

### The three permanent holdings, unchanged

- "Our permanent holdings - Capital Cities/ABC, Inc., GEICO Corporation, and The
  Washington Post Company - remain unchanged."
- With the managers named individually: "Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Cap Cities,
  Bill Snyder and Lou Simpson at GEICO, and Kay Graham and Dick Simmons at The
  Washington Post."

### Selection framework, restated

The five categories and the single criterion are repeated verbatim from 1987:
"We have no particular bias when it comes to choosing from these categories. We
just continuously search among them for the highest after-tax returns as measured
by "mathematical expectation," limiting ourselves always to investment
alternatives we think we understand."

### NYSE listing, 29 November 1988

- "As the letter explains, our primary goal in listing was to reduce transaction
  costs, and we believe this goal is being achieved."
- "Generally, the spread between the bid and asked price on the NYSE has been well
  below the spread that prevailed in the over-the-counter market."

A shareholder-cost decision rather than a prestige one — consistent with the
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] argument that market mechanics should serve
owners.
