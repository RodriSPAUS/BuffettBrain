# 2020 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting Q&A Summary

Berkshire's first fully virtual annual meeting, held May 3, 2020 in an empty CHI Health Center in Omaha at the height of the COVID-19 shutdown. Charlie Munger, then 96, did not travel; Ajit Jain stayed in New York; Greg Abel sat on stage in Munger's place, the first time a vice chairman rather than Munger fielded questions beside Buffett. The meeting has four parts: a long prepared monologue built around slides — a historical case for "never bet against America" — a brief recap of first-quarter results and the April airline sale, the formal shareholder meeting, and roughly two hours of Becky Quick relaying questions submitted by e-mail (and by Carol Loomis and Andrew Ross Sorkin). The dominant threads are the twin uncertainty of the pandemic on the health and economic sides, Berkshire's Fort-Knox balance sheet as protection against a credit freeze that nearly happened in March, and Buffett's flat admission that buying into the four big airlines was his mistake.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Never Bet Against America: the Slide Monologue
The core of the prepared remarks is a historical argument that the United States has overcome far worse than the pandemic. Buffett estimates the country's wealth in 1789 at roughly $1 billion (using the 1803 Louisiana Purchase — 800,000 square miles for $15 million — as the "best comp"), against Federal Reserve household net worth of more than $100 trillion in 2020: a real gain he puts at about 5,000-for-1 in 231 years. He stresses that this was not steady progress. The Civil War deliberately killed roughly 6% of the country's males aged 18 to 60; the 1929 crash (Dow closed 381.17 on September 3, 1929) led to a Great Depression so scarring that the market did not permanently reclaim its 1929 level until the early 1950s. He recounts being born August 30, 1930, into that market: $1,000 invested in the Dow the day he was born would have fallen to $170 in less than two years, and his father lost his job when the bank he worked at closed.

> "But anyway, this time, we just pulled the train off the tracks and put it on a siding." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

The conclusion, repeated as the meeting's refrain and echoing that year's chairman's letter, is that the American tailwind — the Dow turning $1 into roughly $100 over his lifetime — remains intact for anyone who owns a cross-section of the country and does not use leverage.

> "But in the end the answer is, never bet against America." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

### The FDIC as the Lesson of the Depression
Buffett singles out one institution as the change that would have blunted the Depression: federal deposit insurance, created January 1, 1934. Over 4,000 banks failed in the early 1930s — 4,000 local experiences of savers reaching for money that was gone — and he argues the psychological damage of that, repeated across all 48 states, prolonged the downturn far beyond its real economic effects. The FDIC, funded by assessments on banks rather than taxpayers, then held about $100 billion.

> "But the FDIC has not cost the American taxpayer a dime." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

He returns to the point later: the banking system in 2020 is "in far better shape" than 2008-09, which made Chairman Jerome Powell's job easier because the banks were not themselves the problem this time.

### The Farm and the Manic-Depressive Neighbor
Restating the [[Concepts/MrMarket]] parable in agricultural terms, Buffett asks the listener to imagine owning a 160-acre farm bought for its productive value, with a neighbor owning an identical farm who — being "a manic depressive" who "drinks, maybe smokes a little pot" — shouts out a different price to buy or sell every single day. Stocks, uniquely, give you exactly that obliging neighbor, and most people turn the advantage into a disadvantage.

> "Now the only thing you have to do is to remember that this guy next door is there to serve you and not to instruct you." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

The lesson feeds directly into his standing recommendation for non-professionals: buy a cross-section of America and forget about it.

> "In my view, for most people, the best thing to do is to own the S&P 500 Index fund." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

### Leverage, Worst Cases, and the Pandemic That Was Foreseen
Buffett frames Berkshire's whole posture as preparing for improbable events that arrive together, "maybe partly caused by the first, but maybe independent even of the first" — a hurricane and then an earthquake a month later — which is why the company never uses margin: any series of numbers multiplied by a single zero is zero. He points out that pandemics were not unforeseeable. He cites Sam Nunn's Nuclear Threat Initiative and a 300-page October 2019 Johns Hopkins/NTI/Economist study of global pandemic preparedness that gave every country a failing grade, delivered to him in person in November 2019 — warnings that, like Bill Gates's TED talk, drew almost no attention beforehand.

### First-Quarter Results and the March Credit Freeze
Operating earnings for Q1 (about $5-6 billion) are dismissed as meaningless for forecasting; the virus will cost Berkshire money, but the three largest businesses — insurance, the BNSF railroad ([[Cases/BNSF]]) and energy ([[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]]) — remain in decent shape. Buffett addresses the roughly $125 billion of cash and Treasury bills at March 31 by arguing it is not a 40% cash position at all, because Berkshire also owns "hundreds of billions" of wholly-owned businesses that carry no quote. He then describes how close the credit market came to freezing entirely around March 23, 2020: investment-grade companies that had leaned on commercial paper rushed to draw down bank lines, the Treasury market itself grew disorganized, and only the Federal Reserve's intervention reopened issuance — turning March into a record month for corporate debt, then April into a larger one. He credits Powell and the Fed board directly (invoking Paul Volcker's book *Keeping At It* and Mario Draghi's "whatever it takes"), while insisting Berkshire must never depend on such rescue.

> "And fear is the most contagious disease you can imagine. It makes the virus look like a piker." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

### The Airline Sale: "That Was My Mistake"
The most-quoted moment of the meeting. Berkshire had built roughly 10% stakes in the four largest U.S. airlines — American, Delta, Southwest and United — paying between $7 billion and $8 billion for what it estimated as about $1 billion of underlying annual earnings, treating the position mentally as if buying whole businesses. In April 2020 Berkshire sold the entire stake in all four, at prices well below cost.

> "And we did not take out anything like $7 billion or $8 billion, and that was my mistake." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

Buffett stresses this was no criticism of four excellent CEOs — the world simply changed for the airline business through no fault of its own — and explains the all-or-nothing exit as a matter of method, not a market prediction:

> "I mean we don't trim positions." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

Because so many people fly, and because grounded aircraft "don't disappear" when demand returns at 70-80%, he judged the industry's future genuinely less clear, and would not fund a business he now expected to burn cash — the same discipline that made Berkshire exit the textile business after twenty years.

### Why Not a "Lender of Support" Like 2008
Several questioners ask why Berkshire, which financed eight rescue deals in 2008-09 on rich terms, has done nothing comparable. Buffett's answer is that the 2008 deals were never about making a statement — they were simply attractive terms available because markets were in panic — and that the Fed's speed in 2020 meant even marginal companies could raise money in the public market "at terms that we wouldn't have given it to them." Nothing attractive appeared. Greg Abel notes Berkshire Hathaway Energy did issue $4 billion of securities after March 23 to lengthen out short-term obligations and prefund a PacifiCorp capital program.

### Insurance: GEICO Givebacks, Business Interruption, and Pandemic Coverage
On [[Cases/GEICO]], reduced driving during lockdown means the company will return roughly $2.5 billion to policyholders (a 15% credit) to reflect lower accident frequency — the largest such giveback in the industry — while also extending payment deadlines. On coronavirus insurance losses generally, Buffett explains that Berkshire is "not big in the commercial multiple peril business" where business-interruption claims will concentrate, and that standard policy language usually requires physical damage for a business-interruption claim; litigation costs alone will be huge. Asked whether Berkshire would write pandemic coverage in future, he says yes, at the right price — the same appetite for very large, unusual limits that led Berkshire and AIG to write heavy volume after 9/11.

### Succession: Abel, Combs and Weschler on Capital; Jain on Insurance
Asked whether Berkshire can keep negotiating opportunistic deals once he and Munger are gone, Buffett is unusually bullish on his successors, saying the three of them thinking about capital allocation "could very well be a significant improvement" over him and Munger. When a shareholder notes he omitted Ajit Jain ([[People/AjitJain]]) from that list, Buffett clarifies that Jain's genius is insurance-risk evaluation, not capital allocation — recalling that he once wrote to Jain's father that if he had another son like this, to "send him over from India because we'll own the world."

> "Anybody will tell you that's had any contact with him and particularly anybody in the insurance business where they know him well, he is absolutely one of a kind." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

Greg Abel ([[People/GregAbel]]), fielding several questions in Munger's usual seat, argues the culture and business acumen that let Berkshire act quickly will not change without the founders.

### Capital-Intensive Businesses, Inflation, and the Energy Runway
Abel walks through Berkshire Hathaway Energy's capital program: roughly $40 billion of projects over the coming decade (about half maintenance, half incremental growth such as wind, solar and transmission), another ~$30 billion nearly ready, and regulated returns on more than 80% of the asset base — a business where a transmission project begun with the 2008 PacifiCorp purchase reaches service only in 2020. Buffett's summary of the economics is one of his most memorable formulations:

> "I've always said about the energy business, it's not a way to get real rich, but it's a way to stay real rich." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

He contrasts this with capital-light compounders. The dream business needs no capital as it grows and delivers cash to be redeployed elsewhere tax-efficiently:

> "I mean See's never required capital." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

On inflation, he says he would be "very happy" to own the railroad in a high-inflation world because pricing power lets it earn more nominal dollars, but a business that needs no capital would always be better.

### Occidental, Oil at Minus $37, and the Equity Puts
On the $10 billion Occidental preferred financing ([[Cases/OccidentalPetroleum]]) that helped Oxy win Anadarko, Buffett concedes the investment depends on the price of oil, which had briefly gone to minus $37 a barrel on the expiring May contract. The Anadarko deal "doesn't work at $20 a barrel," and he tells Oxy shareholders they will "join me in having made a mistake so far" Separately he walks through Berkshire's old long-dated equity index put contracts: 48-50 written between 2004 and 2006 for about $4.8 billion of premium, 15-to-20-year terms, no collateral posted, roughly $14 billion nominal remaining, the last expiring in 2023 — "so far, so good."

### Buybacks, Negative Rates, and Sovereign Default
Three macro/policy threads recur in the Q&A. On buybacks (a question from finance professor David Kass), Buffett gives the three-partner McDonald's-franchise analogy: repurchasing the share of the partner who wants cash, rather than forcing a dividend on the two who don't, is simply distributing capital to those who want it — provided the stock is bought below intrinsic value.

> "And to my knowledge, JPMorgan, Jamie Dimon said it once and we've said it various times, we will repurchase shares when it's to the advantage of the continuing shareholder to have us do so." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

On the possibility of the U.S. government defaulting, he is dismissive — a country that borrows in its own currency need never default, though the currency's purchasing power can be in doubt (his "Buffett bucks" thought experiment; Argentina as the counter-case of debt in someone else's currency).

> "If you print bonds in your own currency, what happens to the currency can be a question but you don't default." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

On negative interest rates and their threat to the value of insurance float ([[Concepts/Float]]), he admits he has been "wrong so far" in expecting inflation, and calls the sustainability of the whole low-rate, high-debt experiment the deepest open question in the field:

> "It's probably the most interesting question I've ever seen in economics is, can you keep doing what we're doing now?" [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]]

### The Formal Meeting: Directors Elected, Diversity Proposal Fails
Between the monologue and the Q&A, the formal meeting convened with 699,123 Class A and 1,382,352,370 Class B shares outstanding. All 14 directors were re-elected — Buffett noting that new director Ken Chenault received the highest vote of any nominee, "well ahead of me." The advisory vote on executive compensation passed, and shareholders chose a triennial frequency for it. A shareholder proposal from four New York City pension systems (presented on the Comptroller's behalf by Marc Hamburg) asking Berkshire to adopt a board-and-CEO diversity search policy was opposed by the directors and failed, 65,925 for to 485,824 against; Buffett said he is "in sync with the Comptroller in terms of how he wants the world to evolve" but disagrees on the specifics as applied to Berkshire's board.

### Closing: Capitalism, the Uncounted Workers, and Ben Graham
In the final questions Buffett defends capitalism as marvelous but brutal if left to itself — "creative destruction" that needs government and does not reward front-line pandemic workers (health care, food supply, delivery) anything like their contribution, using Ted Williams's $20,000 salary for batting .406 in 1941 versus modern athletes' pay to illustrate how a "winner-takes-all" market has widened. Asked to name the other two of the three smartest people he has met besides Ben Graham ([[People/BenGraham]]), he declines — noting IQ does not equal wisdom or rationality — and closes the meeting where he began: never bet against America.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Airlines — entire stake sold (April 2020)**: roughly 10% of American, Delta, Southwest and United, bought for $7-8 billion against an estimated ~$1 billion of underlying earnings, sold in full at a loss; called Buffett's mistake, not a market prediction
- **Cash and Treasury bills**: about $125 billion at March 31, 2020, held only in Treasury bills (never commercial paper, no reliance on bank lines); framed as far less than 40% of value once wholly-owned businesses are counted
- **Q1 2020 equities**: bought about $1.7 billion of stock, a couple of billion more than sold; net *sold* about $6 billion in April (mostly the airlines)
- **GEICO givebacks**: roughly $2.5 billion returned to policyholders (a 15% credit) for reduced accident frequency during lockdown, plus extended payment deadlines
- **No 2008-style rescue deals**: nothing attractive appeared after the Fed's March 23 intervention let even marginal companies raise money cheaply in the public market
- **Berkshire Hathaway Energy**: issued $4 billion of securities post-March 23 to lengthen short-term obligations and prefund a PacifiCorp program; ~$40 billion of projects over the coming decade plus ~$30 billion more nearly ready
- **Occidental Petroleum**: the $10 billion preferred position acknowledged as a mistake "so far" with oil having touched minus $37 a barrel
- **Legacy equity index puts**: 48-50 contracts written 2004-2006 for ~$4.8 billion premium, ~$14 billion nominal remaining, no collateral, last expiring 2023
- **Formal meeting**: all 14 directors re-elected (Ken Chenault highest vote); executive-compensation advisory vote passed with a triennial frequency; NYC pension funds' board-diversity proposal failed 65,925 for / 485,824 against
