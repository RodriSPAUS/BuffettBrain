# 2007 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 11.0% against the S&P 500's 5.5%. The letter opens
by naming the mortgage crisis just as it becomes visible — quoting Wells Fargo
CEO John Stumpf on lenders who "invented new ways to lose money when the old
ways seemed to work just fine" — and closes its insurance section with a
warning that the two catastrophe-light years just enjoyed are ending. In
between sits the letter's most quoted essay — titled "Businesses – The Great,
the Good and the Gruesome" — which states the moat criteria in testable form
and works See's Candy's fifty-year economics down to the dollar as the
standing example of a great business. Buffett also confesses three of his own worst
capital-allocation mistakes in one sitting, and closes the accounting section
with a sustained argument that public-company pension assumptions are
fictions waiting to be discovered.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The mortgage crisis, seen from the lending side

> "It is interesting that the industry has invented new ways to lose money
> when the old ways seemed to work just fine." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Buffett traces the "weakened lending practices" he had flagged the year before
to their logical end: a universal belief that house prices would only rise,
which made a borrower's income and equity seem irrelevant to lenders.

> "You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out – and
> what we are witnessing at some of our largest financial institutions is an
> ugly sight." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Berkshire's own housing-linked units (brick, carpet, real estate brokerage)
had setbacks Buffett calls minor and temporary, distinguishing operating
softness from the structural failures at lenders. He also puts a number on
the insurance industry's coming reversal after two catastrophe-light years:

> "It's a certainty that insurance-industry profit margins, including ours,
> will fall significantly in 2008." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

### Yardsticks: two buckets of value

Buffett restates the framework introduced the year before — investments
(stocks, bonds, cash, $141 billion at yearend) and non-insurance operating
earnings (66 businesses) — and updates both series at 14-year intervals:

| Measure | 1965 | 1979 | 1993 | 2007 | Compound rate (last period) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Per-share investments | $4 | $577 | $13,961 | $90,343 | 14.3% (1993-2007) |
| Non-insurance pre-tax earnings per share | $4 | $18 | $212 | $4,093 | 23.5% (1993-2007) |

The full-period compounded rates are 27.1% and 17.8% respectively — but
Buffett is explicit the trend line is meaningless as a forecast:

> "Berkshire's past record can't be duplicated or even approached. Our base of
> assets and earnings is now far too large for us to make outsized gains in
> the future." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

### Acquisitions: from a 1954 cocoa tender offer to Marmon

Buffett traces the Marmon acquisition back to a 1954 Rockwood Chocolate
shareholders' meeting he attended for Ben Graham and Jerry Newman, where a
young Jay Pritzker's cocoa-bean tender offer taught him the deal that decades
later produced Berkshire's "largest cash purchase" to date:

> "We will soon purchase 60% of Marmon and will acquire virtually all of the
> balance within six years. Our initial outlay will be $4.5 billion, and the
> price of our later purchases will be based on a formula tied to earnings."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Marmon operates 125 businesses through nine sectors, anchored by Union Tank
Car's 94,000 leased rail cars, for $7 billion of sales and about 20,000
employees. The deal was struck on financial statements alone, no advisors, no
renegotiation — the same standard Buffett applies to every Berkshire deal:

> "With the Pritzkers, as with Berkshire, a deal is a deal." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

**Richline Group** — a jewelry supplier built by combining Dennis Ulrich's gold
manufacturer with a similarly-sized peer — arrived through a Ben Bridge vendor
lunch, below Berkshire's normal acquisition size but kept for its people. See
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Businesses: the Great, the Good and the Gruesome

The letter's fullest statement of the acquisition criteria: "a) a business we
understand; b) favorable long-term economics; c) able and trustworthy
management; and d) a sensible price tag." The moat test follows directly:

> "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent
> returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that
> competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning
> high returns." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Two disqualifiers are named explicitly: an industry of rapid, continuous
change ("A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat
at all") and a business whose success depends on one exceptional manager
rather than the structure itself:

> "But if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the
> business itself cannot be deemed great. ... You can count, though, on the
> moat of the Mayo Clinic to endure, even though you can't name its CEO."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

**See's, worked in full.** Bought for $25 million in 1972 against $30 million
in sales and under $5 million of pre-tax earnings, on $8 million of required
capital — 60% pre-tax return on invested capital, kept low by cash sales (no
receivables) and a short production cycle (low inventory). By 2007, sales had
grown to $383 million and pre-tax profit to $82 million, requiring only $40
million of capital — meaning just $32 million of reinvestment funded 35 years
of growth while $1.35 billion of pre-tax earnings, net of that $32 million,
went to Berkshire to buy other businesses:

> "There's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it.
> Indeed, it's often a mistake to do so: Truly great businesses, earning huge
> returns on tangible assets, can't for any extended period reinvest a large
> portion of their earnings internally at high rates of return."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

**FlightSafety, the "good" case.** Bought in 1996 at $111 million of pre-tax
earnings and $570 million of net fixed assets, FlightSafety required $1.635
billion of capital expenditure (mostly simulators) against $923 million of
depreciation to grow pre-tax earnings by $159 million — a real but far more
capital-hungry return than See's.

**Airlines, the "gruesome" case.** Buffett revisits his own 1989 US Air
preferred-stock purchase as the cautionary tale, and the industry as a whole
as the standing counter-example to a moat:

> "Indeed, if a farsighted capitalist had been present at Kitty Hawk, he would
> have done his successors a huge favor by shooting Orville down."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

The three-savings-accounts summary — the great one paying a rising rate, the
good one an attractive flat rate, the gruesome one an inadequate rate that
still demands fresh deposits — is the chapter's closing frame. See
[[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Cases/SeeCandies]].

### Confession time: three unforced errors

Buffett names three of his own capital-allocation mistakes in sequence,
crediting none of them to advisors or directors. He nearly lost See's itself
by refusing to go above $25 million against a $30 million ask. He turned down
Tom Murphy's 1972 offer of the Dallas-Fort Worth NBC station for $35
million — a See's-like, low-capital business that by 2006 alone was earning
$73 million pre-tax and had a capital value near $800 million:

> "Why did I say 'no'? The only explanation is that my brain had gone on
> vacation and forgot to notify me." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

The costliest error was Dexter, a shoe business bought in 1993 for $433
million in Berkshire stock whose moat "vanished within a few years" — and
because the purchase price was paid in stock rather than cash, the real cost
to shareholders was not $400 million but the value that stock would later
represent:

> "In essence, I gave away 1.6% of a wonderful business – one now valued at
> $220 billion – to buy a worthless business." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

> "To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made. But I'll make more
> mistakes in the future – you can bet on that." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

> "GEICO possesses the widest moat of any of our insurers, one carefully
> protected and expanded by Tony Nicely, its CEO." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

### Regulated Utility Business: the extra nickel

MidAmerican's diluted interest reached 87.4%. Buffett tells the origin of the
odd $35.05 per-share price paid for the original 1999 stake as a rare
concession from his usual one-price rule:

> "I originally decided the business was worth $35.00 per share to Berkshire.
> ... But, finally, they caught me in a moment of weakness, and I caved,
> telling them I would go to $35.05." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

2007 per-share earnings reached $15.78 (or $15.01 normalized, excluding a
one-time U.K. tax benefit), against the original $2.59 in 1999 — validating
the extra nickel many times over. HomeServices had a slow year and Buffett
expects 2008 to be slower still. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: housing hits, and three standouts

Shaw, Acme Brick, Johns Manville and MiTek all took housing-downturn hits,
pre-tax earnings down 27%, 41%, 38% and 9% respectively, combining for $941
million against $1.296 billion in 2006. Against that backdrop, three
retailers stood out. Brad Kinstler, moved to See's CEO from Fechheimer and
Cypress Insurance two years earlier, lifted profits more than 50% in that
span. Susan Jacques, hired as Borsheims CEO in 1994 after starting there as a
$4-an-hour saleswoman with no managerial background, delivered 15.1% sales
growth:

> "She's smart, she loves the business, and she loves her associates. That
> beats having an MBA degree any time." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Nebraska Furniture Mart's Omaha and Kansas City stores each hit about $400
million of sales, the two largest home-furnishings stores in the country,
under Ron and Irv Blumkin. Iscar opened a large plant in Dalian, China, to
process the tungsten its Israeli operations had always imported:

> "For many decades, Iscar moved tungsten to Israel, where brains turned it
> into something far more valuable. ... In effect, we've now moved the brains
> to the tungsten." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Flight Services set a record, pre-tax earnings up 49% to $547 million.
NetJets, after ten years of losses in Europe totaling $212 million, tripled
its earnings there once Mark Booth took over that operation under Rich
Santulli. See [[Cases/ForestRiver]] for the adjacent RV business shown
alongside NetJets and Clayton homes at the annual meeting.

### Finance and Finance Products: Clayton's record share, a shrinking industry

Clayton reached a record 31% market share even as industry-wide manufactured
home sales fell to 96,000 from 131,000 at the time of Berkshire's 2003
purchase. Most of Clayton's earnings now come from an $11 billion loan
portfolio covering 300,000 borrowers, financed by Berkshire at a one-point
markup that generated an $85 million fee in 2007 — with delinquency and loss
rates holding steady through the year's broader mortgage turmoil.

American Express, Coca-Cola and Procter & Gamble grew per-share earnings 12%,
14% and 14%; Wells Fargo's earnings dipped slightly on the real-estate
downturn, though Buffett judges its intrinsic value still rose. He restates
the "moat-widening" test from [[Sources/2005ltr]] applied to the Big Four
directly: "All of the 'big four' scored positively on that test."

PetroChina, bought for $488 million in 2002-03 when the whole company was
priced near $37 billion (against Buffett and Munger's own $100 billion
estimate), was sold entirely in 2007 for $4 billion once the market caught up
to about $275 billion — with a footnote on the resulting tax bill:

> "We paid the IRS tax of $1.2 billion on our PetroChina gain. This sum paid
> all costs of the U.S. government – defense, social security, you name it –
> for about four hours." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

Buffett's personally-managed derivative book grew from 62 to 94 contracts,
split between 54 high-yield credit-default contracts ($3.2 billion of
premiums received, $472 million paid in losses, a worst-case additional
exposure of $4.7 billion) and long-dated equity index put options on the S&P
500 plus three foreign indices, struck at the market with 15-20 year terms
and $4.5 billion of premiums received. Both categories share the same
structural advantage: "in all cases we hold the money, which means that we
have no counterparty risk."

### Currency: the Brazilian real, and Euro-denominated Amazon bonds

Berkshire's only direct currency position in 2007 was the Brazilian real —
notable given that "five versions of Brazilian currency have, in effect,
turned into confetti" over the past century. The real gained against the
dollar every year from 2002 to 2007 (indexed 100 to 199), even as the
Brazilian government worked to hold it down. Direct currency positions
produced $2.3 billion of pre-tax profit over five years; a separate,
indirect currency gain came from €310 million of Amazon.com bonds bought in
2001-02 at 57% of par when they were mispriced as junk credit — a position
Buffett calls proof that "you can occasionally find markets that are
ridiculously inefficient" — [[Sources/2007ltr]]. The trade-deficit argument continues from
[[Sources/2004ltr]], [[Sources/2005ltr]] and [[Sources/2006ltr]], with a new
data point: the trade deficit with Germany and Canada both *grew* between
2002 and 2007 despite a much weaker dollar against both currencies.

### Succession: four investment candidates named

CEO succession remains three internal candidates deep. On the investment
side, the search first described in [[Sources/2006ltr]] has produced results:

> "We have indeed now identified four candidates who could succeed me in
> managing investments. All manage substantial sums currently, and all have
> indicated a strong interest in coming to Berkshire if called."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

> "I've reluctantly discarded the notion of my continuing to manage the
> portfolio after my death – abandoning my hope to give new meaning to the
> term 'thinking outside the box.'" — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

See [[People/WarrenBuffett]].

### Fanciful Figures: pension assumptions as an honor system abused

Buffett traces stock-option expensing's defeat by the 1994 Senate vote
(88-9) — the same episode covered in [[Sources/2002ltr]] and
[[Sources/2004ltr]] — through to backdating, then pivots to a still-live
version of the same problem: pension return assumptions averaging 8% across
the S&P's 363 pension-plan companies. He works the arithmetic: with about
28% of assets in bonds and cash earning at most 5%, the remaining 72% in
equities must earn 9.2% net of fees to hit 8% overall — a bar he calls
unrealistic given the 20th century's actual 5.3% compounded market-value
gain. The logical proof that most "helped" investors must underperform:

> "1) Investors, overall, will necessarily earn an average return, minus costs
> they incur; 2) Passive and index investors, through their very inactivity,
> will earn that average minus costs that are very low; 3) With that group
> earning average returns, so must the remaining group – the active
> investors. But this group will incur high transaction, management, and
> advisory costs." — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

> "That means that the passive group – the 'know-nothings' – must win."
> — [[Sources/2007ltr]]

He quotes Munger's own correction for the habit of pushing assumptions
further each year: "If you've hit three balls out of bounds to the left, aim
a little to the right on the next swing." A parallel and larger version of
the same problem, he warns, sits in public-sector pension promises, whose
"fuse" is long enough that the officials who made them will be gone before
the bill arrives. See [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Loss reserves: buried in a rented suit

Buffett closes the accounting section by admitting Berkshire runs its own
$56 billion insurance-liability estimate on the same honor-system logic he
has just criticized in others — a necessary guess, always wrong by some
margin, corrected only over decades. The story he uses to illustrate the
problem is the funeral-bill parable ending in the line: "We buried Dad in a
rented suit." His own hedge: "I personally believe our stated reserves are
adequate, but I've been wrong several times in the past."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Marmon Group** — 60% for $4.5 billion initial outlay, remainder to be
  acquired within six years on an earnings-based formula; 125 businesses
  across nine sectors including Union Tank Car's 94,000-railcar fleet.
- **Richline Group** — a jewelry supplier built from two combined
  acquisitions, below Berkshire's normal size threshold.

**Sold**

- **PetroChina** — entire stake sold for $4 billion, versus $488 million
  cost, once the market value reached the roughly $100 billion Buffett and
  Munger had estimated years earlier; generated a $1.2 billion tax payment.

**Portfolio**

- No major changes to the Big Four (American Express, Coca-Cola, Procter &
  Gamble, Wells Fargo); all judged to have widened their moats during the
  year.
- Derivative book grew to 94 personally-managed contracts: 54 high-yield
  credit-default contracts and long-dated equity index puts across four
  indices, $7.7 billion of combined premiums received.
- Held a direct Brazilian real position (the only direct currency position
  in 2007) and Euro-denominated Amazon.com bonds bought as mispriced junk
  credit in 2001-02.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew to $58.698 billion at a $3.374 billion underwriting profit.
- BoatU.S. added to the smaller-insurers group.
- MidAmerican's diluted interest reached 87.4%; 2007 per-share earnings of
  $15.78 ($15.01 normalized).

**Succession**

- Four investment-management candidates identified for the eventual
  portfolio-management succession, alongside the three existing internal CEO
  candidates.
