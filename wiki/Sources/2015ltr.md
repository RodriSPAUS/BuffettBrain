# 2015 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share book value grew 6.4% in 2015 (net worth +$15.4 billion) against a 1.4% decline for the S&P 500, extending the 51-year compounded annual gain to 19.2%. BNSF's dramatic service turnaround after 2014's problems was the year's most important development; Berkshire also closed the giant Precision Castparts acquisition (announced during the year, closed just before publication) and completed the Kraft Heinz merger. Beyond the standard business report, the letter carries two long essays — "Productivity and Prosperity," using farming, railroads, GEICO and Berkshire Hathaway Energy as case studies of productivity gains and their costs, and "Important Risks," addressing cyberattack/WMD risk and, via a proxy proposal, climate change.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Year at Berkshire: BNSF's Turnaround, Precision Castparts, and Kraft Heinz
After 2014's service failures, BNSF invested a record $5.8 billion in capital expenditures during 2015 (nearly three times its annual depreciation charge) and posted record pre-tax income of $6.8 billion, up $606 million. The "Powerhouse Five" (BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, Marmon, Lubrizol and IMC) earned $13.1 billion combined, up $650 million from 2014; Buffett previews a "Powerhouse Six" for 2016 once Precision Castparts (PCC), bought for more than $32 billion in cash under CEO Mark Donegan, is added. With PCC, Berkshire would own 10¼ Fortune-500-equivalent companies (Kraft Heinz's 27% stake counting as the ¼).

> "Next year, I will be discussing the 'Powerhouse Six.'" [[Sources/2015ltr]]

The Heinz partnership with Jorge Paulo Lemann's 3G Capital more than doubled in size by merging with Kraft: Berkshire's roughly 53% stake in Heinz (cost $4.25 billion) became 325.4 million Kraft Heinz shares (about 27%, cost $9.8 billion). GAAP required a $6.8 billion non-cash write-up of the investment on completion of the merger even though no shares were sold. Berkshire's Kraft Heinz preferred shares (paying $720 million annually, carried at $7.7 billion) were expected to be redeemed for $8.32 billion mid-2016 — good for Kraft Heinz, "bad news for Berkshire." [[Cases/GEICO]] aside, Buffett again invokes the Woody Allen "bi-sexual" line on capital-allocation flexibility, and reiterates a well-known Mungerism on why Berkshire stays hands-off with acquired managers:

> "If you want to guarantee yourself a lifetime of misery, be sure to marry someone with the intent of changing their behavior." [[Sources/2015ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

Berkshire increased stakes in all of the Big Four: American Express to 15.6%, Coca-Cola to 9.3%, IBM to 8.4%, Wells Fargo to 9.8%.

### America's Golden Goose
Responding to election-year pessimism, Buffett argues per-capita GDP growth compounds powerfully over a generation even at a "much-lamented" 2% growth rate, and that today's children — including in his own middle-class Omaha neighborhood — already live better than John D. Rockefeller Sr. did.

> "That view is dead wrong: The babies being born in America today are the luckiest crop in history." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Intrinsic Business Value: Underwriting Income Joins the Earnings Metric
For the first time, Berkshire's per-share earnings metric (the second of its two quantitative value pillars) includes insurance underwriting income, since results have become steadier as catastrophe-heavy lines were deemphasized. Per-share investments rose 8.3% to $159,794 (Kraft Heinz at market value); per-share earnings including underwriting rose 2.1% to $12,304, of which underwriting itself contributed $1,118. Since 1970, per-share investments have compounded at 18.9% annually and earnings at 23.7%.

### Insurance: Thirteen Years of Underwriting Profit
Float grew from $41 billion to $88 billion over the 13-year underwriting-profit streak ($26.2 billion cumulative underwriting profit), reaching $87.722 billion at year-end 2015:

Buffett describes underwriting discipline at Berkshire in the starkest terms yet:

> "All insurers give that message lip service. At Berkshire it is a religion, Old Testament style." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

Ajit Jain is credited with having "created tens of billions of value for Berkshire shareholders" since Mike Goldberg handed him the reinsurance business in 1986 despite Jain having no prior insurance experience. [[Cases/GEICO]], under Tony Nicely (54 years of service in 2015), ended the year with 11.4% market share versus 2.5% in 1995, and Buffett quotes its ad tagline directly:

> "15 minutes could save you 15% or more on car insurance." [[Sources/2015ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance, under Peter Eastwood, reached $1 billion of annual premium volume less than three years after formation.

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses: Low Prices as Strategy
BHE and BNSF together accounted for 37% of Berkshire's after-tax operating earnings and invested $11.6 billion combined in plant and equipment. BHE's Iowa retail electricity rate (6.8¢/KWH) undercut every neighboring state's utility (Alliant 9.5¢, Nebraska 9.0¢, national average 10.4¢); BNSF's revenue per ton-mile (just under 3¢) ran at least 40% below the other four major U.S. railroads (4.2¢–5.3¢). BHE reached 47% of Iowa's retail electricity from wind. [[Cases/BNSF]] and [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]] earnings tables for 2013–2015 are reported in full.

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: Attacking Fake "Adjusted" Earnings
This segment posted $107.825 billion revenue and $7.115 billion pre-tax earnings for 2015 (up from $6.792 billion in 2014). Buffett again separates real depreciation from non-cash purchase-accounting amortization, but goes further this year, attacking the practice of managements telling investors to ignore stock-based compensation as an expense:

> "'Stock-based compensation' is the most egregious example." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

He also faults Wall Street analysts who repeat management's "compensation-ignoring" adjusted-earnings figures. The segment employed an average $25.6 billion of net tangible assets during 2015, earning 18.4% after-tax.

### Finance and Financial Products: Clayton's Mortgage Practices Under Scrutiny
Clayton Homes, under Kevin Clayton, was America's second-largest home builder in 2015, selling 34,397 homes (about 45% of manufactured homes bought by Americans) with a $12.8 billion mortgage portfolio and 100% risk retention on originated loans (it keeps every mortgage it writes). Buffett contrasts this with the securitized, no-skin-in-the-game mortgage practices that caused the 2008 financial crisis, quoting Congressman Barney Frank's assessment of the Dodd-Frank Act's one major weakness — the decision not to impose risk retention on all residential mortgages. Despite low-FICO, lower-income borrowers, Clayton's 2015 regulatory record across 65 federal/state examinations produced only $38,200 in fines and $704,678 in customer refunds, with 95.4% of borrowers current on payments at year-end. Marmon's railcar fleet expanded to 133,220 units, aided by a 25,085-car purchase from General Electric. Segment pre-tax earnings recap (in millions): Berkadia $74, Clayton $706, CORT $55, Marmon Containers and Cranes $192, Marmon Railcars $546, XTRA $172, net financial income $341, for a total of $2,086 versus $1,839 in 2014.

### Investments: The Big Four and Bank of America
The top-common-stock table at year-end 2015 (Kraft Heinz excluded, as it is accounted for by the equity method) included American Express ($1,287M cost / $10,545M market, 15.6%), AT&T (new position, $1,283M/$1,603M), Coca-Cola ($1,299M/$17,184M, 9.3%), IBM ($13,791M/$11,152M, 8.4%), Moody's ($248M/$2,475M, 12.6%), Phillips 66 ($4,357M/$4,530M, 10.5%), U.S. Bancorp ($3,239M/$4,346M), Wal-Mart ($3,593M/$3,893M) and Wells Fargo ($12,730M/$27,180M, 9.8%), for total common stocks of $58,612M cost / $112,338M market. The Bank of America warrants (700 million shares for $5 billion through September 2021) were worth $11.8 billion at year-end — Berkshire's fourth-largest equity position.

### Productivity and Prosperity
A long essay uses four case studies — 20th-century farming (farm employment fell from 40% to 2% of the workforce while corn output roughly quintupled), U.S. railroads (BNSF moved 71% more ton-miles in 2015 than in 1996 with only 4% more employees), the direct-distribution insurance model (GEICO's 1936 founding by Leo and Lillian Goodwin of USAA, its underwriting expenses of 14.7% of premiums in 2015 versus 25% for agency-model insurers like State Farm and Allstate), and Berkshire Hathaway Energy's Iowa and PacifiCorp utilities (no rate increase for 16 years in Iowa while industry rates rose 44%) — to argue that productivity gains, though broadly beneficial, impose real costs on displaced workers (citing Dexter Shoe's 1,600 laid-off Maine employees and the New Bedford textile mill's Portuguese-speaking older workers with "no Plan B"). Buffett's proposed remedy is not restraining productivity but a stronger safety net:

> "I personally favor a reformed and expanded Earned Income Tax Credit that would try to make sure America works for those willing to work." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Important Risks: Cyberattack and Climate Change
Buffett dismisses standard 10-K risk-factor disclosures as rarely useful, then names Berkshire's specific exposures (BNSF coal-volume decline, driverless cars eventually pressuring GEICO and auto dealerships, print-newspaper circulation decline, online retail). He identifies one risk beyond Berkshire's control: a successful cyber, biological, nuclear or chemical attack on the United States, quoting Einstein on the unknowability of a war fought with such weapons. Addressing a shareholder climate-change proposal, he argues Berkshire's one-year repriced P/C policies largely insulate it from climate risk (a worsening super-cat environment would likely make the insurance business "larger and more profitable," not weaker), while endorsing prompt action on the issue itself via an analogy to Pascal's Wager:

> "Call this Noah's Law: If an ark may be essential for survival, begin building it today, no matter how cloudless the skies appear." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### The Annual Meeting
For the first time, the meeting (Saturday, April 30, 2016) was webcast worldwide via Yahoo Finance, partly to manage record attendance (40,000+ in 2015 overflowed the CenturyLink Center) and partly, Buffett says candidly, so shareholders could observe an 85-year-old Buffett and 92-year-old Munger without needing to travel to Omaha.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Performance**: Book value per share +6.4%; 51-year compounded annual gain of 19.2%
- **Acquisitions**: Precision Castparts (~$32 billion cash, closed just before publication); Kraft Heinz merger completed, converting the Heinz stake to a 27% Kraft Heinz position
- **BNSF turnaround**: Record $5.8 billion capex reversed 2014's service problems; pre-tax income hit a record $6.8 billion
- **Insurance**: 13th consecutive year of underwriting profit; float grew to $87.722 billion
- **Big Four stakes increased**: American Express 15.6%, Coca-Cola 9.3%, IBM 8.4%, Wells Fargo 9.8%
- **New equity position**: AT&T added to the top-holdings table
- **Capital expenditure**: $16 billion companywide, 86% deployed in the United States
