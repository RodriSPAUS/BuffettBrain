# 2015 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire achieved a 6.4% gain in per-share book value in 2015, underperforming the S&P 500's 1.4% decline. The letter focused extensively on stock buybacks, explaining when they are beneficial or harmful to shareholders, and discussed Berkshire's approach to capital allocation. Buffett also addressed the company's insurance operations and investment philosophy.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Stock Buyback Philosophy
Buffett provided a comprehensive explanation of when stock buybacks benefit or harm shareholders, emphasizing that buybacks are only value-creating when shares trade below intrinsic value.

> "A repurchase makes sense if the price is below intrinsic value - and makes no sense if the price is above intrinsic value." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

> "Buybacks should be viewed with suspicion if a CEO engages in them when the stock trades above intrinsic value." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Intrinsic Value Calculation
The letter explained how to think about intrinsic value and why it's crucial for evaluating buyback decisions.

> "Intrinsic value is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Insurance Operations and Float Growth
Berkshire's insurance operations continued to generate substantial float, which grew to over $88 billion by the end of 2015.

> "Our insurance business continues to throw off large sums of money." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

> "Berkshire's float has grown to $88.4 billion." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Investment Philosophy and Process
The letter emphasized Berkshire's approach to investing in businesses with predictable economics and strong competitive positions.

> "We simply want to purchase into businesses whose economic characteristics are virtually certain." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

> "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Capital Allocation Excellence
Emphasis on Berkshire's approach to capital allocation, deploying capital where it can earn the highest returns over time.

> "Our goal is to find businesses that will deliver the highest after-tax returns over time." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Market Environment and Valuations
Discussion of market conditions and the challenge of finding attractively priced investments in the current environment.

> "Finding reasonably-priced businesses is more difficult now than it was a few years ago." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

> "When others are fearful, we are greedy." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Long-Term Business Ownership
Focus on long-term business ownership rather than short-term market timing, with the ideal holding period being forever for exceptional businesses.

> "We are interested in a business with good economics that we can understand." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Corporate Governance and Buybacks
The letter discussed the importance of management teams making buyback decisions based on value creation rather than self-interest.

> "Managers should act in the interests of all shareholders when considering buybacks." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Economic Reality vs. Accounting Numbers
Focus on economic reality rather than accounting metrics when making capital allocation decisions.

> "Accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Business Quality Assessment
The importance of focusing on business quality rather than market timing or macroeconomic predictions.

> "The key is to focus on business fundamentals." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

### Core Insights

#### Intrinsic Value Gap Widening
> "Today, the large – and growing – unrecorded gains at our 'winners' make it clear that Berkshire’s intrinsic value far exceeds its book value." [[p.3]]

- **Per-share investments**: $159,794 (+8.3% YoY, including Kraft Heinz at market)
- **Operating earnings**: $12,304/share (+2.1% YoY), now includes insurance underwriting income
- **Float growth**: $41B (2002) → $88B (2015), creating massive unbooked value
- **Repurchase threshold**: Delighted to repurchase shares at ≤120% of book value

#### Powerhouse Six Economics
> "Next year, I will be discussing the 'Powerhouse Six.' The newcomer will be Precision Castparts Corp. ('PCC'), a business that we purchased a month ago for more than $32 billion of cash." [[p.4]]

#### BNSF Service Recovery & Capital Discipline
> "After a poor performance in 2014, our BNSF railroad dramatically improved its service to customers last year. To attain that result, we invested about $5.8 billion during the year in capital expenditures..." [[p.3]]

- **Capital allocation discipline**: $5.8B spend = ~25% of estimated revenues vs. industry average of 18%
- **Performance metrics**: Maintained volume while competitors saw ton-miles fall
- **Economic impact**: Carries 17% of U.S. intercity freight (revenue ton-miles), 45% more than closest competitor

#### Big Four Equity Strategy
> "For the four companies in aggregate, each increase of one percentage point in our ownership raises Berkshire’s portion of their annual earnings by about $500 million." [[p.6]]

- **Ownership math**: 1% × $500M = $500M annual earnings impact across four companies
- **Unrealized gains**: $42B+ at yearend
- **Strategic flexibility**: "Appetite for either operating businesses or passive investments doubles our chances" [[p.6]]

#### Economic Fortress Architecture
> "The nature of our insurance contracts is such that we can never be subject to immediate or near-term demands for sums that are of significance to our cash resources. This structure is by design and is a key component in the strength of Berkshire’s economic fortress." [[p.10]]

- **Catastrophe resilience**: $250B industry loss would likely produce Berkshire profit due to diversified earnings streams
- **Cash preservation**: No immediate demands on cash resources despite massive liabilities
- **Reinvestment advantage**: $16B total capital expenditures in 2015, 86% in U.S.
