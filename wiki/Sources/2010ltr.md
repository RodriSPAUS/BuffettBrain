# 2010 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 13.0%, with the year's headline being the first full
year of Burlington Northern Santa Fe under Berkshire ownership — a purchase
Buffett says increased "normal" pre-tax earning power by nearly 40%. The
letter is organized as a fuller-than-usual primer: a "Performance" section
that walks new BNSF-era shareholders through 42 rolling five-year comparisons
against the S&P, an "Intrinsic Value" section that measures two of its three
components directly, and the full 60-year GEICO story told from Buffett's own
1951 visit to the company. Two long essays close out the technical sections —
a sustained argument that reported net income is "almost always meaningless"
at Berkshire, and a full statement of the company's aversion to leverage,
illustrated by a 1939 letter
from Buffett's grandfather Ernest. Lou Simpson's retirement and Todd Combs's
hiring mark the first concrete step in the investment-succession search begun
in [[Sources/2006ltr]]. The letter closes with Buffett's biennial memo to
Berkshire's managers on reputation and succession, reproduced in full.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### BNSF, and a normal-earning-power estimate

> "It now appears that owning this railroad will increase Berkshire's 'normal'
> earning power by nearly 40% pre-tax and by well over 30% after-tax."
> — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

Buffett estimates the normal earning power of Berkshire's current assets at
about $17 billion pre-tax, $12 billion after-tax, excluding capital gains —
assuming a year free of insurance mega-catastrophe and a business climate
"somewhat better than that of 2010 but weaker than that of 2005 or 2006."
Capital spending reached $6 billion in 2010 (90% inside the U.S.) and was
budgeted to reach $8 billion in 2011, with the entire increase domestic.
Against talk of "great uncertainty," Buffett names three dates — December 6,
1941, October 18, 1987, September 10, 2001 — as evidence that tomorrow is
always uncertain, and restates his standing optimism: "America's best days lie
ahead."

### Performance: 42 five-year periods, and a shrinking edge admitted early

Buffett restates the discipline behind fixing a standard before results
arrive:

> "Lacking such standards, managements are tempted to shoot the arrow of
> performance" and paint the bull's-eye around wherever it lands.
> — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

A table recasts the 46-year record into 42 overlapping five-year periods:
Berkshire's best relative years, on this view, ended in the early 1980s; the
market's own golden run came in the 17 years that followed, during which
Berkshire's absolute returns stayed strong even as the relative edge narrowed;
after 1999 the market stalled and Berkshire's satisfactory relative results
have translated into only moderate absolute ones. The size confession from
[[Sources/2009ltr]] is repeated and extended: Buffett writes that "bountiful
years, we want to emphasize, will never return." GEICO's $900 million 2010
advertising budget —
spent on policyholders who deliver no immediate profit — is offered as the
clearest example of decisions Berkshire is willing to make against short-term
results.

retained earnings will be redeployed:

A dollar of then-value in the hands of Sears Roebuck's or Montgomery Ward's
CEOs in the late 1960s, Buffett writes, met a far different fate than a dollar
entrusted to Sam Walton. — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

### Berkshire's structural advantages, named directly

Three advantages partially offset the drag of size. First, managers who are
often independently wealthy and stay purely because they love the work:

They are volunteers rather than mercenaries, Buffett writes:

> "Because no one can offer them a job they would enjoy more, they can't be
> lured away." — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

Second, capital-allocation flexibility unconstrained by any single industry —
Buffett's own 1965 counter-example is his early insistence on reinvesting in
the failing textile mill rather than looking elsewhere, capped by "in a final
burst of brilliance, I went out and bought another textile company.
Aaaaaaargh!" Third, a culture that "self-propagates," quoting Churchill on
buildings shaping their occupants and noting Berkshire's directors carry no
D&O liability insurance and hold more than $3 billion of stock bought with
their own money — "if they mess up with your money, they will lose their
money as well." World Headquarters' annual rent is disclosed at $270,212,
against $301,363 of total home-office furniture and equipment investment.

### GEICO: the full sixty-year story

Buffett tells his own 1951 origin story as the frame for a broader lesson on
intrinsic value exceeding book value. A 20-year-old graduate student who
learned Ben Graham chaired GEICO, he took a Saturday train to a closed
Washington headquarters and was let in by a janitor, who pointed him to the
only person present:

> "That was my lucky moment. During the next four hours, 'Davy' gave me an
> education about both insurance and GEICO." — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

Lorimer Davidson's tutorial made GEICO 75% of Buffett's own $9,800 portfolio
soon after. Davidson later became CEO, and after his retirement GEICO nearly
failed in the mid-1970s, its stock falling more than 95% before Berkshire
bought about a third of the company for $46 million, a position that grew to
50% through GEICO's own buybacks with no additional Berkshire purchases.
Berkshire bought the remaining half at the start of 1996, prompting the
95-year-old Davidson to record a video welcoming GEICO's permanent home at
Berkshire, playfully adding that next time, Warren should make an appointment.

Buffett walks through the goodwill math from the 1996 purchase in detail: the
deal implied a $4.6 billion value for 100% of GEICO against $1.9 billion of
tangible net worth, putting goodwill at $2.7 billion — about 97% of the $2.8
billion GEICO's customers paid in 1995 premiums. Carried on the books at a
fixed $1.4 billion (goodwill is written down for declines but never marked up
for gains), that same 97%-of-premium yardstick applied to 2010's $14.3 billion
of premium volume implies a real economic goodwill of about $14 billion:

GEICO, off to a strong start in 2011, is the gift that keeps giving.
— [[Sources/2010ltr]]

Under Tony Nicely, market share reached 8.8% (from 2.0% in 1993), and a
homeowners agency built on top of the auto-insurance relationship sold 769,898
new policies in 2010, up 34%. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

Ajit Jain's operation, from a standing start in 1985, had reached $30
billion of float, adding a new $2 billion annual life-reinsurance premium
stream during the year. Buffett states the four disciplines a sound insurer
must observe — understanding exposures, conservatively pricing them, setting
a profitable premium, and walking away when that premium can't be had — and
names the failure mode most insurers succumb to on the fourth: matching a
rival's underpriced business just to keep pace is trouble in any business, he
writes, but none more so than insurance. Tad Montross's General Re is
credited with observing all four. See [[Concepts/Float]] and
[[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: record-breakers and a turnaround

Four businesses set earnings records: TTI (Paul Andrews) with sales 21% above
its 2008 peak; Forest River (Pete Liegl), nearly $2 billion of sales across 82
plants Buffett admits he has never visited; CTB (Vic Mancinelli), sales per
employee up from $189,365 to $405,878 since the 2002 purchase; and H. H. Brown
(Jim Issler), a new sales and earnings record helped along by 1,110 pairs of
shoes sold at the annual meeting.

**NetJets** completed its turnaround under Dave Sokol: after an 11-year
aggregate pre-tax loss of $157 million through 2009 — understated, Buffett
notes, because Berkshire's credit had subsidized the company's true borrowing
cost — NetJets earned $207 million pre-tax in 2010, a $918 million swing,
even after absorbing a new $38 million arm's-length fee for Berkshire's
guarantee. Marmon (130 businesses, Frank Ptak) is the sector's largest
earner, with Berkshire's stake rising to 80% via a scheduled $1.5 billion
Pritzker-family purchase, full ownership to follow in 2013 or 2014 at the
family's choice. McLane entered wine and spirits distribution via the Empire
Distributors acquisition, led by David Kahn. Iscar's profits rose 159%.
Construction-linked businesses (Johns Manville, MiTek, Shaw, Acme Brick)
earned a combined $362 million pre-tax against $1.3 billion in 2006, with
employment down about 9,400 — yet all four continued bolt-on acquisitions and
capital projects through the trough, on the premise that "our time horizon is
forever."

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses: a new combined sector

BNSF and MidAmerican are given their own reporting sector for the first time,
united by huge investment in long-lived regulated assets funded by debt
Berkshire does not guarantee, and by a shared "social compact" with
regulators and the public. BNSF moves about 28% of the U.S. rail industry's
ton-miles, over 11% of all inter-city freight ton-miles by any mode, across
23,000 miles of track; in a recessionary year with car loadings well off
peak, interest coverage still ran 6:1. MidAmerican's pipelines carry 8% of
U.S. natural gas — Northern Natural rose from dead-last (43rd of 43) in
industry rankings shortly after its 2002 purchase to second place, behind only
Berkshire's own Kern River. Iowa electricity rates have not risen since
MidAmerican's 1999 purchase, while a competing in-state utility raised prices
more than 70% over the same period; wind capacity was set to reach 2,909
megawatts by the end of 2011, more than any other regulated utility, funded
by MidAmerican's practice of retaining all its earnings. See
[[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Finance and Financial Products: leasing recovers, Clayton's loss record

XTRA's equipment utilization rose from 63% to 75%, lifting pre-tax earnings
from $17 million to $35 million; CORT swung from a $3 million loss to $18
million of profit. At Clayton, industry-wide manufactured-home output fell to
50,046 units (Clayton's share: 47%), against a 1998 peak of 372,843. Clayton's
own 200,804 originated mortgages carried an average FICO score of 648 at
origination, with 47% at 640 or below — yet losses stayed contained:

| Year | Net Losses (% of Average Loans) |
| --- | --- |
| 2006 | 1.53% |
| 2007 | 1.27% |
| 2008 | 1.17% |
| 2009 | 1.86% |
| 2010 | 1.72% |

Buffett attributes the resilience to Clayton retaining its own originations
rather than securitizing them, plus sensible down payments and income-geared
payments: "That concentrates the mind." He restates the home-financing policy
lesson from [[Sources/2008ltr]] and [[Sources/2009ltr]]: "Our country's social
goal should not be to put families into the house of their dreams, but rather
to put them into a house they can afford."

### Succession on the investment side: Lou Simpson retires, Todd Combs joins

Lou Simpson, GEICO's investment manager since 1979, announced his retirement
at 74. Buffett's tribute is understated by design: "I have omitted updates
only because his performance made mine look bad." The search begun four years
earlier in [[Sources/2006ltr]] produced Todd Combs, hired on the same
performance-based structure as Simpson — salary plus a contingent payment tied
to results relative to the S&P, with deferrals designed to prevent the
hedge-fund pattern Buffett criticizes directly:

> "Investors who put money with such managers should be labeled patsies, not
> partners." — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

Combs would initially manage $1-3 billion, reset annually, with no restriction
to a single investing style: "At Berkshire our only style box is 'smart.'"
Buffett dismisses commentary that Combs was "little-known": "I wonder how many
of them would have known of Lou in 1979, Ajit in 1985, or, for that matter,
Charlie in 1959." See [[People/WarrenBuffett]].

### Derivatives: 203 contracts, and a Black-Scholes mismatch made concrete

Berkshire's personally-managed derivative book fell from 251 contracts
([[Sources/2008ltr]]) to 203. The high-yield credit-default category, written
2004-2008 and covering five-year exposure to 100-company baskets, had by 2010
paid out $2.5 billion of losses against $3.4 billion of premiums received —
Buffett judges an underwriting profit now "almost certain," plus interest-free
float averaging about $2 billion over the contracts' life. Equity put
contracts were reduced from 47 to 39 after an early unwind of eight contracts
at the counterparty's request, netting Berkshire a $222 million gain. The
remaining 39 contracts carried $4.2 billion of premiums received against a
yearend balance-sheet liability of $6.7 billion — versus a "settlement value"
of only $3.8 billion if index levels held flat from December 31, 2010, a gap
Buffett expects to close in Berkshire's favor as equity prices rise.

A single-quarter illustration makes the Black-Scholes critique from
[[Sources/2008ltr]] concrete: had the equity puts terminated June 30, 2010,
Berkshire would have owed $6.4 billion; by September 30 the settlement figure
had fallen to $5.8 billion as markets rose, yet the formula's balance-sheet
liability *rose* over the same period from $8.9 billion to $9.6 billion,
cutting reported quarterly net income by $455 million. Buffett states plainly
why the company keeps using a formula it distrusts:

> "We would be accused of shoddy accounting if we deviated from it."
> — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

> "We would rather be approximately right than precisely wrong."
> — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

He extends the critique into a broader complaint about finance education,
quoting John Kenneth Galbraith on economists making their graduate-school
ideas "last a lifetime," and recalling how efficient-market theorists
dismissed contrary evidence as "anomalies" through the 1970s and 1980s. See
[[Applications/RedFlags]].

### On Reporting and Misreporting: why net income is ignored

Buffett's fullest statement of why Berkshire treats reported net income as
close to meaningless: realized gains and losses flow through it while
unrealized ones (mostly) don't, so a year with large unrealized gains and a
small realized loss can report worse "earnings" than a year that was
economically far better.

Regardless of how Berkshire's businesses might be doing, Buffett writes, he
and Charlie could — quite legally —

> "cause net income" — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

in any given period to be almost any number they would like.

Buffett states the two of them have never sold a security to manage the
reported number, calls the 1990s-era corporate habit of "game playing" with
earnings a practice they hold "a deep disgust" for, and directs attention
instead to operating earnings and book value. See [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### Life and Debt

The letter's fullest statement of Berkshire's aversion to leverage opens with
a racing metaphor — "to finish first, you must first finish" — and a warning
about leverage's addictive quality once it has worked:

> "As we all learned in third grade – and some relearned in 2008" — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

any series of positive numbers, however impressive, evaporates once
multiplied by a single zero.

Buffett prints a 1939 letter from his grandfather Ernest to his uncle Fred,
illustrating a lesson about liquidity that predates any business-school
training — Ernest never finished high school — and states Berkshire's own
policy directly descended from it: a pledge to hold at least $10 billion of
cash outside the utility and railroad businesses, with $20 billion customarily
kept on hand to survive both an unprecedented insurance loss (Katrina, at
roughly $3 billion, remains the largest to date) and to seize opportunities
during financial turmoil. He credits investment writer Ray DeVoe: "More money
has been lost reaching for yield than at the point of a gun." Forty years
without a dividend or share repurchase have taken net worth from $48 million
to $157 billion — the discipline that let Berkshire deploy $15.6 billion in
the 25 days of panic following the Lehman bankruptcy.

### The biennial memo to managers: reputation and succession

The letter reprints Buffett's periodic memo to Berkshire's operating CEOs in
full. Its central instruction, repeated for more than 25 years:

> "We can afford to lose money – even a lot of money. But we can't afford to
> lose reputation – even a shred of reputation." — [[Sources/2010ltr]]

The test for a borderline action is publicity, not legality: whether Buffett
would be comfortable seeing it "written about on the front page of a national
newspaper in an article written by" an unfriendly but intelligent reporter.
"Everybody else is doing it" is named as an unacceptable justification, and
any hesitation about propriety is treated as a signal to stop: if an action's
propriety is questionable, Buffett writes, "just assume it" is outside the
line "and forget it." The memo also renews an annual succession-planning
request — each manager is asked to send a private letter naming who should
take over if they were incapacitated overnight — and closes with a request
that managers decline, on Buffett's behalf, requests to speak or make
introductions to the Gates Foundation. See [[Principles/Fidelity]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- Increased Marmon ownership toward 80% via a scheduled $1.5 billion
  Pritzker-family purchase; full ownership planned for 2013-2014.
- McLane entered wine and spirits distribution via the Empire Distributors
  acquisition.
- Bolt-on acquisitions at MiTek (five during the year) and Acme Brick
  (Alabama's leading brick manufacturer, $50 million).
- **Todd Combs** hired as the first addition to the investment-succession
  bench identified in [[Sources/2006ltr]] and [[Sources/2007ltr]], initially
  managing $1-3 billion.

**Portfolio**

- No major new equity positions; existing Big Four and secondary holdings
  held largely steady.
- Unwound eight equity-put contracts at counterparty request for a $222
  million gain; equity put book reduced from 47 to 39 contracts.
- Swiss Re note redeemed; Goldman Sachs and General Electric preferred stock
  redemptions expected by yearend 2011, for about $1.4 billion combined call
  premiums.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew to $65.832 billion at a $2.013 billion underwriting profit, the
  eighth consecutive profitable year.
- Ajit Jain added a new $2 billion annual life-reinsurance premium stream.
- MidAmerican's wind capacity set to reach 2,909 megawatts by end of 2011, the
  most of any regulated U.S. utility.

**Corporate**

- Lou Simpson retired after 31 years managing GEICO's equity portfolio.
- BNSF and MidAmerican combined into a new "Regulated, Capital-Intensive
  Businesses" reporting sector.
