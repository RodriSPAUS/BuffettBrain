# 1994 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1994: net worth up 13.9%. The letter contains the definition of
intrinsic value that this wiki uses, the college-education analogy that explains
why book value misleads, and the most direct attack in the collection on how
acquisitions are actually justified inside companies.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Sources of Reported Earnings, and look-through earnings past $1 billion

The 1994 segment table (Berkshire's after-tax share, in thousands) shows Insurance Underwriting $80,860, Insurance Net Investment Income $350,453, Buffalo News $31,685, Fechheimer $7,107, Finance Businesses $14,293, Kirby $27,719, Nebraska Furniture Mart $8,652, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing $24,909, See's Candies $28,247, Shoe Group $55,750, World Book $17,275, plus Sales of Securities of $61,138 and a $172,579 charge for the Decline in Value of USAir Preferred Stock, for Total Earnings — All Entities of $494,798 versus $688,121 in 1993.

Look-through earnings crossed $1 billion for the first time — $1,030 million, up from $841 million — with new investee lines for American Express (5.5% owned), Gannett (4.9%), and PNC Bank (8.3%) joining the table. Buffett flags that the gain was flattered by an abnormally profitable year in the super-cat business, which he addresses at length in the next section.

### Insurance Operations: float past $3 billion, and a $600 million worst case

Berkshire's cost-of-float table now runs through 1994: average float of $3,056.6 million against an underwriting profit, for a cost of float again "less than zero." Buffett repeats the caution he gave in 1993 — "Though we have a fine insurance business, it is not as good as it currently looks." [[Sources/1994ltr]] — since the super-cat line had its second consecutive abnormally profitable year; the only meaningful 1994 loss came from the January California earthquake. He illustrates Berkshire's scale advantage with a real transaction: a primary insurer wanted a same-day quote on $400 million of California earthquake coverage, and Berkshire wrote it immediately. [[Concepts/Float]]

> "All things considered, we believe our worst-case insurance loss from a super-cat is now about $600 million after-tax, an amount that would slightly exceed Berkshire's annual earnings from other sources. If you are not comfortable with this level of exposure, the time to sell your Berkshire stock is now, not after the inevitable mega-catastrophe." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

### Intrinsic value, defined

The sentence that anchors [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]:

- "We define intrinsic value as the discounted value of the cash that can be taken
  out of a business during its remaining life."
- With the caveat attached: "Anyone calculating intrinsic value necessarily comes up
  with a highly subjective figure that will change both as estimates of future cash
  flows are revised and as interest rates move."
- And why it is used anyway: "Despite its fuzziness, however, intrinsic value is
  all-important and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness
  of investments and businesses."

### Why book value misleads, in both directions

- The honest statement of what book value is: "We regularly report our per-share
  book value, an easily calculable number, though one of limited use."
- Berkshire in 1964: "we could state with certitude that Berkshire's per-share book
  value was $19.46. However, that figure considerably overstated the stock's
  intrinsic value since all of the company's resources were tied up in a
  sub-profitable textile business."
- The joke that makes the point: "anyone inquiring into the soundness of Berkshire's
  balance sheet might well have deserved the answer once offered up by a Hollywood
  mogul of dubious reputation: "Don't worry, the liabilities are solid.""
- And the reversal: "Today, Berkshire's situation has reversed: Many of the
  businesses we control are worth far more than their carrying value."
- Why it is still reported: "they serve as a rough, albeit significantly understated,
  tracking measure for Berkshire's intrinsic value."

The college-education analogy follows: cost is the "book value" (and should include
foregone earnings), lifetime earnings differential is the intrinsic value, and the
two need not be related at all. See [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]].

### The acquisition critique

The sharpest sustained passage in the letter, and it names the mechanism rather
than complaining about outcomes.

- The wrong test: "in contemplating business mergers and acquisitions, many managers
  tend to focus on whether the transaction is immediately dilutive or anti-dilutive
  to earnings per share (or, at financial institutions, to per-share book value). An
  emphasis of this sort carries great dangers."
- The analogy that exposes it: "imagine that a 25-year-old first-year MBA student is
  considering merging his future economic interests with those of a 25-year-old day
  laborer. The MBA student, a non-earner, would find that a "share-for-share" merger
  of his equity interest in himself with that of the day laborer would enhance his
  near-term earnings (in a big way!). But what could be sillier for the student than
  a deal of this kind?"
- What Berkshire does instead: "At Berkshire, we have rejected many merger and
  purchase opportunities that would have boosted current and near-term earnings but
  that would have reduced per-share intrinsic value."
- The distribution of spoils: "The sad fact is that most major acquisitions display
  an egregious imbalance: They are a bonanza for the shareholders of the acquiree;
  they increase the income and status of the acquirer's management; and they are a
  honey pot for the investment bankers and other professionals on both sides. But,
  alas, they usually reduce the wealth of the acquirer's shareholders"
- The reason, stated as arithmetic: "That happens because the acquirer typically
  gives up more intrinsic value than it receives."
- On who gets asked: "often the CEO asks a strategic planning staff, consultants or
  investment bankers whether an acquisition or two might make sense. That's like
  asking your interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug."
- And on the person asking: "Many CEO's attain their positions in part because they
  possess an abundance of animal spirits and ego. If an executive is heavily endowed
  with these qualities... they won't disappear when he reaches the top."
- The confession that closes it: ""Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one.""

This is the [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] page's core argument, and every item is
a [[Applications/RedFlags]] entry.

### Ignoring the macro

- "We will continue to ignore political and economic forecasts, which are an
  expensive distraction for many investors and businessmen."
- The list of things nobody predicted: "Thirty years ago, no one could have foreseen
  the huge expansion of the Vietnam War, wage and price controls, two oil shocks,
  the resignation of a president, the dissolution of the Soviet Union, a one-day drop
  in the Dow of 508 points, or treasury bill yields fluctuating between 2.8% and
  17.4%."
- And why it did not matter: "But, surprise - none of these blockbuster events made
  the slightest dent in Ben Graham's investment principles."
- The counterfactual cost: "Imagine the cost to us, then, if we had let a fear of
  unknowns cause us to defer or alter the deployment of capital."
- The inversion: "Indeed, we have usually made our best purchases when apprehensions
  about some macro event were at a peak. Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the
  friend of the fundamentalist."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]].

### The happy zone

- The constraint: "A fat wallet, however, is the enemy of superior investment
  results."
- Made concrete: "We now consider a security for purchase only if we believe we can
  deploy at least $100 million in it. Given that minimum, Berkshire's investment
  universe has shrunk dramatically."
- And the discipline that follows, borrowed from Ted Williams: "My argument is, to be
  a good hitter, you've got to get a good ball to hit. It's the first rule in the
  book. If I have to bite at stuff that is out of my happy zone, I'm not a .344
  hitter. I might only be a .250 hitter."
- "Charlie and I agree and will try to wait for opportunities that are well within
  our own "happy zone.""

The earliest full statement in these letters of what the wiki calls circle of
competence. See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

### Compensation follows the same logic as capital allocation

- "At Berkshire, we try to be as logical about compensation as about capital
  allocation. For example, we compensate Ralph Schey based upon the results of Scott
  Fetzer rather than those of Berkshire."
- The rhetorical question: "What could make more sense, since he's responsible" for
  Scott Fetzer and not for Berkshire's stock price.

The same principle argued at length in the 1985 letter against fixed-price options.
See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The stated formula, and the stated limit

- What still works: "our formula - the purchase at sensible prices of businesses
  that have good underlying economics and are run by honest and able people - is
  certain to produce reasonable success."
- What does not: "One we will confidently offer, however, is that the future
  performance of Berkshire won't come close to matching the performance of the
  past."
- The cause, again: "Berkshire now has a net worth of $11.9 billion compared to
  about $22 million when Charlie and I began to manage the company."

### How capital moves through Berkshire

The clearest statement of the internal mechanism:

- "At Berkshire, our managers will continue to earn extraordinary returns from what
  appear to be ordinary businesses. As a first step, these managers will look for
  ways to deploy their earnings advantageously in their businesses. What's left,
  they will send to Charlie and me. We then will try to use those funds in ways that
  build per-share intrinsic value."
- With the acquisition criteria stated as economics rather than as a checklist: "Our
  goal will be to acquire either part or all of businesses that we believe we
  understand, that have good, sustainable underlying economics, and that are run by
  managers whom we like, admire and trust."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] — this is the reinvestment hierarchy in one
paragraph.

### Common Stock Investments: the portfolio, and four decades of personal history

The over-$300-million common stock list at 12/31/94 (cost/market, in thousands): American Express $723,919/$818,918 (a new position, added to during the year), Capital Cities/ABC $345,000/$1,705,000, Coca-Cola $1,298,888/$5,150,000 (increased to 100,000,000 shares), Freddie Mac $270,468/$644,441, Gannett $335,216/$365,002 (new), GEICO $45,713/$1,678,250, Gillette $600,000/$1,797,000, PNC Bank $503,046/$410,951 (new), Washington Post $9,731/$418,983, Wells Fargo $423,680/$984,727.

Buffett traces the American Express position back to his own investing history — Buffett Partnership Ltd. put 40% of its capital into Amex after the mid-1960s salad-oil scandal — and links it to a broader pattern of his best ideas tracing back to a personal association: his first stock sale was GEICO in 1951, half his early capital came from delivering the Washington Post, and his first business was reselling Coca-Cola six-packs bottle by bottle as a boy.

> "As for Coca-Cola, my first business venture - this was in the 1930's - was buying a six-pack of Coke for 25 cents and selling each bottle for 5 cents. It took only fifty years before I finally got it: The real money was in the syrup." [[Sources/1994ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

### Mistake Du Jour: selling Cap Cities early, and the USAir writedown

Two nominees compete for the year's mistake award. The silver medal: selling 10 million Cap Cities shares at $63 in late 1993, only to watch the price close 1994 at $85.25 — a repeat, Buffett notes, of selling the same stock at $4.30 in 1978-80 before rebuying at $17.25 in 1986.

> "Late in 1993 I sold 10 million shares of Cap Cities at $63; at year-end 1994, the price was $85.25. (The difference is $222.5 million for those of you who wish to avoid the pain of calculating the damage yourself.)... Now I've become a repeat offender." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

Top honors go to the USAir preferred, whose dividend was suspended in September 1994. Buffett attributes the original 1989 "unforced error" to sloppy analysis of an industry whose regulatory-era cost structure could not survive deregulation, and writes the $358 million position down to $89.5 million (25 cents on the dollar):

> "Whatever the outcome, we will heed a prime rule of investing: You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

Buffett and Munger declined to stand for re-election to USAir's board at the next annual meeting.

### Miscellaneous: two retirements, and contributions

Dan Burke of Capital Cities/ABC and Carl Reichardt of Wells Fargo both retired in 1994, each credited with steering their companies to record earnings through difficult industry conditions before departing. Contributions participation was 95.7% of eligible shares, distributing $10.4 million to 3,300 charities. The only 1994 acquisition was a small retail shoe chain; a proposed merger with a large family-controlled business, to be paid partly in Berkshire convertible preferred, fell through.
