# 1961 Partnership Letter Summary

The fifth full year, and the letter that turns the partnership from a record for
the scoreboard into a *method*. The partnerships gained 45.9% against the Dow's
22.2% — compounding to 251.0% versus 74.3% since 1957 — and for the first time
Buffett writes out, category by category, the three avenues he actually invests
in. It is also the letter that names [[Cases/DempsterMill]] as a control
situation in progress and, unusually for him, commits to a ten-year prediction
("And a Prediction") whose shape defines his whole approach to down markets.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The scorecard, and pro-forma partner results

1961: the Dow +22.2% (dividends included); the full-year partnerships +45.9%
on average. The five-year table — 1957 +10.4% / 1958 +40.9% / 1959 +25.9% /
1960 +22.8% / 1961 +45.9% against the Dow's −8.4%, +38.5%, +19.9%, −6.3%,
+22.2% — compounds to **251.0% versus 74.3%**. Because the early partnerships
had different fee splits, he restates everything pro-forma under the current
Buffett Partnership, Ltd. agreement: the limited partners' 1961 gain was 35.9%,
compounded 181.6% against the Dow's 74.3%.

### A Word About Par — the yardstick chosen before the game

> "The rub, then, is in being sure that we all have the same ideas of what is
> good and what is poor. I believe in establishing yardsticks prior to the act;
> retrospectively, almost anything can be made to look good in relation to
> something or other." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

Buffett defends the Dow as a fair, tough competitor, not a straw man: of the 38
mutual funds he judged comparable, 32 did *worse* than the Dow over 1946–60,
and the six that beat it did so by only a few percentage points a year. He also
fixes the test length: three years is "a very minimal test"; the best test is a
period where the terminal Dow is close to the starting one.

### The method, stated for the first time in full

The letter's real contribution is the formal division of the portfolio into
three categories with different behavior against the Dow:

- **Generals** — generally undervalued securities, the largest category and
  where most money has been made. Five or six positions of 5–10% of assets plus
  ten or fifteen smaller ones; no timetable, no say in corporate policy; they
  behave in sympathy with the Dow. The margin argument is stated crisply:

> "A lot of value can be obtained for the price paid. This substantial excess
> of value creates a comfortable margin of safety in each transaction."
> [[Sources/1961ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

- **Work-outs** — securities whose result depends on corporate action (mergers,
  liquidations, reorganizations, spin-offs, oil sell-outs) rather than supply
  and demand, so their outcome follows a timetable. "Reasonably stable earnings
  from year to year" regardless of the Dow — a different kind of safety — and
  the only place he borrows, with a self-imposed limit of 25% of partnership
  net worth.
- **Control situations** — buy enough to influence policy; "Such operations
  should definitely be measured on the basis of several years." A general can
  graduate into a control if the price stays low long enough; if it runs up
  first, he sells at a higher level and it was a general after all. 

### Dempster Mill Manufacturing — revealed as the third category in action

> "Our first stock was purchased as a generally undervalued security five years
> ago. A block later became available, and I went on the Board about four years
> ago. In August 1961, we obtained majority control, which is indicative of the
> fact that many of our operations are not exactly of the 'overnight' variety."
> [[Sources/1961ltr]] [[Cases/DempsterMill]]

The numbers are given as the case opens: ~$9 million sales in 1961, book value
$4.5 million ($75 per share), working capital about $50 a share, the interest
valued at $35 a share against an average cost of about $28 — 21% of partnership
net assets. The valuation rule is the point, and it is a fiduciary one:

> "The estimated value should not be what we hope it would be worth, or what it
> might be worth to an eager buyer, etc., but what I would estimate our interest
> would bring if sold under current conditions in a reasonably short period of
> time." [[Sources/1961ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### The Question of Conservatism — defined by knowledge, not convention

Both popular forms of conservatism fail his test: long bonds have "produced
substantial market depreciation in many cases" and blue chips bought at any
price mean speculating on the multiplier. The standard is doubled:

> "You will not be right simply because a large number of people momentarily
> agree with you. You will not be right simply because important people agree
> with you." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

He offers the evidence he has: never a realized loss of more than 0.5% of 1% of
total net assets, and realized gains to realized losses roughly 100 to 1 — with
the honest caveat that the markets favored him.

### The Question of Size — where scale helps

Larger sums hurt "passive" positions slightly and help control situations
decisively, because the ante mounts and competition falls away:

> "A 'Sanborn Map' cannot be accomplished without the wherewithal."
> [[Sources/1961ltr]] [[Cases/SanbornMap]]

His present opinion: the two forces offset, and 1960–61 results "would not have
been better if we had been operating with the much smaller sums of 1956 and
1957."

### And a Prediction — the exception that defines the rule

The only structural forecast in the corpus so far, and hedged that way on
purpose: he will not predict what the market does in one or two years ("I don't
have the faintest idea"), but over a decade the Dow should compound at something
like 5% to 7%, and the right ambition is ten points a year better than that:

> "Our job is to pile up yearly advantages over the performance of the Dow
> without worrying too much about whether the absolute results in a given year
> are a plus or a minus." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

Concretely: down 15–20% in a year the market is down 35–40%; up about ten points
in a flat year; struggling to match a year up 20% or more — a profile that
compounds to "15% to 17% per year" if the Dow delivers its 5–7%. The closing
clause is the honest one: the variation hit the pleasant side in 1961. "They
won't all be!" On this he is not joking, and [[Sources/1962ltr]] begins by
reprinting the whole section.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Dempster Mill Manufacturing — control begun.** Started as a general in
  1956, board seat ~1958, majority control August 1961; 70% owned with another
  10% held by associates. Valued at $35 per share (book $75, working capital
  ~$50) against a $28 average cost — 21% of net assets. See [[Cases/DempsterMill]].
- **Results:** +45.9% for the full-year partnerships against the Dow's +22.2%;
  limited partners +35.9% pro-forma. Compounded since 1957: +251.0% vs +74.3%.
- **Structure:** overhead budgeted below 0.5 of 1% of net assets; 90+ partners,
  40 or so securities; net assets at the start of 1962 of $7,178,500; Buffett
  family interest $1,025,000 plus relatives' $782,600; minimum for new partners
  under review.
- **Forecast:** target of ten points a year over the Dow, in the shape laid out
  in "And a Prediction."
