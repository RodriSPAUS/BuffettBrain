# 2012 Annual Letter

Book value per share rose 14.4% — a "subpar" year against the S&P for the
ninth time in 48 years, Buffett notes without softening it, since the streak
of never losing a rolling five-year comparison is now at risk after four
straight up years for the index. The letter's two long essays are new
territory for the corpus: "We Buy Some Newspapers... Newspapers?" explains
why Berkshire bought 28 dailies in a business Buffett has spent two decades
telling shareholders is structurally declining, and a "Dividends" section
works a full numerical comparison between a dividend policy and a
share-sell-off policy to explain why Berkshire pays neither. No major
acquisition closed in 2012 itself, but the Heinz deal — agreed in February
2013 alongside Jorge Paulo Lemann's investor group — is reported as news
just breaking as the letter goes to print.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A subpar year, named as such, and the record so far

> "For the ninth time in 48 years, Berkshire's percentage increase in book
> value was less than the S&P's percentage gain" — [[Sources/2012ltr]]

(a calculation that includes dividends as well as price appreciation).

Eight of those nine shortfalls came in years the S&P gained 15% or more —
"we do better when the wind is in our face." The 43-times-unbeaten five-year
record (first shown in [[Sources/2010ltr]]) held again, but Buffett flags
that four consecutive up years for the S&P mean "if the market continues to
advance in 2013, our streak of five-year wins will end." He restates the
standing promise regardless of outcome: book value stays the yardstick,
because Berkshire's job is to beat the S&P over time or add nothing to what
a shareholder could get from an index fund alone.

### Heinz, reported as breaking news

No major acquisition closed during 2012 itself — Buffett names it directly
as the year's second disappointment — but by the time the letter went to
press, Berkshire had agreed to buy 50% of a new holding company owning all
of H. J. Heinz, partnered with Jorge Paulo Lemann's investor group. Each side
contributes about $4 billion of common equity; Berkshire separately invests
$8 billion in 9%-dividend preferred stock carrying redemption and warrant
features that add materially to its value. Buffett frames the roughly $12
billion combined outlay as absorbing "much of what Berkshire earned last
year." He promises the search for further "elephants" continues.

### The powerhouse five, and a record year for bolt-ons

BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon and MidAmerican Energy — the "powerhouse five"
— delivered $10.1 billion pre-tax combined, about $600 million more than
2011, meeting the prior year's forecast. Only MidAmerican was owned eight
years earlier, at $393 million pre-tax; the other four were added on
all-cash terms except BNSF, financed about 70% cash with the remainder in
shares that diluted the count by only 6.1%:

> "The $9.7 billion gain in annual earnings delivered Berkshire by the five
> companies has been accompanied by only minor dilution." — [[Sources/2012ltr]]

Separately, 26 bolt-on acquisitions closed for about $2.3 billion, a record,
all funded without issuing Berkshire shares.

with about $300 million of annual premium, was acquired late in the year.

### Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses: renewables at scale

BNSF's interest coverage held at 9.6x, moving about 15% of all U.S.
inter-city freight ton-miles across every transport mode combined —
"the most important artery in our economy's circulatory system" — while
carrying a ton of freight roughly 500 miles per gallon of diesel, about four
times the fuel efficiency of trucking. MidAmerican reached 6% of the
country's wind-generation capacity from a standing start nine years earlier,
and expected to own about 14% of U.S. solar capacity once three projects
then under construction were complete — a combined renewables portfolio
costing $13 billion. A footnote explains an apparent oddity: HomeServices,
the real estate brokerage MidAmerican owns, sits inside the "capital
intensive" reporting sector only because it came bundled with the original
2000 MidAmerican purchase; it added three brokers in 2012, expanded via a
67% purchase of the Prudential and Real Living franchise networks (544
licensed brokerages), and participated in $42 billion of home sales, up 33%.
See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Manufacturing, Service and Retailing: an accounting lecture, and Marmon's odd math

Buffett walks through why Berkshire reports non-GAAP operating expenses for
this sector, distinguishing amortization charges that represent real economic
cost (software) from those that don't (purchase-accounting artifacts like
customer-relationship amortization), and singles out Wells Fargo's "core
deposit" amortization — about $1.5 billion in the prior year — as a charge
against an asset that in practice keeps growing: "In no sense, except GAAP
accounting, is this whopping charge an expense."

Marmon's accounting is flagged as a genuine puzzle rather than a lecture:
a scheduled purchase of a further 10% of Marmon (bringing Berkshire's stake
to 90%) triggered a mandatory $700 million write-off against 2012's book
value under purchase-accounting rules that neither Buffett, Munger, nor CFO
Marc Hamburg could find a rationale for. The purchase itself implied a $12.6
billion value for the 90% stake against an $8 billion carrying value — a gap
Buffett estimates at $4.6 billion of unrecorded value. Union Tank Car, one of
Marmon's largest units, is traced back to the Standard Oil Trust before its
1911 breakup, with 97,000 tank cars (Union Tank Car and Canada's Procor
combined) carried at $4 billion net book value. See
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### Finance and Financial Products: Clayton as the country's top homebuilder

Clayton's 332,000-mortgage, $13.7 billion portfolio continued performing
through the housing downturn on the same down-payment and income-ratio
discipline described in [[Sources/2011ltr]]; the company built 25,872
manufactured homes, up 13.5%, making it America's largest homebuilder by
unit count at 4.8% of all single-family residences built that year.

### We Buy Some Newspapers... Newspapers?

Buffett explains a $344 million, 28-newspaper acquisition spree against his
own two-decade record of predicting the industry's decline — a decline he
does not retract. He traces the old economics precisely: pre-internet, a
local paper was the only "megaphone" advertisers had, producing a
self-reinforcing "survival of the fattest" that left almost every American
city with one dominant paper and extraordinary, management-proof profits —
quoting an old joke: "I owe my exalted position in life to two great American
institutions – nepotism and monopoly." That megaphone is gone for national
and financial news, lost to television and the internet, but Buffett argues
one category survives:

> "Newspapers continue to reign supreme, however, in the delivery of local
> news. ... A reader's eyes may glaze over after they take in a couple of
> paragraphs about Canadian tariffs or political developments in Pakistan; a
> story about the reader himself or his neighbors will be read to the end."
> — [[Sources/2012ltr]]

He credits Walter Hussman's Arkansas Democrat-Gazette as the clearest proof
that an early paywall preserves circulation better than giving content away
free online, and names his own 1977 Buffalo News fight — a competitor's
lawsuit that nearly killed the paper's new Sunday edition until a 3-0
appellate reversal 17 months later — as the origin of a decades-long
partnership with publisher Stan Lipsey, who retired at the end of 2012:

> "It's no exaggeration for me to say that the News might now be extinct
> were it not for Stan." — [[Sources/2012ltr]]

Buffett is explicit that the newspapers are held to the same no-forever-losses
standard as everything else — one Media General paper acquired in bulk was
shut down after analysis found no path to profitability — while stating the
economic case for the rest plainly: cheap enough acquisition prices can clear
Berkshire's return bar even for a business in structural decline. See
[[Concepts/Moat]] for the same "survival of the fattest" argument made in
[[Sources/2006ltr]] about the same industry.

### Dividends: the sell-off math, worked in full

Buffett's fullest defense of Berkshire's no-dividend policy, built as a
direct numerical comparison. A hypothetical two-owner company with $2 million
of net worth, earning 12% on net worth and able to reinvest at the same rate,
with outside buyers always willing to pay 125% of book value: paying a
one-third dividend each year compounds shareholder value at 8% annually
(12% earned minus 4% paid out), reaching $4,317,850 of net worth after ten
years. Retaining all earnings and instead selling 3.2% of one's shares
annually — the "sell-off" approach — produces the same initial $40,000 of
cash but ends with net worth compounded at the full 12%, reaching
$6,211,696:

> "The market value of your remaining shares would be $2,804,425, about 4%
> greater than the value of your shares if we had followed the dividend
> approach." — [[Sources/2012ltr]]

Two further arguments favor the sell-off approach beyond the arithmetic: it
lets each shareholder pick their own personal cash-out rate rather than
having one policy imposed on 600,000 different preferences, and it is taxed
only on the gain portion of proceeds rather than on the full distribution.
Buffett illustrates the mechanism with his own annual share gifts to
philanthropy — about 4¼% to 4½% of his holdings each year for seven years,
reducing his share count from 712,497,000 to 528,525,623 B-equivalent shares
— while the book value of what remained still grew, from $28.2 billion
(2005) to $40.2 billion (2012). He credits Phil Fisher's *Common Stocks and
Uncommon Profits* for the standing warning against changing a payout policy
capriciously, using Fisher's own restaurant analogy: a business can
successfully serve hamburgers, or alternatively feature Chinese food, but
cannot switch between the two and keep the fans of either. See
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **H. J. Heinz** — agreed February 2013, reported as breaking news: 50%
  of a new holding company alongside Jorge Paulo Lemann's investor group;
  Berkshire contributes about $4 billion of common equity plus $8 billion of
  9% preferred stock with warrants.
- **Guard Insurance** — Wilkes-Barre workers'-compensation writer, about
  $300 million of annual premium.
- 28 daily newspapers acquired over 15 months for $344 million.
- 26 bolt-on acquisitions for about $2.3 billion, a record, funded entirely
  with cash.
- An additional 10% of Marmon (bringing the stake to 90%), implying a $12.6
  billion value for that 90% against an $8 billion carrying value.

**Portfolio**

- Increased ownership across the Big Four: Wells Fargo to 8.7%, IBM to 6.0%,
  Coca-Cola to 8.9% (via buybacks), American Express to 13.7% (via buybacks).
- DIRECTV — the first stock in the annual table chosen by Todd Combs and Ted
  Weschler rather than Buffett.
- Equity-put derivative liability reduced from $8.5 billion to $7.5 billion
  as settlement value fell.

**Insurance and utilities**

- Float grew to $73.125 billion at a $1.625 billion underwriting profit, the
  tenth consecutive profitable year.
- MidAmerican's renewables portfolio reached $13 billion committed, 6% of
  U.S. wind capacity and a planned 14% of U.S. solar capacity.

**Corporate**

- Repurchase limit raised from 110% to 120% of book value in December, after
  a large block became available at about 116%.
- Record $9.8 billion of capital expenditures, about 88% domestic.
