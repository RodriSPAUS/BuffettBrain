# An Owner's Manual Summary

Not an annual letter but a standalone booklet — Buffett's own distillation of the
13 (later 14) "owner-related business principles" he first set down at the 1983
Blue Chip merger, reprinted here in January 1999 for the ~250,000 shareholders the
General Re merger had brought in. Where a letter reports one year, the Manual states
the constitution that governs every year: how Berkshire thinks about ownership,
debt, earnings, accounting and succession. It is the single densest source in the
corpus for [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]], [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and
[[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]], and much of its language recurs verbatim across the
letters because those letters quote it.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The partnership attitude, and eating your own cooking (Principles 1–2)

The Manual opens on the sentence the whole edifice rests on:

> "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]]

Shareholders are "owner-partners," managers are "managing partners," and the
company is "a conduit through which our shareholders own the assets." The alignment
is not rhetorical: Munger's family holds "90% or more of its net worth in Berkshire
shares", Buffett and his wife "more than 99%". [[Sources/OwnersManual]]

> "We eat our own cooking." [[Sources/OwnersManual]]

### Per-share intrinsic value is the only scorecard (Principles 3–4)

The economic goal is "to maximize Berkshire's average annual rate of gain in
intrinsic business value on a per-share basis" — explicitly *not* size.
[[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] Since 1983 intrinsic value had
compounded at "more than 25%," but Buffett caps expectations bluntly: "The best
rate of gain in intrinsic value we can even hope for is an average of 15% per
annum". [[Sources/OwnersManual]] The preferred route is owning whole businesses,
the second is owning pieces via marketable stocks — and a falling market helps both:

> "So when the market plummets - as it will from time to time - neither panic nor mourn." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/MrMarket]]

### Look-through earnings, and ignoring GAAP consolidation (Principles 5–6)

Because of the two-pronged approach, "consolidated reported earnings may reveal
relatively little about our true economic performance." [[Sources/OwnersManual]]
The Manual states the accounting heresy plainly — that reportability is not value:

> "we much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]]

The corrective is look-through earnings — reported operating earnings plus
Berkshire's share of investees' undistributed earnings, net of the tax that would
be due on dividends. The 1992 goal of $604 million growing to $1.8 billion by 2000
had, after shares issued for General Re, been restated to a needed $2.4 billion.
[[Sources/OwnersManual]]

### Conservative leverage, and float as costless debt (Principle 7)

Debt is used "sparingly" and on a "long-term fixed-rate basis," under the racing
maxim "To finish first, you must first finish." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] The two
"low-cost, non-perilous sources of leverage" — deferred taxes and
[[Concepts/Float]] — then totaled about $32 billion, cost-free as long as
underwriting breaks even, which it had over Berkshire's 32 insurance years.
[[Sources/OwnersManual]]

### Retained-earnings test, disciplined share issuance, no gin rummy (Principles 8–11)

Three tests of capital discipline sit together: acquisitions must raise per-share
intrinsic value ("A managerial 'wish list' will not be filled at shareholder
expense"); retained earnings are judged on a five-year rolling one-dollar test; and
shares are issued "only when we receive as much in business value as we give" —
the logic behind stating the 1996 Class B sale was *not* undervalued.
[[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] Against that, a refusal to
churn the portfolio of businesses:

> "Regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire owns." [[Sources/OwnersManual]]

"Gin rummy managerial behavior (discard your least promising business at each turn)
is not our style"; the mid-1980s closure of the original textile business was the
rare exception, made only because it "was doomed to run never-ending operating
losses." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

### Candor as a governance rule (Principles 12–13, and the added 14th)

Reporting aims to give shareholders "the business facts that we would want to know
if our positions were reversed", with no "big bath" accounting and no earnings
"smoothing":

> "We will always tell you how many strokes we have taken on each hole and never play around with the scorecard." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

Buffett will discuss marketable securities "only to the extent legally required"
but shares philosophy freely, crediting "Ben Graham, the greatest teacher in the
history of finance". [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[People/BenGraham]] The added 14th
principle states his preference for a fair stock price over a high one: as bad, in
his view, to be overvalued as undervalued. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### Intrinsic value defined, and book value's limits

The Manual gives the canonical definition the letters lean on:

> "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

Book value is only "a rough, albeit significantly understated, tracking measure":
in 1964 Berkshire's book value of $19.46 *overstated* intrinsic value (all of it
trapped in textiles); by 1999 book value badly understated it. The college-education
analogy makes the point that cost (book value) and discounted future excess earnings
(intrinsic value) are different things.

### Purchase-accounting, economic goodwill, and the EBITDA warning

Amortization of acquisition goodwill is an accounting fiction that runs opposite to
reality: GEICO's $1.6 billion of goodwill would be written off at about $40 million
a year through 2036 even as its *economic* goodwill grows. [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]]
See's is the proof — its book goodwill fell from about $36 million to $21 million
while it went from earning $27 million pre-tax on $11 million of net operating assets
(1983) to $59 million on $5 million (1997). [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Cases/SeeCandies]]
Hence the warning against confusing amortization with depreciation:

> "Furthermore, we do not think so-called EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) is a meaningful measure of performance." [[Sources/OwnersManual]]

### Managing by abdication, and succession

The management model is radical delegation — about 45,000 employees, only 12 at
headquarters:

> "In fact, we delegate almost to the point of abdication: Though Berkshire has about 45,000 employees, only 12 of these are at headquarters." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

On his death, "only about 1% of my stock will have to be sold" to cover bequests and
taxes, with control passing to his wife or a family foundation under the same philosophy. He describes the
succession structure the later letters would fill with names:

> "Essentially my job will be split into two parts, with one executive becoming responsible for investments and another for operations." [[Sources/OwnersManual]] [[People/GregAbel]] [[People/AjitJain]]

## 📊 Investment Decisions

The Owner's Manual makes no new investment or capital-allocation decisions — that
is not its purpose. It states the principles that *govern* those decisions, and
refers back to a few already made only to illustrate them:

- **GEICO (1996 full acquisition)** — used to explain purchase-accounting goodwill:
  $1.6 billion recorded, amortized ~$40 million/year to 2036, against rising economic value. [[Cases/GEICO]]
- **See's Candies** — the worked example of economic goodwill diverging from book goodwill. [[Cases/SeeCandies]]
- **Class B share sale (1996)** — cited as the test case for issuing stock only at fair value: Berkshire stated the stock was *not* undervalued.
- **Textile business (closed mid-1980s)** — the one permitted exception to the no-selling rule, closed only because it faced never-ending losses.
