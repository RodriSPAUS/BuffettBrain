# 2001 Annual Letter

The only year in the corpus with a *loss* in net worth, and the letter in which Buffett names his
own error as its cause. September 11th cost Berkshire $2.4 billion of underwriting loss on risk it
had never been paid to take, and he traces it to a failure to convert a known judgment into
action. Out of that come the three underwriting principles, the Noah rule, and the demolition of
the phrase "loss development" — the most concentrated section on insurance discipline anywhere in
the letters.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A loss, and whose fault it was

Net worth fell $3.77 billion, decreasing per-share book value by 6.2% against the S&P's −11.9% —
a relative gain of 5.7 points. Over 37 years book value per share went from $19 to $37,920, 22.6%
compounded annually. Per-share intrinsic value grew somewhat faster than book value over those 37
years and probably decreased a bit less in 2001.

Buffett separates the company's result from his own without softening either: corporate
performance was satisfactory, his was anything but. He manages most of the equity portfolio and
his results were poor, as they had been for several years. And more importantly:

> "I allowed General Re to take on business without a safeguard I knew was important, and on
> September 11th, this error caught up with us." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

On the relative-versus-absolute question, he reaches back to the founding of his first partnership
on 5 May 1956. The short paper he handed his seven founding limited partners that evening, "The
Ground Rules", contained the sentence:

> "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is to be measured against the general experience in
> securities." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

The benchmark was the Dow, then the S&P 500 once that index became widely used. To the objection
that you cannot eat relative performance, his answer is that if owning the index produces
reasonably satisfactory results over time, then small annual advantages over it must prove
rewarding to a long-term investor — just as See's, which loses considerable money each summer,
still feeds you across the year.

Another 1956 ground rule still applies: "I cannot promise results to partners." What he and Munger
*can* promise is that their economic result will parallel shareholders' — no cash compensation,
restricted stock or option grants that would make their results superior. He will keep well over
99% of his net worth in Berkshire, and neither he nor his wife has ever sold a share.

The passage that follows is the angriest in the corpus. Written as Enron collapsed, it condemns
the pattern of shareholders losing billions while the CEOs and promoters who caused the disasters
walked away with extraordinary wealth, some urging investors to buy while dumping their own shares
by methods that hid the fact:

> "To their shame, these business leaders view shareholders as patsies, not partners."
> — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

The joke he uses to make the point about option repricing — a woman offering a CEO anything he
wants, and his reply being "Reprice my options." — is the sharpest thing he ever wrote about
executive incentives.

He also restates the size problem plainly: a good $10 million idea once did wonders, as with The
Washington Post in 1973 or GEICO in 1976; today ten such ideas each tripling would raise
Berkshire's net worth by only a quarter of 1%. Elephants are needed now, and they are hard to
find.

> "We now have completed 37 Berkshire years without having a CEO of an operating business elect to
> leave us to work elsewhere." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

### Acquisitions of 2001, all for cash

Shaw and Johns Manville closed from the 2000 agreements; MiTek and XTRA were acquired;
Larson-Juhl and Fruit of the Loom were contracted for. Every purchase was for cash, so
shareholders became owners of the new businesses without relinquishing any interest in the ones
they already owned.

- **MiTek** arrived as a heavy package from St. Louis containing an unidentifiable chunk of metal
  and a letter from CEO Gene Toombs, explaining that it was a connector plate used in roofing
  trusses, that MiTek was the world's leading producer, and that its U.K. parent wished to sell.
  A phone call was enough. The structural detail worth keeping: 55 members of the MiTek team bought
  10% of the company, each putting up a minimum of $100,000 in cash, many borrowing to do it.

  > "They face the downside of decisions as well as the upside." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

  They incur a cost of capital and cannot reprice their stakes — what they paid is what they live
  with. It is the option critique turned into a positive design. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].
- **XTRA**, a leading lessor of truck trailers, came through Julian Robertson's liquidation of
  Tiger Fund. The tender offer expired on 11 September and contained the usual out allowing
  withdrawal if the stock market closed before expiration. Buffett records that CEO Lew Rubin spent
  that day unable to locate a son-in-law who worked in the World Trade Center while knowing
  Berkshire could walk away. The son-in-law escaped serious harm and Berkshire completed the
  transaction.
- **Larson-Juhl**, U.S. leader in custom-made picture frames, came from a 3 December call by owner
  Craig Ponzio, who had worked in its manufacturing plant while at college, bought the company in
  1981, and grown sales from $3 million to $300 million. Buffett had never heard of it; a few
  minutes of conversation — straightforward about the business, caring who bought it, realistic on
  price — was enough. Agreement reached in ninety minutes two days later, contract in ten days. It
  serves about 18,000 framing shops in the U.S. and leads in Canada and much of Europe.
- **Fruit of the Loom**, offered to creditors in bankruptcy, closes a fifty-year circle. In August
  1955 Buffett was one of five employees at Graham-Newman, which controlled Philadelphia and
  Reading Coal and Iron; P&R bought Union Underwear — then only a licensee of the Fruit of the Loom
  name — from Jack Goldfarb for $15 million, of which $2.5 million came from Union's own $5 million
  of cash and $9 million was satisfied by non-interest-bearing notes payable from half of any
  earnings above $1 million. Fruit later earned annual pre-tax earnings exceeding $200 million.
  John Holland ran its best years, retired in 1996, watched management load the company with debt
  for disappointing acquisitions, and was rehired after bankruptcy to rebuild — cutting employment
  from a bloated 40,000 to 23,000 while repairing chaotic deliveries and damaged customer
  relations. Berkshire's offer carried no financing conditions but one unusual proviso: Holland had
  to stay as CEO. "To us, John and the brand are Fruit's key assets."

**A bolt-on with a long memory.** H.H. Brown bought the inventory and trademarks of Acme Boot for
$700,000 — a company P&R had bought for $3.2 million just before Buffett left Graham-Newman in
1956, which grew to be the world's largest bootmaker before hitting the skids.

> "Many shall be restored that now are fallen and many shall fall that are now in honor."
> — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

He notes it is the Horace line Graham and Dodd put in the frontispiece of *Security Analysis*, and
that his appreciation of it keeps growing fifty-two years after first reading it.

**R. C. Willey, continued.** The Boise store from [[Sources/1999ltr]] is now joined by Henderson,
next to Las Vegas — a metropolis where Buffett was even more sceptical that closing on Sundays
could work. It outsells every other store in the chain, at twice what he anticipated. He again
told the crowd at the ribbon-cutting that it had been his idea, and reports the result: "Today,
when I pontificate about retailing, Berkshire people just say, 'What does Bill think?'"

*(Yearend float, $ millions.)* Buffett had predicted that, barring a mega-catastrophe, float cost
would drop from 2000's 6%. He had natural catastrophes in mind; instead came a man-made one that
delivered the insurance industry its largest loss in history, and float cost came in at a
staggering 12.8% — the worst since 1984, and to a significant degree self-inflicted.

The forward view sharpens why cost now matters more than it used to: float costing 4% was once
tolerable when government bonds yielded twice that and stocks promised more. With fat returns
nowhere to be found and short-term funds earning under 2%, every insurance operation save one must
deliver an underwriting profit to be judged a good business. The exception is retroactive
reinsurance, whose economics are desirable despite an annual underwriting loss of about $425
million. See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### The three principles of underwriting

The most reusable section of the letter, and the reason it belongs in
[[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]]. Judged by cost of float, very few property/casualty
companies are satisfactory businesses — and unlike most industries, neither size nor brand
determines profitability. Many of the biggest and best-known deliver mediocre results. What counts
is underwriting discipline, and the winners stick to three principles:

1. **Accept only risks you can properly evaluate** — staying within the circle of competence — and
   which, after weighing all relevant factors *including remote loss scenarios*, carry the
   expectancy of profit. Such insurers ignore market share and are sanguine about losing business
   to competitors offering foolish prices or conditions.
2. **Limit the business accepted** so that no aggregation of losses from a single event or related
   events can threaten solvency, searching ceaselessly for correlation among seemingly unrelated
   risks.
3. **Avoid moral risk.** No matter what the rate, trying to write good contracts with bad people
   does not work.

See [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### What went wrong, in his own words

September 11th made clear that General Re's implementation of rules 1 and 2 had been dangerously
weak: in pricing and in evaluating aggregation risk, the possibility of large-scale terrorism
losses had been overlooked or dismissed. It was a relevant underwriting factor and it was ignored.

> "In short, all of us in the industry made a fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on
> experience, rather than exposure, thereby assuming a huge terrorism risk for which we received no
> premium." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

The experience-versus-exposure distinction is developed with an example that has nothing to do
with terrorism and everything to do with cycles. Late in a bull market, large D&O losses are
relatively rare — rising stocks mean few targets to sue, and questionable accounting and
management chicanery go undetected — so experience on high-limit D&O looks great precisely when
exposure is exploding through ridiculous public offerings, earnings manipulation and
chain-letter-like promotions. When stocks fall, the sins surface, and juries deciding whether
losses fall on small investors or big insurers deliver verdicts bearing little relation to
bull-market ones. The correct exposure-based rate for D&O excess might be five or more times what
experience dictates.

On terrorism itself he sets out four things known: the probability of a nuclear detonation in a
major metropolis, or of biological or chemical agents introduced simultaneously into multiple
buildings, is very low at present but not zero; the probabilities are increasing in an irregular
and immeasurable way as knowledge and materials spread; insurers and reinsurers have until now
blithely assumed these incalculable risks; and under a close-to-worst-case scenario involving
perhaps $1 trillion of damage, the industry would be destroyed unless it dramatically limits its
assumption of terrorism risk, since only the U.S. Government has the resources to absorb such a
blow.

Then the admission that gives the section its force — he had recognised all of this *before*
September 11th:

> "The answer, sadly, is that I did – but I didn't convert thought into action. I violated the
> Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

He notes that much uncompensated risk remained on the books, running off by the day.

The conclusion is not retreat. Berkshire has for years been willing to assume more risk than any
other insurer knowingly takes on, and is perfectly willing to lose $2 billion to $2½ billion in a
single event — as it did on September 11th — *if properly paid for the risk*, which on that
occasion it was not. Massive liquid resources, substantial non-insurance earnings, a favourable
tax position and a shareholder body willing to accept volatile earnings let Berkshire assume risks
beyond its largest competitors' appetite. It will write some terrorism coverage, including a few
non-correlated policies with very large limits, but will control total exposure no matter what the
competition does.

### General Re, Ajit Jain, GEICO

**General Re.** Buffett states flatly that he had assured shareholders General Re was underwriting
with discipline and had been proven wrong: the company broke each of the three rules. It did not
reserve correctly and therefore severely miscalculated the cost of what it was selling — and in
long-tail reinsurance, where years of unawareness promote and prolong severe underpricing,
ignorance of true costs is dynamite. It was also overly competitive in chasing and keeping
business, which he generalises:

> "'No' must be an important part of any underwriter's vocabulary." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

If winning is equated with market share rather than profits, trouble awaits. Joe Brandon became
CEO in September with Tad Montross as president, both committed to underwriting profits.

**Ajit Jain** runs one of the world's largest reinsurance operations by assets, and the largest by
size of individual risks assumed, with 18 associates. Buffett has known the details of almost
every policy Ajit has written since 1986 and has never once seen him break any of the three rules.

> "And that's the key: Just as is the case in investing, insurers produce outstanding long-term
> results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions, rather than by making brilliant ones."
> — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

Post-11 September policies written and retained entirely for Berkshire's own account include $578
million of property coverage on a South American refinery above a $1 billion loss; $1 billion of
non-cancelable third-party liability for terrorism at several large international airlines; £500
million of property coverage on a North Sea oil platform above £600 million retained elsewhere;
and significant coverage on the Sears Tower above a $500 million threshold — plus the World Cup
and the 2002 Winter Olympics. Aggregation is controlled deliberately: Berkshire will not write a
large number of towers in one metropolis without excluding nuclear explosion and the fires that
would follow.

The competitive-advantage argument is about balance sheets, not skill. Reinsurers accustomed to
laying off business on retrocessionaires are weakened and would struggle to survive a second
mega-catastrophe, and a single weak link in a daisy chain endangers everyone — so insurers must
stress-test every participant in the chain against a catastrophe arriving in a bad economy.

> "After all, you only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out."
> — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

> "At Berkshire, we retain our risks and depend on no one. And whatever the world's problems, our
> checks will clear." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

**GEICO** made major progress: premium volume grew 6.6%, float grew $308 million, and it earned a
$221 million underwriting profit — meaning Berkshire was paid that amount to hold $4.25 billion of
float. The disappointment, and Buffett calls it important, was the inability to add policyholders:
preferred customers (81% of the total) grew 1.6% while standard and non-standard policies fell
10.1%, so policies in force fell 0.8%. He and Tony Nicely are eager to commit far more than the
$219 million spent on marketing but cannot at the moment see how to do so effectively. See
[[Cases/GEICO]].

The other primary insurers had combined premium volume of $579 million, up 40%, float up 14.5% to
$685 million, and a $30 million underwriting profit.

### "Loss development": the terminology takedown

> "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

The target is language that smuggles in a conclusion. Loss reserves are not funds tucked away for
a rainy day but a liability account stating what an insurer will have to pay for losses already
occurred, including those incurred but not reported — sometimes losses the insured itself does not
yet know about. Getting that figure roughly right is vital, because otherwise management does not
know its own costs. GEICO's near-death in the early 1970s came exactly this way: severe
underreserving made it believe insurance protection cost far less than it did, so it sailed along
underpricing and selling ever more policies at ever-larger losses.

At General Re in 2001, a staggering $800 million of loss costs that had actually occurred in
earlier years were belatedly recognised and charged against current earnings. Buffett says the
mistake was honest, and names three consequences: it made costs look lower than they were and so
contributed to woefully inadequate pricing; the overstated profits led Berkshire to pay
substantial incentive compensation it should not have; and it incurred income taxes far earlier
than necessary.

Then the demand that the words be scrapped:

> "The losses didn't 'develop' – they were there all along." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

What developed was management's understanding of the losses — or, in cases of chicanery, its
willingness to finally fess up. He proposes "loss costs we failed to recognize when they occurred"
or simply "oops", and dismisses the twin phrase "reserve strengthening" by asking whether any
insurer finding its reserves excessive has ever described the reduction as reserve weakening.

Two further arguments follow, both worth carrying into [[Applications/RedFlags]]:

> "In effect, insurance accounting is a self-graded exam, in that the insurer gives some figures to
> its auditing firm and generally doesn't get an argument." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

Major underreserving is common in companies struggling for survival, which seldom prove tough
graders — "Who, after all, wants to prepare his own execution papers?" And the rented-suit story
lands the point about latent liabilities: a brother pays $4,500 for his father's funeral and then
$10 invoices keep arriving, until his sister explains "We buried dad in a rented suit." There are
a lot of rented suits buried in insurers' past operations, sometimes dormant for decades, as
asbestos was.

Finally he opposes discounting property/casualty reserves. It would be acceptable only if reserves
could be precisely established, which they cannot — judicial broadening of policy language and
medical inflation constantly work to make them inadequate — so discounting would worsen a serious
situation and hand a new tool to companies inclined to fudge.

### Reported earnings, Dexter, and the operating businesses

Operating earnings were $488 million pre-tax and *negative* $47 million after taxes and minority
interests, against $1,699 million and $936 million in 2000; reinsurance underwriting alone lost
$4,318 million pre-tax. Capital gains of $1,320 million brought total earnings to $1,808 million
pre-tax and $795 million after.

> "I've made three decisions relating to Dexter that have hurt you in a major way: (1) buying it in
> the first place; (2) paying for it with stock and (3) procrastinating when the need for changes
> in its operations was obvious." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

He refuses to lay them on Munger or anyone else. Shoe operations lost $46.2 million pre-tax, with
H.H. Brown and Justin profits swamped by Dexter; the operation was placed under Frank Rooney and
Jim Issler. This is the third successive year Dexter appears as a named mistake — after the
goodwill write-off in [[Sources/2000ltr]] — and the list grows each time.

- **MidAmerican Energy** had a good year, swapping properties in England to add Yorkshire Electric
  and its 2.1 million customers, bringing U.K. customers to 3.6 million as the second-largest
  electric utility there, alongside Iowa operations and generating facilities in California and the
  Philippines. It also holds the second-largest residential real estate brokerage in the country,
  built by Ron Peltier, handling about 106,000 transactions on properties worth nearly $20 billion.
  See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].
- **Retailing** held up in a recession: jewelry same-store sales fell 7.6% with pre-tax margins of
  8.9% against 10.7%; home-furnishings same-store sales and the 9.1% pre-tax margin were unchanged.
  [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] is building a 450,000 square foot store for greater Kansas City
  opening autumn 2003 — and, Buffett notes, it will stay open on Sundays.
- **Shaw, Johns Manville and Benjamin Moore** together earned about $659 million pre-tax in their
  first year. Shortly after yearend Berkshire exchanged 4,740 A shares for Shaw's 12.7% minority
  interest, taking it to 100%; Shaw is now the largest non-insurance operation.
- **FlightSafety** earnings rose 2.5%, with return on invested capital slipping because of $258
  million invested in simulators and fixed assets. Al Ueltschi, 84, still runs it. The 2002 plan is
  $162 million for 27 simulators against a $95 million depreciation charge:

  > "Those who believe that EBITDA is in any way equivalent to true earnings are welcome to pick up
  > the tab." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

- **NetJets** sold a record number of planes and grew service income 21.9%, but operated at a small
  loss as European losses exceeded U.S. earnings. It accounts for about half the industry by value
  of customers' planes, and Buffett believes the other participants in aggregate lost significant
  money. More than 300 planes constantly in the air cut positioning costs and let the company
  reach a customer on short notice. The Buffetts log in excess of 800 fractional-ownership hours
  annually, on exactly the same planes and crews as other customers.

Few changes were made. The larger holdings have performed poorly, some through disappointing
operating results; Buffett still likes the basic businesses but does not believe the equity
holdings as a group are undervalued, and expresses decidedly lukewarm feelings about stocks
generally over the next decade or so. American business will do fine, but the market outperformed
business for a very long period and that had to end.

The coincidence he records: the Great Bubble ended on 10 March 2000, the day NASDAQ hit its
all-time high of 5,132 — and the same day Berkshire shares traded at $40,800, their lowest price
since mid-1997.

**Junk bonds.** Berkshire was more active than usual, with heavy caveats: these are not suitable
for the general public, Berkshire has never purchased a newly-issued junk bond, and losses when
they occur are often disastrous. The fifty-year record in distressed debt has been rewarding —
Washington Public Power System bonds in the 1984 report, then Chrysler Financial, Texaco and RJR
Nabisco.

Two positions became something bigger:

- **Fruit of the Loom** — public and bank debt bought at about 50% of face early in the bankruptcy,
  in an unusual case where senior interest payments continued, giving about a 15% current return.
  Holdings reached 10% of senior debt and will probably return about 70% of face, indirectly
  reducing the purchase price of the whole company.
- **FINOVA** — about 13% of its $11 billion of debt bought at roughly two-thirds of face in late
  2000, expecting bankruptcy but believing liquidation would pay out well above cost. With Leucadia
  National, Berkshire presented a prepackaged bankruptcy plan; creditors received 70% of face plus
  full interest and a new 7½% note for the rest. Berkadia, the jointly-owned entity, borrowed $5.6
  billion through FleetBoston and re-lent it to FINOVA with a priority claim, Berkshire guaranteeing
  90% and holding a secondary guarantee on Leucadia's 10%, with about a two-point spread flowing
  90/10. Berkshire's separate offer for up to $500 million of the new bonds was terminated when the
  NYSE closed in the week of 11 September, one of the stated conditions. FINOVA's prospects are
  worse than when the proposal was made, since many of its loans involve aircraft assets.

The FleetBoston story is the one Buffett tells for its own sake: in 1969, needing $10 million to
buy the Illinois National Bank of Rockford, he contacted two banks and got no response from New
York while two Boston representatives came straight to Omaha and said they would supply the money
and work out the details later. Three decades later, structuring FINOVA — "(Debt is a four-letter
word around Berkshire.)" — he called again, and Chad Gifford said the same thing. The syndicated
loan was oversubscribed by 17 banks worldwide.

And a correction he makes about attribution: reports that Buffett is buying something are sometimes
actually Lou Simpson, who runs a $2 billion portfolio for GEICO quite independently and normally
does not tell him what he is doing.

> "In recent years, Lou's performance has been far better than mine." — [[Sources/2001ltr]]

### Charitable contributions, stated as policy

Berkshire's approach is unusual and the letter explains it as a matter of ownership rather than
generosity. Operating subsidiaries make their own decisions, on the condition that owner-managers
fund their personal charities from their own money rather than company funds; subsidiary
contributions totalled $19.2 million. At parent level Berkshire makes no contributions except
those designated by shareholders — no matching of director or employee gifts, and nothing to the
Buffetts' or Mungers' favourite charities. Registered A holders (86.6% of equity capital) each
direct a per-share amount to as many as three charities; in 2001, $16.7 million went to 3,550
charities named by 5,700 shareholders, bringing the program's total to $181 million. Recipients
included 437 churches and synagogues and 790 schools.

He answers the weekly criticism over Planned Parenthood by pointing at the mechanism: it is not
Berkshire but its owners making the choice, and they are about as diverse as can be imagined —
Berkshire will follow instructions naming Planned Parenthood or Metro Right to Life alike, so
long as the charity has 501(c)(3) status. It is as if a dividend were paid and the shareholder
donated it, only more tax-efficient. He adds that in buying goods and hiring people, Berkshire
never considers religious views, gender, race or sexual orientation — it would not only be wrong
but idiotic, since able and trustworthy people come from a very wide spectrum. See
[[Principles/Fidelity]].

### The meeting and the head office

The meeting returns to the Civic Auditorium on Saturday 4 May. NFM's Berkshire Weekend grew from
$5.3 million in 1997 to $11.5 million in 2001. Berkshire added about 40,000 employees during the
year, bringing the workforce to 110,000, while headquarters — all 5,246.5 square feet of it — added
one person to reach 14.8.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired — all for cash**

- **Shaw Industries** and **Johns Manville** closed from the 2000 agreements; Shaw taken to 100%
  shortly after yearend by exchanging 4,740 A shares for the 12.7% minority.
- **MiTek** — world leader in connector plates for roofing trusses, sourced from an unsolicited
  package. 55 managers bought 10% of the company for cash, minimum $100,000 each.
- **XTRA** — truck trailer lessor, from Julian Robertson's liquidation of Tiger Fund. Tender expired
  11 September; Berkshire did not exercise its right to withdraw.
- **Larson-Juhl** — U.S. leader in custom picture frames, agreed in ninety minutes.
- **Fruit of the Loom** — offered in bankruptcy with no financing conditions, conditional on John
