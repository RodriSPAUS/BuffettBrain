# 1989 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1989, and containing "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years" — the
single most important section in the early letters. It is where Buffett abandons
the cigar-butt strategy in print, names the institutional imperative, and
explains why he refuses leverage.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Taxes: the deferred-tax liability as an interest-free loan

Berkshire's 1989 gain of $1.5 billion was taken after a $712 million tax charge, of which $540 million was deferred — almost all tied to the year's increase in unrealized gains on the stock portfolio, reserved at a 34% rate. Buffett uses the balance-sheet liability this creates to make a broader point about how deferred taxes should be read:

> "Is this $1.1 billion liability equal, or even similar, to a $1.1 billion liability payable to a trade creditor 15 days after the end of the year? Obviously not - despite the fact that both items have exactly the same effect on audited net worth, reducing it by $1.1 billion." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "On the other hand, is this liability for deferred taxes a meaningless accounting fiction because its payment can be triggered only by the sale of stocks that, in very large part, we have no intention of selling? Again, the answer is no. In economic terms, the liability resembles an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election... In effect, this deferred tax liability is equivalent to a very large transfer tax that is payable only if we elect to move from one asset to another." [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]

He then works a stylized comparison: a dollar doubled 20 times through 20 separate one-year trades, each taxed at 34% on sale, compounds to about $25,250 after tax; the same dollar left to compound untouched through 20 doublings and cashed out once compounds to about $692,000 after the same 34% tax.

> "The sole reason for this staggering difference in results would be the timing of tax payments. Interestingly, the government would gain from Scenario 2 in exactly the same 27:1 ratio as we - taking in taxes of $356,500 vs. $13,000 - though, admittedly, it would have to wait for its money." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

He is careful to add that this arithmetic is not why Berkshire favors long holding periods — the reason given is the value of the business relationships themselves, not the tax edge.

### Sources of Reported Earnings, and "look-through" earnings

The 1989 segment table (Berkshire's after-tax share, in thousands) shows Insurance Underwriting -$12,259, Insurance Net Investment Income $213,642, Buffalo News $27,771, Fechheimer $6,789, Kirby $16,803, Nebraska Furniture Mart $8,441, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing $19,996, See's Candies $20,626, Wesco (non-insurance) $9,810, World Book $16,372, plus goodwill amortization, interest expense, and Sales of Securities of $147,575, for Total Earnings — All Entities of $447,477 versus $399,270 in 1988.

Buffett names the gap between this reported figure and Berkshire's true economic earnings: the five major investees paid Berkshire about $45 million in dividends in 1989, but Berkshire's share of their *retained* earnings was about $212 million — undistributed largely because accounting rules cap recognition below a 20% ownership stake.

> "In our view, Berkshire's fundamental earning power is best measured by a "look-through" approach, in which we append our share of the operating earnings retained by our investees to our own reported operating earnings, excluding capital gains in both instances. For our intrinsic business value to grow at an average of 15% per year, our "look-through" earnings must grow at about the same pace." [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### Non-Insurance Operations: "The Sainted Seven Plus One"

With the addition of Borsheim's early in 1989, Buffett's running nickname for the non-insurance operating businesses — Borsheim's, The Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing, See's Candies, and World Book — becomes "The Sainted Seven Plus One." Combined, they earned 57% on average equity with no net leverage.

- **Borsheim's**, run by Ike Friedman, more than doubled sales in its four years at its present location; its cost advantage comes from volume at a single huge store, holding expenses to about a third of comparable jewelers'. Buffett tells the Santa Fe story of Ike bringing $20 million of jewelry to an informal gathering; asked about security, Ike leaned in and confided that the jewels weren't in the safe at all. [[Sources/1989ltr]] The underlying principle: "If you don't know jewelry, know your jeweler" [[Sources/1989ltr]]
- **See's Candies**, under Chuck Huggins, had an 8% increase in pounds sold on top of a record 1988, with advertising spend raised from $4 million to $5 million. [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Cases/SeeCandies]]
- **Nebraska Furniture Mart** had record sales, but Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B") quit in May at 96 after a dispute with family management over the carpet department — a dispute Buffett says the family was right about, since carpet sales had fallen 17% over three years while the rest of the store grew 24%. "You will be pleased to know that Mrs. B continues to make Horatio Alger's heroes look like victims of tired blood" — she promptly started a new competing carpet-and-furniture business. [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]]
- **The Buffalo News**, under publisher Stan Lipsey and editor Murray Light, led major American papers in household penetration and posted its seventh consecutive profit record, despite Erie County's population declining. [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Cases/BuffaloNews]]
- **Fechheimer**, run by the Heldman family, saw profits dip on integration costs from a 1988 acquisition but retained splendid returns on invested capital.
- **Scott Fetzer (World Book, Kirby, and the Manufacturing Group)**, run by Ralph Schey, contributed about 40% of non-insurance earnings; Kirby's export unit-sales share rose from 5% to 20% in four years.

### Insurance Operations: the industry cycle, and the Hurricane Hugo CAT-cover story

A statistical table tracks the industry's combined ratio climbing from 106.0 in 1981 to an estimated 110.4 in 1989, against premium growth running well below the roughly 10% Buffett estimates is needed just to hold losses steady. He rejects "underwriting cycle" as a misleading term now that the old cartel-era rate-setting lags are gone:

> "Good profits will be realized only when there is a shortage of capacity. Shortages will occur only when insurers are frightened. That happens rarely - and most assuredly is not happening now." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

The exception in 1989 was catastrophe reinsurance ("CAT covers"). Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake struck within weeks of each other, leaving many primary insurers and reinsurers bare or uncertain of their remaining coverage, and CAT premiums instantly became attractive:

> "Just as instantly, Berkshire Hathaway offered to write up to $250 million of catastrophe coverage, advertising that proposition in trade publications. Though we did not write all the business we sought, we did in a busy ten days book a substantial amount." [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Concepts/Float]]

Berkshire's willingness to retain the full loss itself, rather than lay it off as other reinsurers must, rests on its roughly $6 billion insurance net worth and its indifference to quarterly earnings volatility. Buffett frames the resulting behavior as involuntarily stabilizing: "Nevertheless, our behavior steadies the market. In this case, Adam Smith's invisible hand works as advertised." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

### Marketable Securities: the portfolio, and four convertible preferreds

The over-$100-million common stock list at 12/31/89 (cost/market, in thousands) was unchanged in composition from 1988 except for Coca-Cola: Capital Cities/ABC $517,500/$1,692,375, The Coca-Cola Co. $1,023,920/$1,803,787, Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corp. $71,729/$161,100, GEICO Corp. $45,713/$1,044,625, and The Washington Post Company $9,731/$486,366.

Berkshire also sold much of its WPPSS and medium-term tax-exempt bond position during the year for about $51 million of pre-tax gains, redeploying the proceeds — along with excess cash — into three new convertible preferred stocks: $600 million of Gillette 8¾% preferred (convertible at $50), $358 million of USAir Group 9¼% preferred (convertible at $60), and $300 million of Champion International 9¼% preferred (convertible at $38), joining the 1987 Salomon convertible preferred. Each carried full voting rights on an as-converted basis and mandatory ten-year redemption.

> "In one major respect, however, these purchases are not different: We only want to link up with people whom we like, admire, and trust." [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

Buffett is candid that the structure caps the upside: "The only way Berkshire can achieve satisfactory results from its four preferred issues is to have the common stocks of the investee companies do well." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

### Zero-Coupon Securities: Berkshire's own issue, and an essay against "EBDIT"

Berkshire itself issued $902.6 million principal amount of 15-year Zero-Coupon Convertible Subordinated Debentures in September, priced at 44.314% of maturity value (a 5.5% semi-annual-compounded equivalent yield), raising about $400 million net and convertible at $9,815 per Berkshire share — a 15% premium to the market price at issuance.

Buffett distinguishes Berkshire's zero from the broader wave of junk zero-coupon and PIK bonds used to finance increasingly aggressive leveraged deals, and singles out one measure for particular contempt:

> "Soon borrowers found even the new, lax standards intolerably binding. To induce lenders to finance even sillier transactions, they introduced an abomination, EBDIT - Earnings Before Depreciation, Interest and Taxes - as the test of a company's ability to pay interest." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "Such an attitude is clearly delusional. At 95% of American businesses, capital expenditures that over time roughly approximate depreciation are a necessity and are every bit as real an expense as labor or utility costs." [[Sources/1989ltr]] [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]]

He invokes Galbraith's "the bezzle" — the undiscovered-embezzlement addition to psychic national wealth — as the closest analogy to the fictitious income a zero-coupon capital structure can manufacture, and offers a standing test for the reader: "Ask the investment banker for the one-year budgets that his own firm prepared as the last few years began and then compare these with what actually happened." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

### The cigar-butt confession

- The admission it opens with: "My first mistake, of course, was in buying control
  of Berkshire. Though I knew its business - textile manufacturing - to be
  unpromising, I was enticed to buy because the price looked cheap."
- The strategy named: "I call this the "cigar butt" approach to investing. A cigar
  butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may not offer much of
  a smoke, but the "bargain purchase" will make that puff all profit."
- And rejected: "Unless you are a liquidator, that kind of approach to buying
  businesses is foolish."
- Two reasons are given. First: "In a difficult business, no sooner is one problem
  solved than another surfaces - never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen."
- Second, the arithmetic of holding period: "Time is the friend of the wonderful
  business, the enemy of the mediocre."
- The replacement rule: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price
  than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a
  slow learner."
- Where it leads: "But now, when buying companies or common stocks, we look for
  first-class businesses accompanied by first-class managements."

This is the hinge of the whole collection. See [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]],
[[Concepts/Moat]] and [[People/CharlieMunger]].

### Good jockeys, bad horses

- "That leads right into a related lesson: Good jockeys will do well on good
  horses, but not on broken-down nags."
- The evidence, again from his own record: "Both Berkshire's textile business and
  Hochschild, Kohn had able and honest people running them. The same managers
  employed in a business with good economic characteristics would have achieved
  fine records. But they were never going to make any progress while running in
  quicksand."
- The maxim, and the wince attached to it: "I've said many times that when a
  management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation
  for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact. I
  just wish I hadn't been so energetic in creating examples."

See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] — this is the limiting statement on what a
manager can do.

### Easy does it

- "After 25 years of buying and supervising a great variety of businesses, Charlie
  and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have
  learned is to avoid them."
- The image: "we concentrated on identifying one-foot hurdles that we could step
  over rather than because we acquired any ability to clear seven-footers."
- Generalised: "in both business and investments it is usually far more profitable
  to simply stick with the easy and obvious than it is to resolve the difficult."
- With the exception named honestly: "a great investment opportunity occurs when a
  marvelous business encounters a one-time huge, but solvable, problem as was the
  case many years back at both American Express and GEICO."
- "Overall, however, we've done better by avoiding dragons than by slaying them."

See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

### The institutional imperative

Buffett's own label for the force he says he had never been warned about.

- "My most surprising discovery: the overwhelming importance in business of an
  unseen force that we might call "the institutional imperative.""
- "I thought then that decent, intelligent, and experienced managers would
  automatically make rational business decisions. But I learned over time that
  isn't so. Instead, rationality frequently wilts when the institutional imperative
  comes into play."
- The four mechanisms, quoted in full: "(1) As if governed by Newton's First Law of
  Motion, an institution will resist any change in its current direction; (2) Just
  as work expands to fill available time, corporate projects or acquisitions will
  materialize to soak up available funds; (3) Any business craving of the leader,
  however foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and
  strategic studies prepared by his troops; and (4) The behavior of peer companies,
  whether they are expanding, acquiring, setting executive compensation or
  whatever, will be mindlessly imitated."
- The diagnosis is structural, not moral: "Institutional dynamics, not venality or
  stupidity, set businesses on these courses, which are too often misguided."
- And it changed how Berkshire is organised: "After making some expensive mistakes
  because I ignored the power of the imperative, I have tried to organize and
  manage Berkshire in ways that minimize its influence."

Mechanisms (2) and (3) are the textile-reinvestment trap of 1985 stated as a
general law. See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

### People, as a hard filter

- "I learned to go into business only with people whom I like, trust, and admire."
- With the honest limit: "this policy of itself will not ensure success: A
  second-class textile or department-store company won't prosper simply because its
  managers are men that you would be pleased to see your daughter marry."
- And the flat rule: "We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad
  person."

See [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### The mistakes of omission

- "Some of my worst mistakes were not publicly visible. These were stock and
  business purchases whose virtues I understood and yet didn't make."
- The distinction that matters: "It's no sin to miss a great opportunity outside
  one's area of competence. But I have passed on a couple of really big purchases
  that were served up to me on a platter and that I was fully capable of
  understanding."
- "For Berkshire's shareholders, myself included, the cost of this thumb-sucking
  has been huge."

### Why leverage is refused

The clearest statement of risk tolerance in the letters, and it is not framed as
caution but as arithmetic about ruin.

- The concession first: "In retrospect, it is clear that significantly higher,
  though still conventional, leverage ratios at Berkshire would have produced
  considerably better returns on equity than the 23.8% we have actually averaged."
- The odds, granted generously: "Even in 1965, perhaps we could have judged there
  to be a 99% probability that higher leverage would lead to nothing but good."
- And rejected anyway: "We wouldn't have liked those 99:1 odds - and never will. A
  small chance of distress or disgrace cannot, in our view, be offset by a large
  chance of extra returns."
- The reason it costs nothing to refuse: "If your actions are sensible, you are
  certain to get good results; in most such cases, leverage just moves things along
  faster. Charlie and I have never been in a big hurry: We enjoy the process far
  more than the proceeds"

See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] — this is margin of safety applied to the balance
sheet rather than to a purchase price.

### Miscellaneous: Harry Bottle again, the NYSE specialist, the jet, and the Orpheum

- Harry Bottle, who rescued Dempster Mill in 1962 and K&W Products in 1987, returns for a third act: "last year Harry, now 70, attended a bankruptcy auction and, for a pittance, acquired a product line that is a natural for K&W." [[Sources/1989ltr]]
- Berkshire's NYSE specialist, Jim Maguire of Henderson Brothers, is credited with cutting dealer spreads from 3% or more to under 1%: "Jim has maintained the spread at 50 points or less, which at current prices is well under 1%. Shareholders who buy or sell benefit significantly from this reduction in transaction costs." [[Sources/1989ltr]]
- Berkshire sold its $850,000 corporate jet and bought a $6.7 million replacement, prompting a naming contest between Buffett's "The Charles T. Munger," Munger's "The Aberration," and the eventual choice: "We finally settled on "The Indefensible."" [[Sources/1989ltr]]
- About 96.9% of eligible shares participated in the 1989 shareholder-designated contributions program, distributing $5.9 million to 2,550 charities.
- The 1990 annual meeting moves to the Orpheum Theatre in downtown Omaha after 1989 attendance of about 1,000 outgrew the Joslyn Museum's Witherspoon Hall.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Coca-Cola increased to 23,350,000 shares

The position built in 1988 is roughly doubled, and Buffett tells the story
against himself.

- "Our holdings of Coca-Cola increased from 14,172,500 shares at the end of 1988 to
  23,350,000."
- "This Coca-Cola investment provides yet another example of the incredible speed
  with which your Chairman responds to investment opportunities, no matter how
  obscure or well-disguised they may be. I believe I had my first Coca-Cola in
  either 1935 or 1936."
- The fifty-two-year delay: "I continued to note these qualities for the next 52
  years as Coke blanketed the world. During this period, however, I carefully
  avoided buying even a single share, instead allocating major portions of my net
  worth to street railway companies, windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers,
  textile businesses, trading-stamp issuers, and the like."
- "Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes."
- What changed at the company: "After drifting somewhat in the 1970's, Coca-Cola
  had in 1981 become a new company with the move of Roberto Goizueta to CEO."

Note that the purchase is justified by management change plus a durable product —
not by a low price. It is the cigar-butt lesson applied.

### Three new convertible preferreds: Gillette, USAir, Champion

- **Gillette**: $600 million, 8¾% dividend, mandatory 10-year redemption, convertible at $50/share; Buffett joined the board.
- **USAir Group**: $358 million, 9¼% dividend, convertible at $60/share.
- **Champion International**: $300 million, 9¼% dividend, convertible at $38/share.

All three joined the 1987 Salomon convertible preferred as Berkshire's fixed-income redeployment of WPPSS and tax-exempt bond sale proceeds; all four carry full voting rights on an as-converted basis.

### Zero-Coupon Convertible Subordinated Debentures issued

Berkshire issued $902.6 million principal amount (15-year maturity, priced at 44.314% of maturity value, ~$400 million net proceeds), convertible at $9,815/share — financing raised opportunistically rather than against a specific need, consistent with the "financial firepower" policy stated in [[Sources/1980ltr]].

### Acquisition criteria, restated

The same six criteria published in 1986 are repeated unchanged, including
"demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest
to us, nor are "turnaround" situations)". See
[[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].
