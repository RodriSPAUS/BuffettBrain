# 2020 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire earned $42.5 billion under GAAP in 2020 ($21.9 billion operating earnings, $4.9 billion realized gains, a $26.7 billion unrealized-gains swing, and an $11 billion write-down — almost entirely a Precision Castparts impairment Buffett attributes to his own overpayment in 2016). Operating earnings actually fell 9% and no sizable acquisitions were made, though Berkshire repurchased about 5% of its own shares. The letter is structured around a defense of Berkshire's conglomerate structure ("Two Strings to Our Bow"), a tour of its four most valuable assets ("The Family Jewels"), a tribute to entrepreneurship in Omaha and Knoxville ("A Tale of Two Cities"), and a detailed history of who actually owns Berkshire stock and why ("The Berkshire Partnership"). The COVID-19 pandemic gets only passing mention — a six-week 2020 closure of Nebraska Furniture Mart — rather than sustained discussion.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The Precision Castparts Write-Down: An Admitted Overpayment
Buffett attributes the $11 billion write-down almost entirely to his 2016 purchase of Precision Castparts (PCC), stating plainly that he overpaid:

> "No one misled me in any way – I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential." [[Sources/2020ltr]]

He still credits PCC as a fine, well-run business under CEO Mark Donegan, framing the error as one of price rather than of business quality — his estimate of PCC's future earning power, not its durability, was wrong.

### Two Strings to Our Bow: Defending the Conglomerate Structure
Buffett contrasts Berkshire with the "prototype conglomerate," which historically had to overpay for mediocre businesses using an artificially inflated stock as acquisition currency — a dynamic he illustrates memorably: "I'll pay you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats." [[Sources/2020ltr]] Berkshire's alternative, made possible by decades of learning (including Buffett's own 20-year struggle with the original textile operation), is indifference to control: Berkshire will own all or part of a business, whichever makes more sense, based on competitive strength, management quality, and price. He closes the point by quoting Ronald Reagan: "It's said that hard work never killed anyone, but I say why take the chance?" [[Sources/2020ltr]]

### The Family Jewels: Berkshire's Four Most Valuable Assets
Buffett identifies four assets that hold most of Berkshire's value: the property/casualty insurance operation (53 years at Berkshire's core, run by Ajit Jain since 1986, with float of $138 billion at year-end — an equity-heavy investment strategy few competitors can match because of Berkshire's unusual capital strength); BNSF (100% owned); a 5.4% stake in Apple; and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (91% owned, earnings grown from $122 million to $3.4 billion over 21 years of ownership). [[Cases/GEICO]] and the wider insurance discussion are deferred to a reprint of the 2019 letter's insurance section rather than repeated in full.

### The Power of Repurchases, Illustrated by Apple
Berkshire spent $24.7 billion repurchasing the equivalent of 80,998 "A" shares in 2020, raising continuing shareholders' stake in every Berkshire business by 5.2% at no cost to them. Buffett uses the Apple position to make the mechanism vivid: Berkshire finished buying Apple shares in mid-2018 at 5.2% ownership for a $36 billion cost; by year-end 2020, after selling some shares for $11 billion, Berkshire's stake had actually risen to 5.4% — purely because Apple itself was repurchasing its own stock — and Berkshire's own concurrent buybacks meant shareholders owned 10% more of Apple's assets and earnings than they had in July 2018, without buying a single additional Apple share themselves.

> "And as a sultry Mae West assured us: 'Too much of a good thing can be . . . wonderful.'" [[Sources/2020ltr]]

### Investments: New Positions and the Top-15 Table
New 2020 positions included Chevron and Verizon, plus continued building of Merck, AbbVie and the Japanese trading house Itochu. The Kraft Heinz stake (325,442,152 shares, carried at $13.3 billion GAAP, market value only $11.3 billion) remained excluded from the ranked table. Top holdings by cost/market (in millions) included American Express ($1,287/$18,331, 18.8%), Apple ($31,089/$120,424, 5.4%), Bank of America ($14,631/$31,306, 11.9%), Coca-Cola ($1,299/$21,936, 9.3%), Moody's ($248/$7,160, 13.2%) and U.S. Bancorp ($5,638/$6,904, 9.8%), for total equity investments of $108,620M cost / $281,170M market.

### A Tale of Two Cities: Omaha and Knoxville
Buffett tells the founding stories of four Berkshire businesses built by local families: See's Candy (Mary See, 1921 Los Angeles); GEICO (Leo and Lillian Goodwin, $100,000 in 1936); National Indemnity (Jack Ringwalt, $125,000 in 1940, bought by Berkshire in a 15-minute handshake deal in 1967); and Nebraska Furniture Mart (Rose "Mrs. B" Blumkin, a Russian immigrant who started with $2,500 in 1936, whose 1946 store had a net worth of just $72,264 and $50 of cash). He also profiles Clayton Homes (Jim Clayton, founded 1956) and Pilot Travel Centers ("Big Jim" Haslam, founded 1958 with a single $6,000 service station), both Knoxville, Tennessee companies now earning over $1 billion pre-tax annually and employing about 47,000 combined.

> "Our unwavering conclusion: Never bet against America." [[Sources/2020ltr]]

### The Berkshire Partnership: Five Buckets of Owners
A detailed history traces Berkshire's shareholder base back to Buffett Partnership Ltd. (BPL, formed from 12 partnerships merged in 1962) and Munger's own partnership (1962–1977), through BPL's 1969 dissolution (distributing its 70.5% Berkshire stake pro-rata) and the 1983 Berkshire/Blue Chip Stamps merger. Buffett identifies five "buckets" of current ownership: his own (destined to empty via philanthropic distribution), index funds (mechanical holders), active institutional managers, active individual shareholders, and — the group he feels closest to — long-term individual "partners," exemplified by Stan Truhlsen, an Omaha ophthalmologist who joined Buffett's 1959 partnership Emdee, Ltd. and turned 100 in November 2020 still holding his original Berkshire shares. Buffett cites Berkshire's 1983 shareholder-principles opener as the enduring statement of this relationship:

> "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership." [[Sources/2020ltr]]

### BNSF and BHE: America's Largest Owner of Domestic Fixed Assets
Buffett highlights that Berkshire's depreciated cost of U.S. property, plant and equipment ($154 billion) exceeds that of any other American company, including AT&T ($127 billion). BNSF and BHE's combined earnings grew from $4.2 billion in 2011 (Berkshire's first full year owning BNSF) to $8.3 billion in 2020. BNSF has invested $41 billion in fixed assets since Berkshire's 2010 purchase (about $20 billion above depreciation) while paying Berkshire $41.8 billion in cumulative dividends; CEO Carl Ice retired at year-end, succeeded by Katie Farmer. BHE pays no common dividend and is mid-way through an $18 billion Western U.S. transmission-grid project begun in 2006 and expected to complete around 2030.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **GAAP earnings**: $42.5 billion, including an $11 billion write-down (almost entirely Precision Castparts)
- **Operating earnings fell 9%**; no sizable acquisitions made in 2020
- **Share repurchases**: About 5% of shares outstanding bought back, spending $24.7 billion (80,998 "A"-share equivalents)
- **New equity positions**: Chevron and Verizon added; Apple stake grew to 5.4% (via Apple's own buybacks, at no incremental Berkshire cost)
- **Insurance**: Float reached $138 billion at year-end
- **BNSF leadership change**: Carl Ice retired as CEO at year-end, succeeded by Katie Farmer
