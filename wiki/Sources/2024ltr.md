# 2024 Annual Letter Summary

Berkshire's per-share market value grew 25.5% in 2024, essentially matching the S&P 500's 25.0% total return, extending the compounded annual gain since 1965 to 19.9%. Operating earnings reached $47.4 billion despite 53% of Berkshire's 189 operating businesses individually reporting an earnings decline — offset by insurance strength (led by a major GEICO turnaround under Todd Combs) and rising Treasury bill income. The letter opens with an unusually direct essay on Berkshire's own mistakes, tells the story of the late Pete Liegl of Forest River, and closes with a record-breaking statistic: Berkshire's 2024 federal tax payment was the largest any single American company has ever made in a year, including the largest tech companies at their peak.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Mistakes — Yes, We Make Them at Berkshire
Buffett opens with a candid essay distinguishing capital-allocation mistakes (misjudging a business's future economics) from personnel mistakes (misjudging a manager's ability or "fidelity," which he says can hurt "beyond their financial impact, a pain that can approach that of a failed marriage"). He notes using the words "mistake" or "error" 16 times in his letters over 2019–2023, contrasting that with companies where such words are effectively forbidden at board meetings and analyst calls — a taboo he says "always made me nervous." The guiding principle for handling problems, credited to Tom Murphy:

> "Praise by name, criticize by category." [[Sources/2024ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

He also names the cardinal sin in fixing personnel mistakes — delay — quoting Charlie Munger's term for it: "thumb-sucking."

### Pete Liegl and Forest River
A tribute to Pete Liegl, founder of RV manufacturer Forest River, who died in November 2024 still working at 80. Liegl approached Berkshire directly to sell in 2005, and in their first meeting proposed his own compensation:

> "Well, I looked at Berkshire's proxy statement and I wouldn't want to make more than my boss, so pay me $100,000 per year." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

Liegl added a request for a 10% bonus on earnings growth above the company's then-current level — a handshake structure that held for the next 19 years, during which "no competitor came close to his performance." Buffett uses the story to reject academic pedigree as a hiring filter, also citing Bill Gates's decision to leave Harvard and Ben Rosner (a retailing manager for Buffett and Munger) who, per his step-granddaughter, "never went past 6th grade." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

### Last Year's Performance: GEICO's Turnaround Leads Insurance Strength
Operating earnings reached $47.4 billion in 2024 (versus $37.35 billion in 2023) even though 53% of Berkshire's 189 operating businesses reported lower individual earnings, offset by rising investment income from an enlarged Treasury bill position and a major insurance improvement. Buffett credits Todd Combs with a five-year turnaround of GEICO's efficiency and underwriting practices, calling 2024's improvement "spectacular" though not yet complete. Full 2023–2024 operating-earnings breakdown (in millions): insurance-underwriting $9,020 (2024) vs. $5,428 (2023); insurance-investment income $13,670 vs. $9,567; BNSF $5,031 vs. $5,087; Berkshire Hathaway Energy $3,730 vs. $2,331; other controlled businesses $13,362 vs. $13,072; non-controlled businesses (Kraft Heinz, Occidental, Berkadia, etc.) $1,750 vs. $1,519; other (including FX gains on yen debt) $1,395 vs. -$175; for total operating earnings of $47,437M vs. $37,350M. Late in the year, Berkshire raised its Berkshire Hathaway Energy ownership from about 92% to 100% for roughly $3.9 billion ($2.9 billion cash, the balance in Berkshire "B" shares).

### Surprise, Surprise: The Largest Tax Payment Any Company Has Ever Made
Buffett contrasts 1965, when Berkshire's textile business paid no federal income tax at all, with 2024, when Berkshire's four IRS payments totaled $26.8 billion — about 5% of everything corporate America paid the Treasury that year, and more than any single American company (including the largest tech companies at peak valuation) has ever paid in one year. He notes this was achieved despite Berkshire having paid only one cash dividend in its 60-year history — $101,755 total ($0.10 per A share) on January 3, 1967 — with cumulative federal income tax payments since 1965 now exceeding $101 billion. He renders the scale physically: sending the Treasury $1 million every 20 minutes for all of 2024 would still have left Berkshire owing money into January of the following year. The section closes:

> "So thank you, Uncle Sam." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

### Where Your Money Is: Controlled vs. Marketable Equities
Berkshire's 189 controlled subsidiaries (generally 100%-owned) are described as including "a few rare gems, many good-but-far-from-fabulous businesses and some laggards" alongside a dozen or so large marketable-equity holdings (Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Moody's, etc.) valued at $272 billion at year-end 2024, down from $354 billion the year before as Berkshire trimmed positions. Buffett distinguishes the two forms of ownership by flexibility: marketable stakes can be exited (though Berkshire's scale now makes even that slow), while controlled businesses are almost never sold except when problems appear unending. He reaffirms that despite an unusually large cash position, "the great majority of your money remains in equities" and that this preference is permanent — Berkshire will never prefer cash-equivalents over good businesses.

### Property-Casualty Insurance: The "Money-Up-Front, Loss-Payments-Later" Model
A dedicated essay explains why P/C insurance's unusual cash-collection timing (premiums received upfront, losses sometimes not fully known for 30+ years — Berkshire is still paying asbestos claims from 50+ years ago) creates a structural risk of "fictitious profits" being reported for years by insurers with optimistic or dishonest management. Over the past two decades, Berkshire's insurance operation generated $32 billion of after-tax underwriting profit (about 3.3 cents per dollar of sales after tax) while float grew from $46 billion to $171 billion. Buffett quotes Mike Goldberg, the Berkshire executive who originally recruited Ajit Jain, on the right underwriting temperament:

> "We want our underwriters to daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed." [[Sources/2024ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

### Berkshire Increases Its Japanese Investments
Nearly six years after first buying into the five Japanese trading houses (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo, all initially purchased starting July 2019), Berkshire's aggregate cost stood at $13.8 billion against a year-end market value of $23.5 billion. The five companies agreed to relax Berkshire's self-imposed 10% ownership ceiling as Berkshire approached it, meaning stakes are likely to rise further. The position remains financed mostly with fixed-rate yen-denominated debt for currency-neutrality, producing a $2.3 billion cumulative after-tax GAAP gain from yen movements ($850 million of it in 2024 alone). Expected 2025 dividend income from the five holdings (~$812 million) is projected to far exceed the roughly $135 million annual interest cost of the yen debt funding them.

### The Annual Meeting and an Update on Bertie
The 2025 meeting (May 3) featured a new commemorative book, *60 Years of Berkshire Hathaway*, created by former Berkshire staffer Carrie Sova, with proceeds from 20 signed copies benefiting Omaha's Stephen Center homeless shelter (matched by Buffett). Bertie, introduced as Buffett's shareholder stand-in in the 2023 letter, returns at age 91, and Buffett closes with a characteristically wry aside about her using a cane at the meeting.
