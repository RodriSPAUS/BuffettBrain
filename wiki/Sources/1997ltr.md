# 1997 Annual Letter

The letter of a very good year that Buffett refuses to be pleased about. Book value per share
rose 34.1%, and he spends the opening pages explaining why that number proves nothing — the
index rose almost as fast, and anyone can look clever in a bull market. The substance is
elsewhere: the clearest statement of the cost of float he had yet written, a warning that a
catastrophic year in super-cat reinsurance is a certainty rather than a risk, an admission that
every share he has ever issued in a merger has cost shareholders money, and the observation
that today's prices have eaten the margin of safety.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The duck rating: a gain that proves nothing

Net worth rose $8.0 billion in 1997, lifting per-share book value of both Class A and Class B
stock by 34.1%. Over 33 years under present management, per-share book value went from $19 to
$25,488 — 24.1% compounded annually. Buffett immediately deflates it:

> "In a bull market, one must avoid the error of the preening duck that quacks boastfully after
> a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world."
> — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

A right-thinking duck compares itself to the other ducks on the pond. His verdict on 1997:
"Quack." The letter notes that passive ducks in the S&P Index rose almost as fast, without
giving the index figure in the text — the comparison table sits on the facing page and is not
in `Raw/`, so no S&P number should be quoted from this summary.

He also names the structural handicap: the Index bears no tax costs and neither do mutual
funds, whereas Berkshire paid or accrued $4.2 billion in federal income tax, about 18% of
beginning net worth. Berkshire must overcome that drag to justify its existence, and a modest
advantage over the Index across time is the yardstick he asks shareholders to use — not the
Chicago Cubs fan's standard: "Everyone has a bad century now and then."

### The two-column view of intrinsic value

Buffett repeats the table he considers central to estimating intrinsic value, splitting
Berkshire into an entity holding the investments and an entity operating the businesses and
bearing all corporate costs:

| Year | Investments per share | Pre-tax earnings per share excluding all income from investments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1967 | $41 | $1.09 |
| 1977 | $372 | $12.44 |
| 1987 | $3,910 | $108.14 |
| 1997 | $38,043 | $717.82 |

The second column is aimed at pundits who treat Berkshire as a de facto investment company and
ignore what its 38,000 employees contribute. Since the first business acquisition in 1967,
pre-tax operating earnings have grown from $1 million to $888 million — and that is after
assigning *all* corporate overhead ($6.6 million), interest ($66.9 million) and shareholder
contributions ($15.4 million) to the operating side, though some could fairly sit with the
investments.

In 1997 investments rose $9,543 per share (33.5%) and operating earnings $296.43 per share
(70.3%). He flags the caveat himself: super-cat was lucky and GEICO's underwriting gain was well
above a normal year, so 1997 operating earnings exceeded both expectation and what he expects
for 1998. See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### Size is the enemy of return

> "For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

The arithmetic is personal. In 1951, attending Ben Graham's class at Columbia, an idea worth a
$10,000 gain improved his performance for the year by a full 100 percentage points; an idea
producing a $500 million pre-tax profit now adds one percentage point. His 1950s results beat
any subsequent decade by nearly thirty percentage points. The explanation is not skill:

> "We weren't smarter then, just smaller." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

### Ted Williams and the fat pitch

Prices are high for both businesses and stocks. Buffett takes no view on whether they will
fall, only that fresh money buys relatively little in prospective earnings. The discipline he
invokes is Ted Williams carving the strike zone into 77 cells in *The Science of Hitting*:
swinging only at the best cell would allow a .400 average, reaching for the low outside corner
would drop him to .230.

> "In other words, waiting for the fat pitch would mean a trip to the Hall of Fame; swinging
> indiscriminately would mean a ticket to the minors." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

Today's business pitches are barely catching the lower outside corner. And he admits the cost
of the discipline honestly: "just standing there, day after day, with my bat on my shoulder is
not my idea of fun". This is [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]] and
[[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]] expressed as patience rather than as criteria.

### Unconventional commitments: oil, silver and zeros

When the favourite commitment — a well-run, sensibly priced business — cannot be found, cash
equivalents are the default, but 1997 had three exceptions. Buffett draws the distinction
plainly: with a wonderful business at an attractive price he *knows* he will make money and
only the timing is open; with these he only thinks so, and expects occasional substantial
losses.

- **Oil derivatives** — 14.0 million barrels remaining of a 45.7 million barrel position
  established in 1994-95. Contracts for 31.7 million barrels settled in 1995-97 for a pre-tax
  gain of about $61.9 million; the remainder carried an unrealized gain of $11.6 million at
  yearend and expires in 1998-99. He now has no opinion on oil's attractiveness.
- **Silver** — 111.2 million ounces purchased, a pre-tax gain of $97.4 million marked to market.
  Bullion inventories had fallen materially and he concluded a higher price was needed for
  equilibrium between supply and demand. He is explicit that inflation expectations play no part
  in the calculation.
- **Zero-coupon Treasuries** — $4.6 billion at amortized cost, the largest of the three, showing
  an unrealized pre-tax gain of $598.8 million after rates fell. A macro-based commitment that
  never has anything close to certainty, taken because shareholders pay him to use judgment
  rather than to avoid embarrassment.

The alignment note: the Munger family has more than 90% of its net worth in Berkshire and the
Buffetts more than 99%. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

### A net saver should want prices to fall

The section is one of the letter's most quoted arguments and turns on a quiz — if you eat
hamburgers and do not raise cattle, do you want beef cheaper or dearer? Investors who will be
net buyers for years nonetheless rejoice when prices rise, which makes no sense.

> "Prospective purchasers should much prefer sinking prices." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

For Berkshire owners the case is stronger still: the company retains all earnings and saves on
their behalf, and their investees repurchase shares — the buybacks Coca-Cola, The Washington
Post and Wells Fargo made at low prices in earlier years benefited Berkshire far more than
today's, made at loftier prices. About 97% of Berkshire's shares are held at year end by the
same investors who held them at the start, which makes the shareholder body savers by
definition.

> "there is a buyer for every seller and what hurts one necessarily helps the other"
> — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

He gained enormously from the low prices of the 1970s and 1980s: markets hostile to investment
transients were friendly to those taking up permanent residence. This is the same disposition
as [[Concepts/MrMarket]], argued from the saver's balance sheet rather than from temperament.

### Float, and how to measure its cost

The clearest float exposition in the corpus to this point, and Buffett says outright that
without it no informed judgment about Berkshire's intrinsic value is possible.

> "To begin with, float is money we hold but don't own." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

Float arises because premiums come in before losses are paid. Typically the premiums do not
cover the eventual losses and expenses, and that underwriting loss *is* the cost of float. The
business has value if that cost is below what the company would otherwise pay for money, and is
a lemon if it is above market rates.

The caution attached is as important as the definition: because loss costs must be estimated,
insurers have enormous latitude in figuring underwriting results, errors are usually innocent
but sometimes not, and the general public can typically do no more than accept what is
presented. He notes he has been amazed at numbers big-name auditors have implicitly blessed.
Berkshire's own posture is conservative because virtually all surprises in insurance are
unpleasant.

The float table runs 1967–1997. Average float reached $7,093.1 million in 1997 with an
underwriting profit, so the cost of funds was less than zero for the fifth consecutive year:

> "In effect, we have been paid for holding money." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

Since entering insurance in 1967, float has compounded at 21.7% annually — and has cost less
than nothing. Hence the accounting irony he draws out: float sits on the balance sheet as a
liability while being worth more to Berkshire than an equal amount of net worth. He warns that
the expiration of several large contracts will shrink float in the first quarter of 1998, while
expecting long-term growth. See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

### Super-cat: the bad year is a certainty, not a risk

The counterweight to the float story. Super-cat is the most volatile line in insurance —
large profits in most years and a huge loss occasionally — so its attractiveness takes many
years to measure.

> "a truly terrible year in the super-cat business is not a possibility -- it's a certainty.
> The only question is when it will come." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

1997 was lucky: no catastrophe caused huge insured damage and virtually all premiums dropped to
the bottom line. The dark side is that "innocent" investors have entered reinsurance by buying
catastrophe bonds, and Buffett dismantles the label — the second word is an Orwellian misnomer,
since "A true bond obliges the issuer to pay" while these lay a provisional promise to pay on
the purchaser. The structure exists to circumvent laws prohibiting unlicensed entities from
writing insurance, with the side benefit that calling it a bond makes it sound safer than it is.

The dice illustration is the most transferable piece of reasoning in the letter: the odds of
throwing a 12 are 1 in 36; agree to pay $50 million if a 12 appears and take a $1 million annual
premium, and you have badly underpriced the risk — yet there is a 75.4% probability of going a
full decade without paying out a dime before eventually going broke. Real hurricanes and
earthquakes are fuzzier still, which plays into promoters' hands, since an expert can always be
found to say the chance is 1 in 100:

> "The expert puts no money on the table." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

He adds, in fairness, that the expert probably believes his own odds — which makes him less
reprehensible but more dangerous. Money flowing into catastrophe bonds has deteriorated
super-cat pricing materially, so Berkshire will write less business in 1998; large multi-year
contracts (Florida hurricanes, and the California Earthquake Authority policy) mitigate the
drop. Worst-case loss remains about $600 million after-tax, roughly 1% of Berkshire's market
value — and he would gladly increase that exposure at appropriate prices. The business was
built from scratch by Ajit Jain:

> "Ajit would have been a star in whatever career he chose; fortunately for us, he enjoys
> insurance." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

See [[Applications/RedFlags]] for the mispricing-hidden-by-time pattern.

### GEICO accelerating, and paid for what it controls

Tony Nicely, 54, with GEICO 36 years, had his best year. Voluntary auto policies in force grew
10% in 1996 and 16% in 1997:

| Year | New voluntary auto policies | Voluntary auto policies in force |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1993 | 354,882 | 2,011,055 |
| 1994 | 396,217 | 2,147,549 |
| 1995 | 461,608 | 2,310,037 |
| 1996 | 617,669 | 2,543,699 |
| 1997 | 913,176 | 2,949,439 |

Underwriting profit was 8.1% of premiums — which Buffett calls *higher than we wish it to be*,
because the goal is to pass most of the low-cost advantage to customers and hold to about 4%.
Rates were cut a little in 1997 and may be cut again. The industry as a whole had unsustainably
good results, and he welcomes the coming squeeze on margins: a tough market helps the low-cost
operator.

Compensation is the theme underneath. The profit-sharing chart had to be enlarged for the
second year running: 10,500 associates received 26.9% of base compensation, $71 million, against
1996's record 16.9%. Two variables set it — policy growth and profitability of seasoned
business — and the same two drove executive bonuses starting with Tony.

> "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets" — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

No Berkshire subsidiary ties compensation to the stock price, which associates cannot affect in
any meaningful way. See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Cases/GEICO]].

Growth is being funded deliberately: 1998 marketing spend will top $100 million, up 50%, against
a 3% market share in an industry doing about $115 billion of volume annually. Buffett also
records his debt to Lorimer Davidson, who in 1951 explained GEICO and its industry to a
20-year-old stranger who had turned up uninvited; Davy turned 95 and travel is difficult, and
Buffett hopes to get him to the annual meeting.

### The other primary insurers

Smaller than GEICO and, in aggregate, "fully as stunning": National Indemnity's traditional
business earned a 32.9% underwriting profit under Don Wurster (24.3% over three years); the
homestate operation 14.1% under Rod Eldred while absorbing geographical expansion (three-year
15.1%); workers' compensation under Brad Kinstler took a modest loss in a difficult environment
(three-year +1.5%); Central States Indemnity under John Kizer set a volume record; and Kansas
Bankers Surety under Don Towle more than met expectations from its 1996 purchase. The five
together recorded a 15.0% underwriting profit.

### Reported earnings, and why subsidiary records understate them

Pre-tax operating earnings were $1,715.7 million (1996: $1,221.4 million), $1,194.5 million
after taxes and minority interests. Capital gains of $1,111.9 million pre-tax brought total
earnings to $2,827.6 million pre-tax and $1,901.6 million after — down from 1996's $3,705.9
million and $2,488.6 million, because 1996 carried far larger capital gains. Helzberg's improved
after a disappointing 1996, following decisive early action by CEO Jeff Comment.

The argument worth carrying forward: comparing a Berkshire subsidiary's earnings history to
publicly-owned peers understates it, because public companies retain two-thirds or more of
earnings to fund their own growth while Berkshire subsidiaries pay 100% up to the parent. The
Buffalo News, See's and Scott Fetzer alone have paid $1.8 billion to Berkshire, deployed
elsewhere. See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]], [[Cases/BuffaloNews]] and [[Cases/SeeCandies]].

### Look-through earnings

Reported earnings are a poor measure of economic progress because they include only dividends
received from investees, and Buffett prefers the undistributed portion when investees can
reinvest at high rates. Look-through earnings add Berkshire's share of investees' retained
operating earnings and subtract a hypothetical tax at 14%, the rate Berkshire pays on dividends
received.

| Investee | Ownership at yearend | Berkshire's share of undistributed operating earnings ($m) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| American Express | 10.7% | 161 |
| Coca-Cola | 8.1% | 216 |
| Walt Disney | 3.2% | 65 |
| Freddie Mac | 8.6% | 86 |
| Gillette | 8.6% | 82 |
| The Washington Post | 16.5% | 30 |
| Wells Fargo | 7.8% | 103 |

Share of undistributed earnings $743 million, less $105 million hypothetical tax, plus $1,292
million of reported operating earnings, gives total look-through earnings of $1,930 million. See
[[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]].

### Acquisitions: Star Furniture and International Dairy Queen

Both agreed in 1997 (IDQ closing early 1998), both meeting the standard criteria — understandable,
excellent economics, run by outstanding people.

**Star Furniture** is a fourteen-year referral chain. On buying [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]]
in 1983, Buffett asked the Blumkins the question he always asks:

> "Are there any more at home like you?" — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

They named three outstanding furniture retailers; none was for sale. Years later Irv Blumkin
learned Bill Child of R.C. Willey — one of the three — might merge, which became the 1995 deal;
asked the same question, Bill Child named the remaining two, one being Star Furniture of
Houston. Bob Denham of Salomon relayed that Melvyn Wolff wanted to talk on the Thursday before
the 1997 annual meeting; Wolff came to Omaha, and a few days later they made the deal in a
single two-hour session in New York.

> "I knew I was dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

No leases checked, no employment contracts worked out. The Wolff family's association with Star
dates to 1924, though the business struggled until Melvyn and his sister Shirley Toomin took
over in 1962; it now operates 12 stores — ten in Houston, one each in Austin and Bryan — with
San Antonio next. The detail Buffett lingers on is that on announcing the sale, Melvyn and
Shirley made large special payments to everyone in the business, funded from their own proceeds
rather than Berkshire's. The transaction closed July 1. See [[Principles/Fidelity]].

**International Dairy Queen**: 5,792 stores in 23 countries, nearly all franchisee-run, plus 409
Orange Julius and 43 Karmelkorn operations, with 190 locations combining them. A Minneapolis
group led by John Mooty and Rudy Luther took control in 1970 and rationalized a jumble of
franchise agreements and unwise financing. Luther's death forced his estate to sell, and
Berkshire offered the FlightSafety structure — cash or Berkshire shares of slightly lower
immediate value, deliberately tilted to encourage cash. Even so, only 45% of IDQ shares elected
cash.

### A Confession: issuing stock has cost shareholders money

The most self-critical section of the letter, and the one most worth carrying into
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]. Aggregating all stock-only mergers (excluding Diversified
Retailing and Blue Chip Stamps), shareholders are slightly worse off than if the transactions
had not happened.

> "Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money."
> — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

He is explicit that no seller misled him and none failed to manage well afterwards. The problem
is structural: Berkshire owns a marvellous collection of businesses, so trading a portion of
them for something new almost never makes sense.

> "For a baseball team, acquiring a player who can be expected to bat .350 is almost always a
> wonderful event" — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

— except when a .380 hitter must be traded to get him. "Marrying well is extremely difficult for
Berkshire." Cash acquisitions, by contrast, have gone far better: National Indemnity in 1967,
then See's, Buffalo News, Scott Fetzer and GEICO among others.

The generalisable rule: a takeover premium makes sense only if the acquirer's stock is
overvalued relative to the acquiree's, or the two will earn more combined than separately. Few
acquirers will admit the first, so they assert the second — though voracious share issuers are
tacitly conceding the first, and are often running Wall Street's version of a chain letter. He
also attacks options accounting here: an acquiree's reported costs *rise* after purchase because
Berkshire replaces option plans with equivalent cash compensation, bringing a real cost out of
the closet.

> "Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

### The portfolio, and a market that has eaten its margin of safety

Holdings above $750 million in market value at 12/31/97:

| Shares | Company | Cost ($m) | Market ($m) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 49,456,900 | American Express | 1,392.7 | 4,414.0 |
| 200,000,000 | Coca-Cola | 1,298.9 | 13,337.5 |
| 21,563,414 | Walt Disney | 381.2 | 2,134.8 |
| 63,977,600 | Freddie Mac | 329.4 | 2,683.1 |
| 48,000,000 | Gillette | 600.0 | 4,821.0 |
| 23,733,198 | Travelers Group | 604.4 | 1,278.6 |
| 1,727,765 | The Washington Post | 10.6 | 840.6 |
| 6,690,218 | Wells Fargo | 412.6 | 2,270.9 |
| | Others | 2,177.1 | 4,467.2 |
| | **Total** | **7,206.9** | **36,247.7** |

Net sales during the year came to about 5% of the beginning portfolio — mostly positions below
the itemization threshold, with modest trims to larger ones, partly to shift the bond-stock
ratio in response to relative values. Reported positions sometimes reflect Lou Simpson, who runs
an equity portfolio of nearly $2 billion at GEICO independently.

On valuation, Buffett gives a conditional rather than a forecast: at the 1997 annual meeting,
with the Dow at 7,071 and long-term Treasury yields at 6.89%, he and Munger said the market was
not overvalued *if* rates stayed or fell and American business kept earning its recent
remarkable returns on equity. Rates have fallen, returns on equity remain exceptionally high —
so on those conditions stocks are not generally overvalued. But returns on equity are not a sure
thing to persist. In 1979 he wrote a *Forbes* article titled "You pay a very high price in the
stock market for a cheery consensus"; now the consensus is very cheery. Corporate America earns
far more than a few years ago and lower rates make each dollar of earnings more valuable, so it
is not automatically the wrong time to buy — but today's price levels have materially eroded
what Ben Graham identified as "the cornerstone of intelligent investing". See
[[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

**Roberto Goizueta**, Coca-Cola's CEO since 1981, died in October. Buffett read all of the more
than 100 letters and notes Goizueta had written him over nine years and says they could serve as
a guidebook for success in business and life — a brilliant strategic vision aimed at
shareholders' well-being, coupled with a burning sense of urgency.

> "By the way, I have told Olguita that what she refers to as an obsession, you call focus. I
> like your term much better." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

Goizueta had prepared the succession long before it seemed necessary, working with Doug Ivester
so no momentum would be lost. See [[Concepts/Moat]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### The convertible preferreds, resolved

An update on the five convertible preferreds bought privately in 1987-1991. Champion
International had already been sold at a small profit. Of the rest, all four grew significantly:

- **Gillette** — $600 million committed in 1989, worth $4.8 billion at yearend.
- **First Empire State** — $40 million committed in 1991, worth $236 million.
- **Salomon** — merged into Travelers Group. Buffett credits Deryck Maughan and Bob Denham with
  saving Salomon after its 1991 scandal and restoring it enough to make it an attractive
  acquisition. Of 1991-92 he quotes the drama critic: "I would have enjoyed the play except that
  I had an unfortunate seat. It faced the stage."
- **US Airways** — the resuscitation "borders on the miraculous", and he takes none of the
  credit.

> "Those who have watched my moves in this investment know that I have compiled a record that is
> unblemished by success." — [[Sources/1997ltr]]

He was wrong to buy and wrong again in repeatedly trying to unload at 50 cents on the dollar.
Two changes coincided with the rebound — he and Munger leaving the board, and Stephen Wolf
becoming CEO — and he says the second was the key. The company made up the dividend arrearages
with extra payments for the delay; the common rose from a low of $4 to a recent high of $73; the
preferred was called for redemption on March 15, and conversion rights once thought worthless
turned valuable.

Separately, $300 million of American Express Percs bought in 1991 became a $3 billion gain, which
he attributes to "110% luck, the balance skill": mulling a sale before the August 1994
conversion, he played golf with Frank Olson of Hertz, who convinced him by the second green that
Amex's corporate card was a terrific franchise. He turned buyer on the back nine and Berkshire
owned 10% of the company within months. Harvey Golub's management was the reason to hold at all.
See [[Cases/GEICO]] for the parallel of a franchise recognized late.

### Housekeeping, contributions and the meeting

- **Quarterly reports** — printed copies now go only to shareholders who request them, as costs
  balloon; all shareholders still receive the printed annual report. Quarterly information will
  be filed with the SEC and posted to the website on the same Friday nights (May 15, August 14,
  November 13, 1998), so direct and street-name holders are on an equal footing. The website
  carries every annual letter since 1977.
- **Shareholder-designated contributions** — 97.7% of eligible shares participated; $15.4 million
  went to 3,830 charities. Over the program's 17 years Berkshire has contributed $113.1 million
  at shareholders' direction, and subsidiaries gave a further $8.1 million in 1997, including
  $4.4 million in kind.
- **The annual meeting** — May 2-4, 1998 at Aksarben. About 7,500 attended in 1997 from all 50
  states and 16 countries; capacity is now over 11,000. The letter's closing pages are the usual
  Omaha itinerary — NFM, Borsheim's under Susan Jacques, Gorat's, the Dairy Queen on 114th, and
  the Royals game where Buffett promises to finally throw his curve ball, having abstained since
  learning that Harvard's president once declared "Harvard is not in the business of teaching
  deception."

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired or agreed**

- **Star Furniture** — agreed 1997, closed July 1. Twelve stores in Houston, Austin and Bryan.
  Sourced through the referral chain that began with the Blumkins in 1983 and passed through
  Bill Child of R.C. Willey.
- **International Dairy Queen** — agreed 1997, closed early 1998. 5,792 stores in 23 countries.
  Structured cash-or-shares with the tilt toward cash; 45% of shares elected cash.

**Non-traditional positions held at yearend**

- 14.0 million barrels of oil derivative contracts, expiring 1998-99, unrealized gain $11.6
  million.
- 111.2 million ounces of silver, pre-tax gain $97.4 million for the year.
- $4.6 billion at amortized cost of long-term zero-coupon U.S. Treasury obligations, unrealized
  pre-tax gain $598.8 million.

**Sold or resolved**

- Net equity sales of about 5% of the beginning portfolio, part of a bond-stock realignment
  continued into 1998.
- Salomon preferred resolved through the merger into Travelers Group.
- US Airways preferred called for redemption on March 15, 1998, with arrearages paid and
  conversion rights now valuable.

**Not done, and why**

- No major new equity commitment. Prices are high for both businesses and stocks; the available
  pitches are barely in the strike zone, and Buffett would rather hold short-term instruments
  than lock in low returns.
- Super-cat volume will be cut in 1998 because catastrophe-bond money has deteriorated pricing —
  a deliberate contraction, not a loss of appetite. He would increase worst-case exposure beyond
  $600 million after-tax at appropriate prices.
- Share issuance is to be avoided in future acquisitions, with a cash option attached whenever
  stock must be offered.
