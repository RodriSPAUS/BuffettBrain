# 1990 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1990: a 7.3% gain in net worth, the Wells Fargo purchase during a
banking panic, the entry into super-catastrophe reinsurance, and the letter that
defines how Berkshire actually measures insurance — cost of float, not combined
ratio.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Cost of float — the measurement that replaces the combined ratio

The most durable contribution of this letter. Buffett rejects the industry's
standard yardstick and substitutes one that treats insurance as borrowing.

- The industry's rule of thumb: "the typical property-casualty insurer can absorb
  losses and expenses that exceed premiums by 7% to 11% and still be able to break
  even on its business."
- Why the ratio alone misleads — float varies enormously by line: "insurance
  covering losses to crops from hail damage produces virtually no float at all."
  Against: "malpractice insurance covering the potential liabilities of doctors,
  lawyers and accountants produces a very high amount of float compared to annual
  premium volume."
- The trap in long-tail business: "Liability business written in a given year and
  presumed at first to have produced a combined ratio of 115 may eventually smack
  the insurer with 200, 300 or worse when the years have rolled by and all claims
  have finally been settled."
- The replacement measure: "We believe a better measure, however, to be a
  comparison of underwriting loss to float developed."
- And what it tells you: "when the ratio takes in a period of years, it gives a
  rough indication of the cost of funds generated by insurance operations. A low
  cost of funds signifies a good business; a high cost translates into a poor
  business."

See [[Concepts/Float]].

### Margin of safety, applied to underwriting

- The operating principle: "Though certain long-tail lines may prove profitable at
  combined ratios of 110 or 115, insurers will invariably find it unprofitable to
  price using those ratios as targets."
- "Instead, prices must provide a healthy margin of safety against the societal
  trends that are forever springing expensive surprises on the insurance industry."
- The conclusion, stated flatly: "Setting a target of 100 can itself result in
  heavy losses; aiming for 110 - 115 is business suicide."

This is [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] moved from the purchase price to the premium —
the same idea in a different market.

### Super-cat: concentrating risk on purpose

Berkshire enters catastrophe reinsurance, and the letter is unusually explicit
about what that will do to reported results.

- Who buys it and why: "The buyers of these policies are reinsurance companies
  that themselves are in the business of writing catastrophe coverage for primary
  insurers and that wish to "lay off," or rid themselves, of part of their
  exposure to catastrophes of special severity."
- The competitive advantage: "Because the need for these buyers to collect on such
  a policy will only arise at times of extreme stress - perhaps even chaos - in the
  insurance business, they seek financially strong sellers. And here we have a
  major competitive advantage: In the industry, our strength is unmatched."
- The admission that inverts standard insurance practice: "Note that we are not
  spreading risk as insurers typically do; we are concentrating it."
- What that does to the numbers: "our yearly combined ratio on this business will
  almost never fall in the industry range of 100 - 120, but will instead be close
  to either zero or 300%."
- Why competitors cannot follow: "Most insurers are financially unable to tolerate
  such swings. And if they have the ability to do so, they often lack the desire."
- The shareholder bargain, stated as a preference: "we believe that Berkshire
  shareholders, if properly informed, can handle unusual volatility in profits so
  long as the swings carry with them the prospect of superior long-term results.
  (Charlie and I always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%.)"
- And three warnings given in advance, including: "While we expect our super-cat
  business to produce satisfactory results over, say, a decade, we're sure it will
  produce absolutely terrible results in at least an occasional year"
- On the limits of analysis here: "Our expectations can be based on little more
  than subjective judgments - for this kind of insurance, historical loss data are
  of very limited value to us as we decide what rates to charge today"

See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]] — telling owners about the
downside before it arrives is the fidelity standard in operation.

### Falling prices are good news for a buyer

The clearest statement in the letters of why volatility helps rather than hurts.

- The food analogy: "Investors who expect to be ongoing buyers of investments
  throughout their lifetimes should adopt a similar attitude toward market
  fluctuations; instead many illogically become euphoric when stock prices rise and
  unhappy when they fall. They show no such confusion in their reaction to food
  prices"
- Applied to Berkshire: "We will be buying businesses - or small parts of
  businesses, called stocks - year in, year out as long as I live... Given these
  intentions, declining prices for businesses benefit us, and rising prices hurt
  us."
- Where cheapness comes from: "The most common cause of low prices is pessimism -
  some times pervasive, some times specific to a company or industry."
- And the sentence that separates this from contrarianism: "None of this means,
  however, that a business or stock is an intelligent purchase simply because it is
  unpopular; a contrarian approach is just as foolish as a follow-the-crowd
  strategy. What's required is thinking rather than polling."

See [[Concepts/MrMarket]].

### Price versus value, for the shareholder's own return

- "Berkshire's corporate gains will produce an identical gain for a specific
  shareholder only if he eventually sells his shares at the same relationship to
  intrinsic value that existed when he bought them."
- Which is why Buffett wants the discount stable: "Charlie Munger, Berkshire's Vice
  Chairman and my partner, and I hope for Berkshire to sell consistently at about
  intrinsic value."
- With the two-year illustration: "In 1989 intrinsic value grew less than did book
  value, which was up 44%, while the market price rose 85%; in 1990 book value and
  intrinsic value increased by a small amount, while the market price fell 23%."
- And the standing admission of imprecision: "We can't tell you the exact
  differential because intrinsic value is necessarily an estimate; Charlie and I
  might, in fact, differ by 10% in our appraisals."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### Wells Fargo — just under 10%, for $290 million

The year's major purchase, made into a collapsing sector.

- The stated aversion to the industry: "The banking business is no favorite of
  ours. When assets are twenty times equity - a common ratio in this industry -
  mistakes that involve only a small portion of assets can destroy a major portion
  of equity."
- The cause of those mistakes, named as the same force identified in 1989: "Most
  have resulted from a managerial failing that we described last year when
  discussing the "institutional imperative:" the tendency of executives to
  mindlessly imitate the behavior of their peers, no matter how foolish it may be
  to do so."
- Which is why cheapness alone is not enough: "Because leverage of 20:1 magnifies
  the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses, we have no interest in
  purchasing shares of a poorly-managed bank at a "cheap" price. Instead, our only
  interest is in buying into well-managed banks at fair prices."
- The price paid: "we purchased our 10% interest in Wells Fargo for $290 million,
  less than five times after-tax earnings, and less than three times pre-tax
  earnings."
- The stress test, done in public: "If 10% of all $48 billion of the bank's loans -
  not just its real estate loans - were hit by problems in 1991, and these produced
  losses (including foregone interest) averaging 30% of principal, the company
  would roughly break even."
- And the reaction to the price halving: "fears of a California real estate
  disaster similar to that experienced in New England caused the price of Wells
  Fargo stock to fall almost 50% within a few months during 1990. Even though we
  had bought some shares at the prices prevailing before the fall, we welcomed the
  decline because it allowed us to pick up many more shares at the new, panic
  prices."

Note the structure of the argument: an industry he dislikes, bought anyway because
of management, at a price that survives an explicitly stated disaster scenario. It
is [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] applied together.

### Everything else left alone

- "This year we neither bought nor sold a share of five of our six major holdings."
- Holdings at year end include Coca-Cola at 46,700,000 shares (cost $1,023,920
  thousand, market $2,171,550 thousand) — roughly doubled again from 1989.

### RJR Nabisco bonds — $440 million

- "Just as buying into the banking business is unusual for us, so is the purchase
  of below-investment-grade bonds."
- The principle that permits it: "we will look at any category of investment, so
  long as we understand the business we're buying into and believe that price and
  value may differ significantly."
