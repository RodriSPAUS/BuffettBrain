# 2002 Annual Letter

The derivatives letter. Buffett calls them "financial weapons of mass destruction" five years
before the crisis that proved the point, and explains the mechanism — mark-to-myth valuation,
collateral triggers that fire exactly when a company can least afford them, and daisy chains of
receivables that correlate under stress. Around it sits the year's other main subject: what went
wrong in corporate governance, why "independent" directors failed, and the four questions an audit
committee should ask. A banner year financially — book value +10.0% against the S&P's −22.1% —
reported with a caveat that Berkshire's *pro-forma* earnings were **lower** than the audited ones.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A banner year, discounted honestly

Net worth rose $6.1 billion, lifting per-share book value 10.0%. Over 38 years book value per
share went from $19 to $41,727, 22.2% compounded annually. The gain outstripped the S&P by 32.1
percentage points — a result Buffett calls aberrational, since he and Munger hope for an average
annual advantage of at most a few points, and expect the index to soundly trounce Berkshire in a
strong bull market now that the share of assets in common stocks has significantly declined.

The summary of why it was a banner year:

- Non-insurance operations performed exceptionally well in a sluggish economy. A decade earlier
  Berkshire's annual pre-tax earnings from non-insurance businesses were $272 million; that sum is
  now earned monthly.
- Insurance float rose $5.7 billion to $41.2 billion, at a cost of only 1%.
- New businesses were acquired with "economic characteristics ranging from good to great, run by
  managers ranging from great to great." Those are two legs of the entrance strategy; the third is
  a sensible purchase price.
- Marketable securities outperformed most indices — old stuff for Lou Simpson, a welcome change for
  Buffett after several dismal years.

Then the caveat, which is the letter's first attack on reporting practice. Pro-forma statements
invariably show earnings far above what auditors allow; the CEO in effect tells owners "don't count
this, don't count that – just count what makes earnings fat." Berkshire makes a little history by
going the other way: on a pro-forma basis its 2002 earnings were *lower* than reported, by about
$500 million pre-tax. Two favourable breaks caused it — no megacatastrophe occurred, and the bond
market favoured certain finance-division strategies that will diminish or disappear.

The reasoning on megacats generalises beyond insurance:

> "'Except for' losses will forever be part of the insurance business, and they will forever be
> paid with shareholders' money." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

He closes the point with Jack Benny on receiving an award: "I don't deserve this honor – but, then,
I have arthritis, and I don't deserve that either."

### Eddie Bennett, the managerial model

In 38 years no CEO of a subsidiary has left Berkshire to work elsewhere. Counting Munger, six
managers are over 75, and Buffett hopes that number rises: "It's hard to teach a new dog old
tricks."

The model he offers is Eddie Bennett, a batboy. In 1919, at 19, Eddie started with the Chicago
White Sox, who went to the World Series; the next year he switched to the Brooklyn Dodgers, who won
their league title; in 1921 he joined the Yankees, who won their first pennant in history and five
American League titles in the next seven years. In 1927 he received $700 for the one-eighth World
Series share voted him by the Ruth and Gehrig team — roughly a full year's pay for batboys working
with ordinary associates, earned in four days because New York swept.

> "It's simple – to be a winner, work with winners." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

Eddie understood that how he lugged bats was unimportant; what counted was hooking up with the
cream of those on the field. Buffett's own job is to stay out of his managers' way and allocate the
excess capital their businesses generate. See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

The related entrance/exit point is one of the most quoted lines about Berkshire's structural
advantage: unlike LBO operators and private equity firms, Berkshire has no exit strategy — it buys
to keep, which is why it is usually the first and sometimes the only choice for sellers and their
managers. See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

### Acquisitions

**Completed from 2001:** Albecca (operating as Larson-Juhl), the U.S. leader in custom picture
frames, under Steve McKenzie; and **Fruit of the Loom**, producer of about 33.3% of men's and boys'
underwear sold in the U.S., under John Holland — who had retired in 1996, rejoined three years ago
and rescued the company from the path it went down after he left.

**New, below the normal size threshold** but together earning more than $60 million pre-tax
annually, both in industries with tough economics but with competitive strengths that let them earn
decent returns on capital:

- **CTB** — worldwide leader in equipment for poultry, hog, egg production and grain industries,
  under Vic Mancinelli.
- **Garan** — children's apparel, best known for Garanimals, under Seymour Lichtenstein.

**The Pampered Chef**, the largest acquisition initiated in 2002, comes with the letter's best
founding story. In 1980 Doris Christopher was a 34-year-old suburban Chicago home economics teacher
with two small daughters and no business background. She borrowed $3,000 against her life insurance
policy — all the money ever injected into the company — bought a dozen each of this and that at the
Merchandise Mart, and set up in her basement. Driving to her first in-home presentation she almost
talked herself into turning back, convinced she would fail; the women there bought $175 of goods.
First-year business was $50,000. Twenty-two years later it exceeds $700 million annually through
67,000 kitchen consultants. Doris brought in Sheila O'Connell Cooper as CEO two years earlier, and
Buffett says it took him about ten seconds in the August meeting to decide he wished to partner
with them.

**Through MidAmerican Energy Holdings** (80.2% owned, but limited to 9.9% voting control by the
Public Utility Holding Company Act, so it cannot be fully consolidated). MEHC already has $18
billion of assets and delivers Berkshire's largest stream of non-insurance earnings.

- **Kern River**, a pipeline from southwest Wyoming to southern California moving about 900 million
  cubic feet of gas a day, undergoing a $1.2 billion expansion that will double throughput — enough
  gas to generate electricity for ten million homes.
- **Northern Natural Gas**, a 16,600-mile line from the Southwest into the Midwest, whose history
  Buffett tells as an Omaha story. One of Omaha's premier businesses from the 1930s, renamed
  InterNorth in 1980, it merged in July 1985 with Houston Natural Gas — a business less than half
  its size — on the announced basis that the enlarged operation would be headquartered in Omaha
  under InterNorth's CEO. Within a year both promises were broken: the Houston CEO took the top
  job, the headquarters moved to Houston, and the new name he chose was Enron. Fifteen years later
  Enron pledged the pipeline to Dynegy as collateral, ownership passed to Dynegy, and Dynegy's own
  troubles led to a Friday call on 26 July. A contract was signed on 29 July, and Northern Natural
  returned home.

On completion of the Kern River expansion MEHC will transport about 8% of all gas used in the U.S.
**HomeServices of America**, MEHC's residential brokerage arm, participated in $37 billion of
transactions, up 100% — now the second largest in the country, with Prudential California Realty
its largest 2002 acquisition. See [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

*(Yearend float, $ millions.)* The standard definition and caution are restated, with one addition

The 1% cost came *after* absorbing $1.75 billion of charges — about 4.6% of float — from a painful

The three conditions for low-cost float over time are stated as a test: underwrite with unwavering
discipline; reserve conservatively; and avoid an aggregation of exposures that would let a
supposedly impossible incident threaten solvency.

### General Re: "I was dead wrong"

The most direct retraction in the corpus. When Buffett agreed to merge with Gen Re in 1998 he
believed it stuck to the three rules — he had studied the operation for decades and detected no
slippage.

> "I was dead wrong." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

Gen Re's culture and practices had substantially changed, and unbeknownst to its management and to
him the company was grossly mispricing current business. It had also accumulated an aggregation of
risk that would have been fatal had terrorists detonated several large nuclear bombs in an attack
on the U.S. — improbable, but insurers must limit risk so their finances stay rock-solid if the
impossible happens. He states plainly that had Gen Re remained independent, the World Trade Center
attack alone would have threatened its existence.

By 2002 the nuclear, chemical and biological aggregation was reduced to a tolerable level. Joe
Brandon and Tad Montross judge themselves only by underwriting profitability — size simply does not
count. On reserving, an additional $1.31 billion was recorded to correct earlier estimation
mistakes, after the company had *attempted* to reserve adequately at yearend 2001 and failed badly:

> "I wish I didn't know now what I didn't know then." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The generalisable observation is about incentives, not competence: the natural tendency of most
casualty-insurance managers is to underreserve, and overcoming that devastating bias requires a
particular mindset which — Buffett notes it may surprise readers — has nothing to do with actuarial
expertise. A reinsurer faces far more difficulty reserving properly than a primary insurer does.
See [[Applications/RedFlags]].

One advantage arrived from outside: each of General Re's three largest worldwide competitors,
previously rated AAA, was demoted by at least one rating agency, leaving General Re alone among the
giants with AAA across the board. Buffett makes the case that this is the attribute that matters
most, citing one of the world's largest reinsurers — regularly recommended by leading brokers — that
has all but ceased paying valid and due claims, leaving hundreds of primary insurers facing massive
write-offs.

> "'Cheap' reinsurance is a fool's bargain" — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

**GEICO** had a year to pinch yourself over: substantial growth, outstanding profits, retention up,
sales productivity sharply higher. Tony Nicely has been in love with GEICO for 41 years, since
joining at 18. The company saves policyholders about $1 billion annually against what other
insurers would on average have charged; 19,162 associates received profit-sharing equal to 19% of
base salary; premium volume has gone from $2.9 billion when Berkshire acquired full ownership in
1996 to $6.9 billion, with Internet new business up 75%.

The footnote Buffett attaches to GEICO is the sharpest illustration of counterparty risk in the
letters. In 1981-83 GEICO's then-managers wrote commercial umbrella and product liability
insurance, taking in $3,051,000 and spending almost all of it — $2,979,000 — on reinsurance, keeping
$72,000 for the risk retained. Losses from that venture now total $94.1 million, about 130,000% of
the net premium received, of which $90.3 million is uncollectable receivables from deadbeat
reinsurers. See [[Cases/GEICO]].

**Ajit Jain's** division was the main reason float cost so little.

> "If we ever put a photo in a Berkshire annual report, it will be of Ajit. In color!"
> — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

His operation has amassed $13.4 billion of float from a standing start in 1986 with a workforce of
20, and has produced underwriting profits. His $534 million underwriting profit in 2002 came *after*
a $428 million charge attributable to retroactive insurance — the mechanism explained again: an
insurer pays a large upfront premium less than the losses Berkshire expects to pay, accepted
willingly because payments are capped and the money is usable meanwhile, often for a decade or
more. About 80% of the $6.6 billion of asbestos and environmental loss reserves arises from capped
contracts, so their costs cannot skyrocket. The deferred-charge asset stood at $3.4 billion, and
its amortization — $440 million in 2002 including Gen Re — creates an underwriting loss that is
intentional and desirable.

> "At Berkshire, we would rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%."
> — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

> "If you see Ajit at our annual meeting, bow deeply." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The smaller insurers grew float 38% with a $32 million underwriting profit — though Buffett flags
terrible results and severely missed reserving in the California workers' compensation operation,
which will be kept small until the business is figured out.

### Derivatives: the warning, and the mechanism

> "We view them as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system."
> — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

General Re Securities came with the Gen Re purchase, was judged dangerous, could not be sold, and
is being terminated — losing $173 million pre-tax in 2002, partly a belated acknowledgment of
faulty though standard accounting used earlier.

> "In fact, the reinsurance and derivatives businesses are similar: Like Hell, both are easy to
> enter and almost impossible to exit." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The analysis identifies five distinct hazards, and it is the reason this section belongs in
[[Applications/RedFlags]]:

1. **Earnings booked before money moves.** Counterparties record profits and losses — often huge —
   in current earnings statements without a penny changing hands, on contracts sometimes running 20
   or more years and tied to several variables.
2. **Mark-to-myth.** Traders are usually paid on earnings calculated by mark-to-market, but often
   there is no real market, so mark-to-model is used. Contracts with multiple reference items and
   distant settlement dates increase the scope for fanciful assumptions — both parties to a contract
   can use differing models and both show profits for years. Buffett notes the marking errors have
   not been symmetrical: almost invariably they favoured the trader eyeing a bonus or the CEO
   wanting impressive earnings, or both, with shareholders learning much later that the reported
   earnings were a sham. General Re Securities held 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties at
   yearend, after ten months of winding down — a portfolio expert auditors could honestly value
   very differently.
3. **The pile-on effect.** Many contracts require a company suffering a credit downgrade to supply
   collateral immediately. A company downgraded because of general adversity faces an unexpected
   and enormous demand for cash exactly when it can least meet it, which can trigger a liquidity
   crisis and further downgrades — a spiral leading to corporate meltdown.
4. **Daisy-chain risk.** Huge receivables from many counterparties build up over time — Gen Re
   Securities still had $6.5 billion after nearly a year of liquidation. A participant may believe
   his exposures are diversified and therefore safe, but an exogenous event that spoils the
   receivable from Company A will often affect those from B through Z. "History teaches us that a
   crisis often causes problems to correlate in a manner undreamed of in more tranquil times."
5. **No central bank.** The linkage problem was one reason for forming the Federal Reserve, which
   now insulates strong banks from weak ones. No such body exists for insurance or derivatives, so
   fundamentally solid firms can be troubled by others further down the chain. Where a chain
   reaction threat exists, it pays to minimise links of any kind — which is how Berkshire conducts
   reinsurance and one reason it is exiting derivatives.

Buffett concedes the micro case: participants unable to bear certain risks can transfer them to
stronger hands, and he himself sometimes engages in large-scale derivatives transactions to
facilitate investment strategies. It is the macro picture he considers dangerous and worsening —
credit risk concentrated in relatively few dealers who trade extensively with each other, owed huge
amounts by non-dealer counterparties linked in ways that could make them fail simultaneously. He
cites Long-Term Capital Management in 1998, whose trades — from a firm unknown to the public
employing a few hundred people — caused the Fed anxieties severe enough to orchestrate a hasty
rescue, and which was far from a worst-case scenario. Total-return swaps, one of LTCM's instruments,
make a joke of margin requirements by financing 100% leverage.

> "When Charlie and I finish reading the long footnotes detailing the derivatives activities of
> major banks, the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the
> institution is running." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

> "In our view, however, derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers
> that, while now latent, are potentially lethal." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

Little was done in equities. Buffett is increasingly comfortable with the major investees, most of
which increased earnings while their valuations decreased — but is not inclined to add, because he
does not yet believe the shares are undervalued. The same conclusion fits stocks generally: after
three years of falling prices, very few even mildly interest him, which is testimony to the insanity
of valuations reached in the Great Bubble.

> "Unfortunately, the hangover may prove to be proportional to the binge." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The aversion is explicitly not congenital — in 61 years of investing, 50 or so have offered the kind
of opportunity he loves, and such years will come again. The hurdle is stated numerically: unless
there is a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns, translating to 6½-7% after
corporate tax, Berkshire sits on the sidelines. With short-term money returning less than 1%
after-tax, that is no fun.

> "But occasionally successful investing requires inactivity." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

**Junk bonds and loans** sextupled to $8.3 billion. Buffett draws the distinction from equities
carefully: both require a price-value calculation and scanning hundreds of securities for the few
with attractive reward/risk ratios, but in stocks he expects *every* commitment to work out,
concentrating on conservatively financed businesses with strong competitive positions run by able
and honest people — over 38 years, gains from equities managed at Berkshire have exceeded losses by
about 100 to one. Junk issuers are far more marginal, usually overloaded with debt, often in
low-return industries, sometimes with questionable management whose interests run directly counter
to debtholders'. Occasional large losses are therefore expected. See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### Corporate governance: why "independent" directors failed

Buffett opens with Luke 16:2 — a rich man telling his manager "Give an account of thy stewardship;
for thou mayest no longer be steward" — and observes that accountability and stewardship withered in
the Great Bubble: as stock prices went up, the behavioural norms of managers went down, so by the
late '90s CEOs travelling the high road did not encounter heavy traffic. Most CEOs are people you
would happily have as trustees for your children's assets, but too many behaved badly at the
office, following the career path of Mae West: "I was Snow White but I drifted."

The diagnosis is social rather than legal. It has always been clear that directors must represent
shareholders; what defeats them is **boardroom atmosphere**. In a room of well-mannered people it is
almost impossible to raise whether the CEO should be replaced, awkward to question an acquisition
the CEO's staff and advisors unanimously support, and — when the compensation committee, armed with
a high-paid consultant, reports a megagrant of options — "like belching at the dinner table" to
suggest reconsideration. He endorses outside directors meeting regularly without the CEO, while
doubting most other new governance rules will deliver benefits commensurate with their costs.

His three requirements, from the 1993 report, are that directors be **independent-minded,
business-savvy, interested and shareholder-oriented**. Over 40 years he has sat on 19 public-company
boards and interacted with perhaps 250 directors; most were independent by today's definition, and
the great majority lacked at least one of the qualities that matter, so their contribution was
minimal at best and often negative. He includes himself:

> "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the
> interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence."
> — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

**The 62-year case study.** Since 1940 federal law has required a large proportion of investment
company directors to be independent — originally 40%, now 50%. Those boards have only two important
responsibilities: obtain the best possible investment manager, and negotiate the lowest possible
fee. Year after year Fund A's directors select manager A and Fund B's select manager B, in a
zombie-like process that makes a mockery of stewardship.

> "But for the most part, a monkey will type out a Shakespeare play before an 'independent'
> mutual-fund director will suggest that his fund look at other managers, even if the incumbent
> manager has persistently delivered substandard performance." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The hypocrisy shows when a management company is sold: the independent directors experience a
counter-revelation and decide the buyer is the best available, though the buyer was available and
ignored in earlier years — and could then have been hired far more cheaply, since it must now recoup
the acquisition cost through fees paid by the shareholders delivered in the deal. He recounts his
own daughter being offered a directorship worth about 50% of her annual income, and turning it down.

The remedy he proposes is not more rules but **big owners acting**: twenty or fewer of the largest
institutions, acting together, could reform governance at a company simply by withholding votes for
directors tolerating odious behaviour. He acknowledges that some large institutions have glass-house
problems, and names Jack Bogle, Chris Davis and Bill Miller as offering leadership.

> "(If you can't tell whose side someone is on, they are not on yours.)" — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

The acid test is CEO compensation: managers will cheerfully agree to board diversity, attest to SEC
filings and adopt meaningless process proposals, but will fight a hard look at their own pay. Where
Samuel Gompers described organized labour's goal in the 1890s as "More!", America's CEOs adopted the
same cry in the 1990s — and it is time for directors to shout "Less!" It would be a travesty if the
bloated pay of recent years became the baseline for future compensation.

**What Berkshire will do.** Rules requiring codified independence will change Berkshire's board, and
Buffett adds a test he considers important though not determinative: directors with huge and true
ownership interests — stock they or their family *purchased*, not received from Berkshire or via
options. He notes the overlooked inversion: directors' fees at public companies average perhaps
$50,000, and a director for whom that is 20% or more of annual income, hoping to be invited onto
more boards, is highly unlikely to offend a CEO — yet Ron Olson may be deemed not independent for
receiving a tiny percentage of a very large income from Berkshire legal fees. Berkshire pays its
directors only a pittance and provides no officers' and directors' liability insurance, so their
behaviour is driven by the effect of decisions on their family's net worth. New directors will be
sought from the shareholder list among those with large, long-held stakes. Buffett family members
stay on the board not to run the business after his death and without compensation of any kind, but
to ensure the culture is nurtured under later CEOs — and the board's most important job is likely to
be selecting his and Munger's successors. See [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and
[[Principles/Fidelity]].

### The audit committee: four questions

> "Audit committees can't audit." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

Only the outside auditor can determine whether purported earnings are suspect, so reforms focused on
committee structure and charter accomplish little. The committee's key job is to get auditors to
divulge what they know — which requires making them worry more about misleading the committee than
about offending management. Auditors have generally viewed the CEO rather than shareholders or
directors as their client, a natural result of working relationships and of knowing who pays their
fees and decides whether they are retained.

The four questions Buffett proposes, with answers recorded and reported to shareholders:

1. If the auditor were solely responsible for preparing the financial statements, would they have
   been prepared differently — material *and* nonmaterial differences? If so, both management's
   argument and the auditor's response should be disclosed.
2. If the auditor were an investor, would he have received, **in plain English**, the information
   essential to understanding the company's financial performance for the period?
3. Is the company following the same internal audit procedure the auditor would follow if he were
   CEO? If not, what are the differences and why?
4. Is the auditor aware of any actions, accounting or operational, that had the purpose and effect
   of moving revenues or expenses between reporting periods?

If those are asked, the committee's composition — the focus of most reforms — is of minor importance.
The questions should be asked at least a week before an earnings release, so differences can be
aired and resolved rather than rubber-stamped under deadline: "Haste is the enemy of accuracy." He
adds that the SEC's recent shortening of reporting deadlines will hurt the quality of information
shareholders receive and should be rescinded. The primary advantage is prophylactic — once auditors
must affirmatively endorse rather than merely acquiesce, they will resist misdoings early.

### Options expensing, and three tests for investors

The Chicago Tribune's four-part series on Arthur Andersen documented the erosion. Andersen's elite
Professional Standards Group took the position in 1992 that the cost of stock options should be
recorded as the expense it clearly was — and was reversed by the rainmaking partners who knew clients
wanted higher reported earnings. The FASB then voted 7-0 for expensing; auditing firms and an army
of CEOs stormed Washington, amplified by large political contributions usually made with the money
of the very owners about to be bamboozled, and the Senate voted 88-9 against, with several senators
calling for the FASB's demise. Arthur Levitt has described his reluctant bowing to that pressure as
the act of his chairmanship he most regrets. FASB retreated to an honour system declaring expensing
preferable but optional — and 498 of the S&P 500 adopted the method deemed less desirable.

Buffett quotes his own 1992 letter: the business elite risks losing its credibility on issues of
significance to society when it advocates the incredible on issues of significance to itself. That
loss has occurred, and CEOs will not regain trust through fatuous ads, meaningless policy statements
or structural changes of boards and committees.

> "Instead, CEOs must embrace stewardship as a way of life and treat their owners as partners, not
> patsies. It's time for CEOs to walk the walk." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

**Three suggestions for investors**, which belong directly in [[Applications/RedFlags]]:

1. **Beware weak accounting.** A company that still does not expense options, or whose pension
   assumptions are fanciful, is signalling. "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen."
   Trumpeting EBITDA is particularly pernicious: it implies depreciation is not truly an expense
   because it is non-cash, when in fact depreciation is a *particularly unattractive* expense
   because the cash goes out up front, before the asset has delivered any benefit. The analogy — a
   company paying all employees for ten years of service in advance, then calling years two through
   ten non-cash — makes the point unanswerable.
2. **Unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management.** If you cannot understand
   a footnote, it is usually because the CEO does not want you to. Enron's descriptions of certain
   transactions still baffle him.
3. **Be suspicious of earnings projections and growth expectations.** Businesses seldom operate in a
   tranquil, no-surprise environment. Buffett and Munger do not know what their businesses will earn
   next year or even next quarter, and become downright incredulous at CEOs who consistently reach
   declared targets.

> "Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the
> numbers." — [[Sources/2002ltr]]

### The operating businesses

Operating earnings were $6,010 million pre-tax and $3,903 million after taxes and minority interests
— against $453 million and *negative* $47 million in 2001. Capital gains of $603 million brought
total earnings to $6,613 million and $4,286 million. Purchase-accounting adjustments dropped sharply
because GAAP no longer requires goodwill amortization, which raises reported earnings without
touching economic earnings.

- **Shoes turned around.** Dexter, the subject of three years of admissions, earned $24 million
  across the shoe business under Frank Rooney and Jim Issler — a $70 million upward swing from 2001.
  Randy Watson at Justin increased margins while trimming invested capital.
- **Home and construction-related businesses** — Acme Brick, Benjamin Moore, Johns Manville, MiTek
  and Shaw — delivered $941 million pre-tax, with Shaw rising from $292 million to $424 million on
  productivity gains and expense control while carpet prices rose only 1%. The cost-consciousness
  parable: a widow told an obituary cost 25 cents a word asks for "Fred Brown died", is told there
  is a seven-word minimum, and replies "make it 'Fred Brown died, golf clubs for sale'."
- **Retailing** did well in a so-so year, best at Homemaker's in Des Moines under the Merschman
  family. [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] opens a 450,000 square foot Kansas City store in August,
  potentially the second largest furniture store in the country by volume — behind Omaha.
- **Flight services** rose only because of a $60 million pre-tax gain on selling the 50% interest in
  FlightSafety Boeing. **NetJets** held 75% industry share by FAA records, flying 132.7 million
  nautical miles to 130 countries — and lost money again, a small U.S. profit more than offset by
  European losses, with the industry as a whole losing significant sums. Buffett makes the customer
  case: some large companies could save over $10 million a year while *increasing* operational
  capability, since a fraction of one plane allows several planes aloft simultaneously and an
  interchange arrangement gives access to 12 other models. General Electric, with a large fleet of
  its own and unsurpassed knowledge of using aircraft economically, is NetJets' largest customer.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Albecca (Larson-Juhl)** and **Fruit of the Loom** — completed from 2001 agreements.
- **CTB** — poultry, hog, egg and grain equipment; **Garan** — children's apparel. Both below the
  normal size threshold, together earning more than $60 million pre-tax.
- **The Pampered Chef** — the largest acquisition initiated in 2002; $700 million of annual sales
  through 67,000 consultants, from $3,000 borrowed against a life insurance policy in 1980.
- Through **MidAmerican**: **Kern River** and **Northern Natural Gas** pipelines, plus three
  residential brokerage acquisitions including **Prudential California Realty**. Berkshire funded
  these by buying $1,273 million of MidAmerican junior debt (total holdings of the 11% obligations
  now $1,728 million) and investing $402 million in a common-equivalent stock, taking its
  fully-diluted equity to 80.2%.

**Exiting**

- **General Re Securities.** Judged dangerous at the time of the Gen Re purchase, unsellable, now
  being terminated — 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties still outstanding at yearend after ten
  months of wind-down, and $6.5 billion of receivables. It lost $173 million pre-tax in 2002.

**Portfolio**

- Little done in equities; no additions to major holdings, which are not considered undervalued
  despite three years of falling prices.
- **Junk bonds and loans sextupled to $8.3 billion** — the year's main capital deployment, taken
  with explicit acknowledgment that occasional large losses are expected in that field.
- The stated hurdle for equities: a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax, or Berkshire
  sits it out.

**Insurance**

- $1.31 billion added at General Re to correct prior-year reserving; nuclear, chemical and
  biological aggregation reduced to a tolerable level.
- Ajit Jain's operation produced a $534 million underwriting profit after a $428 million retroactive
  charge.
- California workers' compensation kept deliberately small after severely missed reserving.
