# 1977 Annual Letter Summary

*The first letter in the collection. Operating earnings of $21.9 million, 19% on
beginning equity — and Buffett opens by attacking the metric most companies lead
with. It also contains the four-point test for buying a stock, the clearest early
statement that insurance has no moat of its own, and the argument that not
controlling a business can be an advantage.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Record earnings per share is the wrong scoreboard

The letter's opening move is to discard the number every other annual report
celebrates, and the reason is compounding arithmetic rather than modesty:

- "Most companies define "record" earnings as a new high in earnings per share.
  Since businesses customarily add from year to year to their equity base, we find
  nothing particularly noteworthy in a management performance combining, say, a 10%
  increase in equity capital and a 5% increase in earnings per share. After all,
  even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest
  earnings each year because of compounding."
- The replacement, with its exceptions named: "Except for special cases (for
  example, companies with unusual debt-equity ratios or those with important assets
  carried at unrealistic balance sheet values), we believe a more appropriate
  measure of managerial economic performance to be return on equity capital."

Then he applies it against his own headline. Operating earnings per share rose
37%, which sounds excellent — but beginning capital rose 24%, "making the gain in
earnings per share considerably less impressive than it might appear at first
glance." Return on beginning equity was 19%.

The savings-account analogy returns eight years later as the central argument
against fixed-price stock options in [[Sources/1985ltr]]. See
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Principles/Fidelity]].

### Insurance has no moat — only managers

The most quoted passage of the letter is a flat denial that the industry has any
structural protection, and it is worth reading before any later claim about
Berkshire's insurance advantage:

- "Insurance companies offer standardized policies which can be copied by anyone.
  Their only products are promises. It is not difficult to be licensed, and rates
  are an open book. There are no important advantages from trademarks, patents,
  location, corporate longevity, raw material sources, etc., and very little
  consumer differentiation to produce insulation from competition."

What follows is not the usual boast about people, because Buffett first concedes
that the usual boast is often empty: "It is commonplace, in corporate annual
reports, to stress the difference that people make. Sometimes this is true and
sometimes it isn't. But there is no question that the nature of the insurance
business magnifies the effect which individual managers have on company
performance."

This is the premise the 1982 letter turns into a general theory of commodity
economics — over-capacity plus an undifferentiated product, escapable only through
a wide and sustainable cost advantage. See [[Concepts/Moat]] and
[[Sources/1982ltr]].

### The four-point test

The standard for buying a fraction of a business is the standard for buying all of
it, stated here for the first time:

- "We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we would
  evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety. We want the business to be
  (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3)
  operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive
  price."

Two consequences follow that most investors get backwards. First, falling prices
are welcome: "if their business experience continues to satisfy us, we welcome
lower market prices of stocks we own as an opportunity to acquire even more of a
good thing at a better price." Second, the reason fractions are available cheaply
at all: "pro-rata portions of truly outstanding businesses sometimes sell in the
securities markets at very large discounts from the prices they would command in
negotiated transactions involving entire companies. Consequently, bargains in
business ownership, which simply are not available directly through corporate
acquisition, can be obtained indirectly through stock ownership."

And what he is buying is business performance, not a trade — positions taken "not
with any intention of taking control and not foreseeing sell-out or merger, but
with the expectation that excellent business results by corporations will translate
over the long term into correspondingly excellent market value and dividend results
for owners, minority as well as majority."

See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]] and [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

### Reported earnings already understate what is owned

Capital Cities cost $10.9 million in 1977. Berkshire's share of its earnings was
about $1.3 million; the cash dividend of $40,000 a year was all that reached the
income statement. The gap between the two is the seed of the look-through earnings
argument developed in [[Sources/1980ltr]] and [[Sources/1981ltr]].

The same disclaimer is applied to unrealized gains, which were about $74 million at
year end: "this figure, like any other figure of a single date (we had an
unrealized loss of $17 million at the end of 1974), should not be taken too
seriously. Most of our large stock positions are going to be held for many years
and the scorecard on our investment decisions will be provided by business results
over that period, and not by prices on any given day."

The generalisation is the one that governs the whole method: "Just as it would be
foolish to focus unduly on short-term prospects when acquiring an entire company,
we think it equally unsound to become mesmerized by prospective near term earnings
or recent trends in earnings when purchasing small pieces of a company; i.e.,
marketable common stocks."

See [[Concepts/OwnerEarnings]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### Not controlling a business can beat controlling it

On Capital Cities, Buffett makes an argument he flags as unorthodox and states
without hedging:

- "To purchase, directly, properties such as Capital Cities owns would cost in the
  area of twice our cost of purchase via the stock market, and direct ownership
  would offer no important advantages to us. While control would give us the
  opportunity - and the responsibility - to manage operations and corporate
  resources, we would not be able to provide management in either of those respects
  equal to that now in place. In effect, we can obtain a better management result
  through non-control than control. This is an unorthodox view, but one we believe
  to be sound."

Two things are conceded at once: that half the price buys the same economics, and
that Berkshire could not run these businesses as well as the people already running
them. The 1981 letter reaches the same conclusion from the opposite direction —
mistakes get corrected faster where there is no control. See [[People/TomMurphy]]
and [[Sources/1981ltr]].

### Textiles: reasons for staying that are not economic

Buffett does not defend the textile business on returns. He reports that
shareholders have questioned staying in an industry "unlikely to produce returns on
capital comparable to those available in many other businesses", then gives three
reasons that are mostly about obligation: the mills in New Bedford and Manchester
are among the largest employers in each town, with "a labor force of high average
age possessing relatively non-transferable skills"; the workers and unions have
cooperated unusually; and Ken Chace's management "generated capital from the
textile division needed to finance the acquisition and expansion of our profitable
insurance operation."

Only the third reason looks forward, and it is hedged: "it seems reasonable that at
least modest profits in the textile division can be achieved in the future."

He also declines to blame the industry alone: "This may say something about our
forecasting abilities, the nature of the textile industry, or both." And: "Many
difficulties experienced in the marketing area are due primarily to industry
conditions, but some of the problems have been of our own making."

The business is finally closed in [[Sources/1985ltr]], where Buffett writes that he
should be faulted for not quitting sooner.

### Good weather, and saying so while it lasts

The 1977 underwriting result was excellent, and Buffett spends most of the section
explaining why it will not last. Rate increases pushed through in 1976 to repair
the 1974–75 disaster only reached earnings in 1977, because policies are annual,
with "pricing mistakes capable of correction only upon renewal".

- "In 1977 the winds in insurance underwriting were squarely behind us."
- The turn, already visible: "The pendulum now is beginning to swing the other
  way." Costs were estimated to be rising close to 1% per month, from monetary
  inflation and from "social inflation", a broadening by society and juries of what
  a policy covers. Unless rates matched that pace, margins had to shrink.
- The commitment made in advance, which is the part that matters: "As markets loosen
  and rates become inadequate, we again will face the challenge of philosophically
  accepting reduced volume. Unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it
  runs counter to normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away
  business - even at foolish prices."

That promise is tested and kept through the terrible underwriting years of
1982–84. See [[Concepts/Float]].

### Mistakes listed by name

The insurance decade is reported with its failures itemised rather than summarised
— a surety operation begun in 1969, the 1973 expansion of Home and Automobile's
urban auto marketing into Miami, an unresolved aviation fronting arrangement, and
the California Worker's Compensation operation.

The conclusion is a statement about which businesses forgive error, and it is the
letter's most portable idea:

- "It is comforting to be in a business where some mistakes can be made and yet a
  quite satisfactory overall performance can be achieved. In a sense, this is the
  opposite case from our textile business where even very good management probably
  can average only modest results. One of the lessons your management has learned -
  and, unfortunately, sometimes re-learned - is the importance of being in
  businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds."

See [[Applications/RedFlags]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### The equity portfolio — $106.9 million at cost, $181.1 million at market

Nine positions above $5 million of market value. The largest:

| Company | Cost | Market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GEICO convertible preferred (1,986,953 sh) | $19.4m | $33.0m |
| The Washington Post Class B (934,300 sh) | $10.6m | $33.4m |
| The Interpublic Group (592,650 sh) | $4.5m | $17.2m |
| Capital Cities Communications (220,000 sh) | $10.9m | $13.2m |
| GEICO common (1,294,308 sh) | $4.1m | $10.5m |

Also Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical, Kaiser Industries, Knight-Ridder and Ogilvy &
Mather. The two GEICO lines together — $23.5 million of cost against $43.5 million
of market — make it the largest holding in the collection's first letter. See
[[Cases/GEICO]].

### Capital Cities — $10.9 million, and a stated preference for not controlling

Bought during 1977. Earnings attributable to the shares were about $1.3 million;
only the $40,000 dividend appeared in operating earnings. Buffett calls the
properties and the management both extraordinary and says direct ownership of
comparable properties would cost roughly twice as much. Berkshire buys three
million shares of the same company at $172.50 in [[Sources/1985ltr]].

### Cypress Insurance — bought for cash at year end

A Worker's Compensation insurer in South Pasadena, with about $12.5 million of
1977 volume that did not enter the results. Kept separate from National Indemnity's
existing California operation rather than merged. Milt Thornton, president since
1968, stays — and is still being named for producing underwriting profits in
[[Sources/1982ltr]].

### Kansas Fire and Casualty — started late in 1977

The fifth homestate company, under Floyd Taylor. The homestate group wrote $23
million of net premium against $5.5 million three years earlier, with all four
companies that operated through the year below a 100 combined ratio — Cornhusker
Casualty best at 93.8.

### Insurance underwriting overall — $151 million of premium volume

Against $22 million in 1967, when National Indemnity and National Fire and Marine
were bought for about $8.6 million. Buffett attaches the fact that makes the growth
meaningful: "No additional shares of Berkshire Hathaway stock have been issued to
achieve any of this growth."

Reinsurance, under George Young, ran a combined ratio of 107.1 — unsatisfactory,
but trending downward through the year, and generating unusually high investment
funds per dollar of premium.

### Illinois National Bank

Earnings of $3.6 million, at about three times the return on assets of most large
banks, while paying savers maximum rates and holding low-risk, highly liquid
assets. Gene Abegg founded it in 1931 with $250,000 and earned $8,782 in its first
full year; no capital has been added since, and $20 million of dividends have been
paid out since Berkshire's purchase in 1969. At 80 he asked for a successor, and
Peter Jeffrey joined as President and CEO from 1 March.

### Blue Chip Stamps — raised to about 36.5%

Blue Chip earned about $12.9 million from operations plus $4.1 million of realized
securities gains. Inside it, See's — bought at the start of 1972 — had taken
pre-tax operating earnings "from $4.2 million to $12.6 million with little
additional capital investment", and did so "while operating in an industry
experiencing practically no unit growth." That sentence is the earliest evidence in
the corpus for the economic-goodwill argument. See [[Cases/SeeCandies]] and
[[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]].
