# Berkshire Hathaway Letter to Shareholders 1977

## Highlights
- Operating earnings of $21,904,000 ($22.54 per share) were moderately better than anticipated
- Return on equity capital was 19%, slightly better than the previous year
- Insurance operations performed exceptionally well, exceeding optimistic expectations
- Textile operations came in well below forecast

## Key Topics Discussed

### Operating Performance
Warren Buffett noted that operating earnings in 1977 were moderately better than anticipated, with $21,904,000 total or $22.54 per share. Of these earnings, $1.43 per share resulted from substantial realized capital gains by Blue Chip Stamps, which were included in the operating earnings figure due to Berkshire's proportional interest.

### Textile Operations
The textile business had another poor year in 1977. Despite strenuous efforts, problems in marketing and manufacturing persisted. Buffett acknowledged that shareholders had questioned the wisdom of remaining in the textile business, which was unlikely to produce returns on capital comparable to other businesses. He outlined three reasons for maintaining operations:
1. Employment considerations for workers with non-transferable skills
2. Recognition of management efforts that generated capital for insurance operations
3. Potential for modest profits with continued hard work

### Insurance Underwriting
The insurance operation continued significant growth in 1977. Premium volume reached $151 million compared to $22 million in 1967 when Berkshire entered the industry. The almost 600% increase was achieved through:
- Large gains in National Indemnity's traditional liability areas
- Starting new companies (Cornhusker Casualty, Lakeland Fire and Casualty, Texas United Insurance, The Insurance Company of Iowa, Kansas Fire and Casualty)
- Purchasing other insurance companies (Home and Automobile Insurance Company, Central Fire and Casualty Company, Cypress Insurance Company)
- Marketing additional products, especially reinsurance

Phil Liesche's team achieved extraordinary underwriting results, with large volume gains accompanied by excellent underwriting margins.

### Insurance Investments
Insurance investments at cost grew from $134.6 million to $252.8 million over two years. Net investment income improved from $8.4 million pre-tax in 1975 to $12.3 million pre-tax in 1977. Capital gains of $6.9 million were realized, with about one-quarter from bonds and the balance from stocks.

Key equity holdings as of December 31, 1977:
- Capital Cities Communications, Inc.: $13,228,000 market value
- Government Employees Insurance Company: $43,549,000 market value (preferred and common)
- The Washington Post Company Class B: $33,401,000 market value
- The Interpublic Group of Companies, Inc.: $17,187,000 market value

### Investment Philosophy
Buffett outlined the approach to selecting marketable equity securities, similar to evaluating a business for acquisition:
1. Understandable business
2. Favorable long-term prospects
3. Operated by honest and competent people
4. Available at a very attractive price

He emphasized that Berkshire was willing to take large positions in selected companies expecting that excellent business results would translate into correspondingly excellent market value and dividend results over the long term.

### Banking Operations
The Illinois National Bank continued to achieve a rate of earnings on assets about three times that of most large banks. The bank earned $3.6 million in 1977, more than many banks two or three times its size. Gene Abegg, founder of the bank in 1931, remained healthy as Chairman while Peter Jeffrey joined as President and CEO.

### Blue Chip Stamps
Berkshire increased its equity interest in Blue Chip Stamps to approximately 36.5% by year-end. Blue Chip earned approximately $12.9 million from operations and had realized securities gains of $4.1 million. Both Wesco Financial Corp and See's Candies made good progress, with See's pre-tax operating earnings growing from $4.2 million to $12.6 million since 1972 with little additional capital investment.

## Notable Quotes
> "Most companies define 'record' earnings as a new high in earnings per share. Since businesses customarily add from year to year to their equity base, we find nothing particularly noteworthy in a management performance combining, say, a 10% increase in equity capital and a 5% increase in earnings per share."

> "We believe a more appropriate measure of managerial economic performance to be return on equity capital."

> "One of the lessons your management has learned - and, unfortunately, sometimes re-learned - is the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds."
