# 1960 Partnership Letter Summary

The fourth full year, and the first with a four-year track record to show: through a
down market (the Dow lost 6.3% including dividends) the partnerships gained 22.8%,
compounding to 140.6% against the Dow's 42.6% since 1957. The letter is best
remembered for its second half — the [[Cases/SanbornMap]] story, one of the great
value-investing case studies, told here in full by the man who ran it.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### The scorecard, and the golf analogy

Buffett restates the only reason the partnership should exist — beating the Dow over
time — and the shape that outperformance will take (won in flat and down years, given
back in strong ones). He frames it with a golf image that prefigures the later
"strokes on each hole" candor of the [[Sources/OwnersManual]]:

> "I would consider a year in which we declined 15% and the Average 30% to be much superior to a year when both we and the Average advanced 20%." [[Sources/1960ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

The four-year table — 1957 +10.4% / 1958 +40.9% / 1959 +25.9% / 1960 +22.8%, against
Dow figures of −8.4%, +38.5%, +19.9%, −6.3% — compounds to 140.6% versus 42.6%. He
warns four years is "entirely too short" to conclude anything.

### Sanborn Map — the map business thrown in for nothing

The 35% position is revealed as Sanborn Map Co., a publisher of extremely detailed
insurance maps that had run as a near-monopoly for seventy-five years before "carding"
gutted its profits. The insight is a valuation gap between the map business and an
investment portfolio Sanborn had accumulated since the 1930s:

> "In the tremendously more vigorous climate of 1958 the same map business was evaluated at a minus $20 with the buyer of the stock unwilling to pay more than 70 cents on the dollar for the investment portfolio with the map business thrown in for nothing." [[Sources/1960ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

The stock had fallen from $110 (1938) to $45 (1958) even as the portfolio alone grew
from about $20 to $65 a share. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

### The activism, and why boards fail

Buffett's account is also a governance case: fourteen directors, nine of them
insurance men holding 46 shares between them, presided over a business whose dividend
was cut five times in eight years while fees were untouched — the misalignment that
[[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] later make central.
He bought the resigning family's block, added to about 24,000 shares (46,000 with
allies), and rather than a proxy fight engineered a plan — SEC-approved — under which
about 72% of the stock was exchanged for portfolio securities at fair value,
separating the two businesses and eliminating a large corporate capital-gains tax.

### Control is the exception, not the business

He is careful to frame Sanborn as unusual:

> "Our bread-and-butter business is buying undervalued securities and selling when the undervaluation is corrected along with investment in special situations where the profit is dependent on corporate rather than market action." [[Sources/1960ltr]]

Housekeeping: he floats combining the several partnerships into one (done in 1962),
and explains accepting advance payments at 6% interest.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

- **Sanborn Map — concluded.** Accumulated to about 24,000 shares (46,000 with
  associates), then separated the investment portfolio from the map business via a
  fair-value exchange taking out ~72% of the stock; the map company kept a bond
  reserve and the remaining holders got higher earnings per share and dividend. See
  [[Cases/SanbornMap]].
- **Results:** +22.8% for the seven full-year partnerships against the Dow's −6.3%,
  the sharpest relative year yet and exactly the down-market outperformance predicted.
- **Structure:** advance payments accepted at 6% interest; partnership consolidation
  proposed.
