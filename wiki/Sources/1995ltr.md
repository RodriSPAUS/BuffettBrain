# 1995 Annual Letter Summary

Net worth grew $5.3 billion (45.0%) in 1995, per-share book value 43.1% — a year Buffett is careful not to take credit for ("any fool could make a bundle in the stock market. And we did."). Three acquisitions of exactly the type Berkshire wants were completed: Helzberg's Diamond Shops, R.C. Willey Home Furnishings, and full ownership of GEICO. The letter carries an unusually long personal-history section (the 1951 Lorimer Davidson meeting, GEICO sold too early, a 1966 Disney position sold even earlier), a confession-laden review of the five 1987-91 convertible preferreds, and the proposal to create Class B stock to block "clone" unit trusts from being sold against Berkshire's name.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Acquisitions: why buyers should distrust sellers' projections

Berkshire made three acquisitions "of exactly the type we desire" while Buffett reiterates that "most deals do damage to the shareholders of the acquiring company":

> "Too often, the words from HMS Pinafore apply: "Things are seldom what they seem, skim milk masquerades as cream." Specifically, sellers and their representatives invariably present financial projections having more entertainment value than educational value." [[Sources/1995ltr]]

He illustrates the seller's-timing problem with a vet joke: a horse owner complains, "Sometimes my horse walks just fine and sometimes he limps." The vet's advice is to sell him on a good day. Berkshire's own advantages, he says, include having no strategic plan forcing acquisitions into an "ordained direction," and stock that lets sellers defer taxes indefinitely while keeping day-to-day autonomy. [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]]

### Helzberg's Diamond Shops and R.C. Willey: both found "by walking around"

Both 1995 deals began with a chance encounter — Barnett Helzberg stopping Buffett on a New York street corner, and Irv Blumkin (Nebraska Furniture Mart) vouching for R.C. Willey's Bill Child. Helzberg's grew from a single 1915 Kansas City store to $282 million in 1994 sales across 134 stores; R.C. Willey grew from $250,000 in 1954 sales under Bill Child to $257 million in 1995, over half of Utah's furniture market. Buffett is explicit that management, not the asset, was the purchase:

> "We would not have bought the business if Jeff had not been there to run it. Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

He distinguishes retailing — a "have-to-be-smart-every-day business," where a weak successor can sink decades of work — from a "have-to-be-smart-once business" like a network TV station, which survives even a "shiftless and backward nephew" at the helm. [[Sources/1995ltr]]

### GEICO: the full personal history, and completing a 45-year position

The letter carries an extended retelling of the 1951 origin story: a Columbia student, taught by Ben Graham, discovers Graham chairs GEICO, takes a Saturday train to its closed Washington headquarters, and gets a four-hour private tutorial from Assistant to the President Lorimer "Davy" Davidson on GEICO's direct-marketing cost advantage. Buffett bought 350 shares in 1951 (funded partly by his Washington Post paper route) — "more than 65% of my net worth" — then sold the entire position in 1952 to chase a cheaper stock, a decision the 1951 shares would have turned into roughly $1.3 million within 20 years. [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

> "Checking back, I find that I purchased GEICO shares on four occasions during 1951... This pattern of persistence suggests to me that my tendency toward self-intoxication was developed early." [[Sources/1995ltr]]

Berkshire began buying GEICO again in 1976 after Jack Byrne's turnaround, held roughly 33-50% for fifteen years without adding to the position (the stake grew via GEICO's own buybacks), then paid $2.3 billion in 1995 for the remaining half — "a steep price," but one that secures a business Buffett says remains exceptional for the same reasons it was in 1951, run by Tony Nicely (insurance) and Lou Simpson (investments, averaging 22.8% annually 1980-95 versus 15.7% for the S&P).

> "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable "moats." Thanks to Tony and his management team, GEICO's moat widened in 1995." [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Concepts/Moat]]

The section closes by publicly thanking Davidson, then 93, for the 1951 afternoon that Buffett says Berkshire "would not be where it is today" without.

### Insurance Operations: float as substitute equity, and Ajit Jain's single-risk deals

Berkshire's cost-of-float table extends to 1995: average float of $3,607.2 million against an underwriting profit, again "less than zero" cost — float that has grown at a 20.7% compounded annual rate since 1967. Buffett works through the mechanics of why cheap float functions as equity without diluting shareholders, and previews that the GEICO purchase alone adds nearly $3 billion of float. [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Concepts/Float]]

Three straight abnormally profitable super-cat years draw the same caution as 1993 and 1994. Ajit Jain's versatility beyond catastrophe reinsurance is illustrated with three 1995 single-risk deals: insuring Mike Tyson's life (declining fight-by-fight), insuring Lloyd's against more than 225 of its "names" dying in a year, and insuring the launch and first year of orbit of two Chinese satellites — all three paid off for Berkshire. [[Sources/1995ltr]] Buffett names the mistake-tuition still being paid from Berkshire's own early-1970s reinsurance underpricing:

> "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." [[Sources/1995ltr]]

An apocryphal story about a gloomy insurance CEO — reserves inadequate, reinsurers gone broke — who brightens at the thought that "it could be my money" is Buffett's summary of why alignment matters: "At Berkshire, it's our money." [[Sources/1995ltr]] Other units posted strong combined ratios: National Indemnity (Don Wurster) at 84.2, homestate (Rod Eldred) at 81.4, and Central States Indemnity (John Kizer) with premium volume up 23% and underwriting profit up 59%.

### Sources of Reported Earnings, and three managers reporting bad news honestly

The 1995 segment table (Berkshire's after-tax share, in millions) shows Insurance Underwriting $11.3, Insurance Net Investment Income $417.7, Buffalo News $27.3, Fechheimer $8.8, Home Furnishings $16.7 (R.C. Willey from June 29), Jewelry $19.1 (Helzberg's from April 30), Kirby $32.1, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing $21.2, See's Candies $29.8, Shoe Group $37.5, World Book $7.0, for Total Earnings — All Entities of $725.2 million versus $494.8 million in 1994.

Buffett cites Munger's rule — "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of itself" [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]] — to explain earnings declines at three units: the shoe group (industry-wide cyclical weakness, not a competitive loss), the Buffalo News (newspapers losing "another notch" of the franchise strength first flagged in 1991), and World Book, squeezed by CD-ROM and online competition and responding with distribution changes and cost cuts.

### Common Stock Investments: Disney bought, sold, and bought again thirty years later

The over-$600-million common stock table at 12/31/95 (cost/market, in millions): American Express $1,392.7/$2,046.3 (increased to ~10% ownership), Capital Cities/ABC $345.0/$2,467.5, Coca-Cola $1,298.9/$7,425.0, Freddie Mac $260.1/$1,044.0, GEICO $45.7/$2,393.2, Gillette $600.0/$2,502.0, Wells Fargo $423.7/$1,466.9, plus $2,655.4 million of smaller "Others," for total common stocks of $5,745.1 million cost / $22,000.3 million market.

Early 1996 brought two portfolio-changing events: the GEICO purchase closed, and Cap Cities/ABC shares were exchanged for cash plus Disney stock in the Disney-Cap Cities merger. Buffett tells the story against himself: he first bought Disney in 1966 at a split-adjusted 31 cents a share, when the company's market value implied "only five times rides" against the newly-opened Pirates of the Caribbean — then sold out in 1967 at 48 cents.

> "At Disneyland, the $17 million Pirates of the Caribbean ride would soon open. Imagine my excitement - a company selling at only five times rides!" [[Sources/1995ltr]]

### Convertible Preferred Stocks: Gillette, and the mistake never shown on a financial statement

Reviewing the five 1987-91 convertible preferreds (Champion International, First Empire State, Gillette, Salomon, USAir), Buffett concedes his 1989 promise that the group would "moderately" beat medium-term fixed income and "under almost any conditions... return us our money plus dividends" was only kept because of one holding:

> "That's one I would like to have back. Winston Churchill once said that "eating my words has never given me indigestion." My assertion, however, that it was almost impossible for us to lose money on our preferreds has caused me some well-deserved heartburn." [[Sources/1995ltr]]

The best performer, Gillette, is also where Buffett identifies his "biggest mistake — of a kind, however, never recognized on financial statements": taking $600 million of convertible preferred instead of negotiating directly for common stock at a similar discount, which the company called in under two years. He estimates that negotiating for common instead would have left Berkshire better off at yearend 1995 by $625 million, minus about $70 million of "excess" dividends the preferred paid in the interim. [[Sources/1995ltr]] Salomon remained a disappointment relative to the fixed-income alternative it replaced — "I had "no special insights regarding the direction or future profitability of investment banking." Even the most charitable commentator would conclude that I have since proved my point." USAir's dividend, unpaid since June 1994, was compounding at 5% over prime; Buffett rates his own USAir forecasting record too poor to guess its outcome. [[Sources/1995ltr]]

### A Proposed Recapitalization: Class B stock to block the "clones"

Buffett proposes creating Class B common stock (1/30th the economic rights of Class A, 1/200th the vote, no charitable-designation rights, convertible one-way from A to B) explicitly to undercut "expense-laden unit trusts purporting to be low-priced 'clones' of Berkshire" that brokers were preparing to market to small investors at high commission. [[Sources/1995ltr]] He states plainly that Berkshire's market price had run ahead of its intrinsic value: "Berkshire is selling at a price at which Charlie and I would not consider buying it." [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] The B shares are framed not as a way to court new capital but as a defensive, shareholder-protecting structure — a "do-it-yourself" split for anyone who wants one, offered specifically to make the clone trusts "unmerchandisable."

### Miscellaneous: how the Disney-Cap Cities deal actually happened

Buffett recounts a chance meeting on Sun Valley's Wildflower Lane on July 14, 1995 — he ran into Disney CEO Michael Eisner en route to golf with Cap Cities/ABC's Tom Murphy, and a stalled merger (blocked for years only by a cash-versus-stock disagreement) was under contract within days. He pays tribute to Murphy at length ("as fine an executive as I have ever seen") and admits selling a third of Berkshire's Cap Cities stake two years earlier for $635 million — stock worth $1.27 billion once folded into the Disney deal. Contributions participation was 96.3% of eligible shares, distributing $11.6 million to 3,600 charities.

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### GEICO — the remaining 50% for $2.3 billion

The largest transaction of the year, closing immediately after yearend: full ownership of the seventh-largest U.S. auto insurer (~3.7 million cars insured), ending a position Buffett first opened in 1951 and rebuilt from 1976.

### Helzberg's Diamond Shops and R.C. Willey Home Furnishings

Both paid for substantially or entirely in Berkshire stock via tax-free exchange; both retailers kept their existing owner-managers (Jeff Comment, Bill Child) running day-to-day operations with full autonomy.

### The convertible-preferred unwind

Champion converted and sold back to the company; Gillette converted in 1991 and was later called; First Empire due to be called and converted to common on March 31, 1996; Salomon and USAir held as disappointing fixed-income positions.

### Marketable securities, largely left alone

Five of six top positions from yearend 1994 were untouched through 1995; American Express was increased to roughly 10% ownership. Cap Cities/ABC's holding was converted into cash and Disney stock in early 1996.
