# Berkshire Hathaway 1995 Annual Letter

## Summary

Warren Buffett's 1995 letter to shareholders highlights a remarkable year with a $5.3 billion gain in net worth (45.0%). The letter details three significant acquisitions: Helzberg's Diamond Shops, R.C. Willey Home Furnishings, and GEICO, which collectively demonstrate Berkshire's acquisition strategy of seeking businesses with excellent economic characteristics run by outstanding managers.

## Key Highlights

### Financial Performance
- Gain in net worth: $5.3 billion (45.0%)
- Per-share book value growth: 43.1%
- Over 31 years, per-share book value grew from $19 to $14,426 (23.6% annually)

### Major Acquisitions

#### Helzberg's Diamond Shops
- Founded in 1915, grew to 134 stores in 23 states by 1995
- Sales grew from $10 million (1974) to $282 million (1994)
- Acquisition completed via tax-free exchange of stock [[Helzberg's]]

#### R.C. Willey Home Furnishings
- Built from $250,000 in sales (1954) to $257 million (1995)
- Accounts for over 50% of furniture business in Utah
- Led by CEO Bill Child [[R.C.-Willey]]

#### GEICO Corporation
- Seventh largest auto insurer in the US with ~3.7 million cars insured
- Buffett's association with GEICO began in 1951 when he met Lorimer Davidson
- Purchased for $2.3 billion to acquire remaining 50% stake [[GEICO]]

### Insurance Operations
- Float grew to $3.6 billion with negative cost (earning money on float)
- Exceptional underwriting performance with continued profits
- Super-cat business showed large profits due to lack of major catastrophes [[Insurance-Operations]]
- Ajit Jain manages the super-cat business with exceptional skill [[Ajit-Jain]]

### Investment Philosophy
- Emphasis on businesses with wide economic moats [[Moat]]
- Preference for businesses with excellent economics and outstanding management
- Willingness to accept volatile results for better long-term earnings
- Focus on owner earnings and intrinsic value rather than accounting metrics [[Owner-Earnings]] [[Intrinsic-Value]]

### Management Approach
- Minimal headquarters staff (12 people for vast operations)
- Autonomy given to subsidiary managers
- No second-guessing by corporate leadership
- Culture of treating managers as owners [[Management-Quality]]

## Notable Quotes

> "There's no reason to do handsprings over 1995's gains. This was a year in which any fool could make a bundle in the stock market. And we did. To paraphrase President Kennedy, a rising tide lifts all yachts."

> "We believe most deals do damage to the shareholders of the acquiring company. Too often, the words from HMS Pinafore apply: 'Things are seldom what they seem, skim milk masquerades as cream.'"

> "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.' Thanks to Tony and his management team, GEICO's moat widened in 1995." [[Moat]]

## Investment Holdings

Major common stock positions at December 31, 1995:
- Coca-Cola: $7.4 billion market value
- American Express: $2.0 billion market value
- Gillette: $2.5 billion market value
- Freddie Mac: $1.0 billion market value
- GEICO: $2.4 billion market value (later converted to wholly-owned subsidiary)

## Corporate Governance

### Proposed Recapitalization
- Creation of two classes of stock (Class A and Class B)
- Each Class B share with rights of 1/30th of Class A share
- Class B shares intended to deter creation of expense-laden unit trusts
- Maintains Berkshire's shareholder-friendly culture [[Corporate-Governance]]
