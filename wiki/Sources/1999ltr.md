# 1999 Annual Letter

The worst year of Buffett's tenure, and the letter opens by saying so and assigning himself the
blame in the first two paragraphs. Book value per share rose 0.5%. Underneath the bad number are
three sections that outlast it: the definitive statement of when a company should repurchase its
own shares, the clearest explanation of why Berkshire owns no technology stocks, and a proposal
for acquisition accounting that anticipates the rule the FASB would eventually adopt.

## 🔑 Key Themes

### A self-assigned D

Net worth rose $358 million, lifting per-share book value 0.5%. Over 35 years book value per
share went from $19 to $37,987, 24.0% compounded annually. Buffett calls it the worst absolute
performance of his tenure and, against the S&P, the worst relative performance as well — and it
is relative results that concern him, since bad relative numbers eventually produce
unsatisfactory absolute ones.

The blame is placed without hedging. He invokes the quarterback whose report card showed four Fs
and a D, and whose coach drawled:

> "I think you're spending too much time on that one subject." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

> "capital allocation, and my grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D" — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

What hurt was the equity portfolio, and responsibility for it — Lou Simpson's small piece aside —
is entirely his. Several large investees badly lagged the market because of disappointing
operating results. He still likes the businesses and is content to hold them, but notes it is no
sure thing they quickly regain their stride.

He also separates the stock from the business in both directions: in 1998 the stock outperformed
the business; in 1999 the business did much better than the stock, a divergence continuing to the
date of the letter.

> "Over time, of course, the performance of the stock must roughly match the performance of the
> business." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

The forward claim is deliberately modest — intrinsic value gain over the next decade should
*modestly* exceed the S&P — and he explains why large superiorities are gone: businesses and
stocks were once available at far more attractive prices, and a much smaller capital base allowed
a much wider range of opportunities. Well over 99% of his net worth remains in Berkshire and
neither he nor his wife has ever sold a share. He also states the expectation, "in our minds, the
virtual certainty", that the S&P will do far less well over the next decade or two than it has
since 1982, enclosing a *Fortune* article setting out why.

### Guides to intrinsic value, with a negative column

| 1979 | $577 | $13.07 |
| 1989 | $7,200 | $108.86 |
| 1999 | $47,339 | $(458.55) |

Per-share investments barely changed while operating earnings fell apart — the second column
turns negative, and the decade growth rate for it is reported as N.A. Most operating managers
earned an A; the damage came from a huge and, Buffett believes, aberrational underwriting loss at
General Re, plus the predicted fall in GEICO's underwriting profit.

He flags one structural item that permanently widens the gap between intrinsic and book value:
the annual charge for amortization of goodwill, now running about $500 million, which reduces
reported book value while having nothing to do with economic goodwill. Book value stood at $57.8
billion, and he says intrinsic value far exceeds it — See's and the Buffalo News are worth fifteen
to twenty times their carrying value. See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] and
[[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]].

### A managerial story you will never read elsewhere

The Bill Child episode is the best single illustration of [[Principles/Fidelity]] in the corpus.
R. C. Willey, bought from the Child family in 1995, has never opened on Sunday because Bill and
most of his managers are Mormons — a hard way to trade, since Sunday is many customers' favourite
shopping day. Bill built the business from $250,000 of annual sales in 1954, when he took over,
to $342 million in 1999 while keeping the policy.

When Bill proposed a Boise store in 1997, Buffett was highly sceptical about taking a no-Sunday
policy into new territory against rivals open seven days — but told him to follow both his
business judgment and his religious convictions, because it was Bill's business to run.

Bill then insisted on terms Buffett had never seen: he would personally buy the land and build
the store, about $9 million as it turned out, and sell it to Berkshire at cost if it succeeded; if
sales fell short, Berkshire could walk away without paying a cent, leaving Bill with a huge
investment in an empty building. Buffett objected that if Berkshire took the upside it should take
the downside. Bill refused — if there was to be failure because of his religious beliefs, he
wanted to take the blow personally.

The store opened in August and was immediately a huge success. Bill handed over the property,
including extra land that had appreciated significantly, took a cheque for his cost — and refused
a dime of interest on two years of tied-up capital.

> "You can understand why the opportunity to partner with people like Bill Child causes me to tap
> dance to work every morning." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

The footnote is the joke Buffett tells against himself, at the Boise grand opening after
announcing the store was by a considerable margin the largest home furnishings store in Idaho:

> "By the end of my talk, it all had come back to me: Opening a store in Boise had been my idea."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

The framing he gives the whole section: Berkshire's managers are largely independently wealthy,
work under no contracts, face no show-and-tell presentations in Omaha, no budgets approved by
headquarters and no dictums about capital expenditures — they are asked only to run their
companies as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next
century. See [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] and [[People/HenrySingleton]] for the contrast in
decentralisation styles.

### Float grew; its cost went positive for the first time

The three determinants are restated — amount of float, its cost, and above all the long-term
outlook for both — along with the standard definition and the reserving caution. He sharpens the
caution for the year: in 1999 a number of insurers announced reserve adjustments that, in his
words, made a mockery of the earnings investors had relied on when making buy and sell decisions.

*(Yearend float, $ millions.)* Growth matters but cost is vital, and 1999 broke the pattern: a
$1.4 billion underwriting loss left a float cost of 5.8%, against a cumulative underwriting
profit through 1998. Buffett splits the loss honestly — $400 million of it was enthusiastically
welcomed because it stems from business that will deliver exceptional float over the next decade;
the balance was decidedly unwelcome and the overall result "extremely poor". Absent a
mega-catastrophe he expects float cost to fall in 2000, tempered by aggressive plans at GEICO.
See [[Concepts/Float]] and [[Synthesis/FloatGrowth]].

**Ajit Jain** gets the most concentrated praise in the corpus. His reinsurance operation, built
from scratch, has earned an underwriting profit across his tenure and holds $6.3 billion of float.

> "It's simply impossible to overstate Ajit's value to Berkshire" — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

> "In Ajit, we have an underwriter equipped with the intelligence to properly rate most risks;
> the realism to forget about those he can't evaluate; the courage to write huge policies when
> the premium is appropriate; and the discipline to reject even the smallest risk when the
> premium is inadequate." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

It is rare to find one of these talents; having all four is remarkable. Buffett adds the honest
qualifier that a super-cat book is bound to be far more volatile than most insurance, and that
Berkshire has so far benefited from good luck on it.

The "other primary" group delivered a $192 million underwriting profit over five years — Rod
Eldred, Brad Kinstler, John Kizer, Don Towle and Don Wurster are thanked by name. At General Re,
business was extremely underpriced both domestically and internationally, a condition improving
but not corrected; incentive compensation at General Re and Cologne is now tied directly to float
growth and cost of float, the same variables that determine owner value. Buffett expects another
unsatisfactory underwriting year while rate increases work through, and says General Re has the
distribution, underwriting skills, culture and — with Berkshire's backing — the financial clout to
become the world's most profitable reinsurer.

### GEICO: spending optional money on purpose

| 1993 | 346,882 | 2,011,055 |
| 1994 | 384,217 | 2,147,549 |
| 1995 | 443,539 | 2,310,037 |
| 1996 | 592,300 | 2,543,699 |
| 1997 | 868,430 | 2,949,439 |
| 1998 | 1,249,875 | 3,562,644 |
| 1999 | 1,648,095 | 4,328,900 |

*(Voluntary only. The new-policy column is footnoted as revised to exclude policies moved between
GEICO companies, which is why it differs slightly from the figures in [[Sources/1997ltr]] — and
why it corrects the spurious leading digit carried in the 1998 letter's version of the same
table.)*

Marketing went from $33 million and 652 telephone counselors in 1995 to $242 million and 2,631 in
1999, with $300-$350 million planned for 2000 — and Buffett says he would happily commit $1 billion
annually if the business could be handled smoothly and the last dollar still produced new business
at an attractive cost.

The economics behind that willingness are set out plainly. Acquisition costs are rising: media
rates are up and inquiries per ad fall for everyone as the whole industry advertises more. Offsetting
it, the closure ratio has steadily improved, cost of new business remains well below the industry's,
and operating costs on renewal business are the lowest among broad-based national auto insurers.
Both advantages are called sustainable — others may copy the model but cannot replicate the
economics. Buffett estimates the policy count could probably be held by spending as little as $50
million a year, which makes almost all the rest an optional outlay chosen to build the brand.

> "Personally, I think these expenditures are the best investment Berkshire can make."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

The reason is the annuity on the other side: GEICO is acquiring direct relationships with
households that will on average send $1,100 year after year. As the customer base grew by 766,256
in 1999, the company gained $590 million of cash from operating earnings and the increase in
float — growth that generates cash rather than consuming it. Market share went from 2.7% to 4.1%
in three years.

Compensation continues to use two variables only — percentage growth in policyholders and earnings
of the seasoned book — with new-business costs deliberately excluded so associates are never
penalised for writing initially unprofitable policies that Berkshire wants. 1999 produced a
profit-sharing payment of 28.4% of salary, $113.3 million in total.

He also warns clearly about the industry: margins fell in 1999 as predicted and should fall again
in 2000, perhaps by around three percentage points, as loss costs rise, rate increases arrive
slowly through regulatory delay and contract terms, and the reservoir of redundant reserves that
has been flattering earnings largely dries up. See [[Cases/GEICO]] and [[Applications/RedFlags]].

**Lorimer Davidson** died in November, a few days after his 97th birthday. Buffett calls him a
business giant who moved GEICO up to the big leagues, and for himself a friend, teacher and hero
whose absence would have made his life develop far differently.

### Aviation services: two leaders with opposite balance sheets

FlightSafety and Executive Jet are both runaway leaders — EJA larger than its next two competitors
combined, FSI about five times its nearest — and both still run by their founding entrepreneurs, Al
Ueltschi, who started FSI in 1951 with $10,000, and Rich Santulli, who invented fractional
ownership in 1986.

The instructive part is that leadership in the same sector can rest on opposite economics. FSI is
capital-hungry: a single flight simulator can cost as much as $15 million, Berkshire has 222 of
them, and only one person can train in a simulator at a time — so capital investment per dollar of
revenue is exceptionally high and operating margins must be high to earn a reasonable return.
Capital expenditure was $215 million in 1999 across FSI and its 50%-owned FlightSafety Boeing
affiliate. At EJA the customer owns the aircraft, so modest pre-tax margins still produce good
returns on equity — though a core fleet is needed for peak days like the Sunday after
Thanksgiving, when fractions of 169 planes are owned by 1,412 customers who mostly want to fly
home between 3 and 6 p.m.

Customers own planes worth over $2 billion with $4.2 billion more on order; EJA is taking delivery
of about 8% of all business jets manufactured in the world and would take more if it could, since
availability of planes is the binding constraint. Recurring revenues rose 46%. Scale is the moat —
"Buy a fraction, get a fleet" — reinforced by the fact that the two largest competitors are
subsidiaries of aircraft manufacturers restricted to their parents' planes, while EJA offers
aircraft from five suppliers. See [[Concepts/Moat]].

### Acquisitions: Jordan's Furniture and MidAmerican Energy

Buffett contrasts Berkshire's sourcing with the auction process, and the satire is worth keeping:
the investment banker's book presents a formerly mild-mannered company emerging from the phone
booth able to leap competitors in a single bound, with earnings projected precisely for years
ahead by an author who would go into a protective crouch if asked what his own firm will earn next
month. The Scott Fetzer story lands it — a major banking house failed to sell the company in 1985,
Buffett wrote to Ralph Schey and had a deal within a week, and the bank still collected a $2.5
million fee, offering in return a copy of its book:

> "I'll pay $2.5 million not to read it." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

> "At Berkshire, our carefully-crafted acquisition strategy is simply to wait for the phone to
> ring." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

**Jordan's Furniture** is the fourth link in the referral chain that began with
[[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] in 1983 and ran through R. C. Willey (1995) and Star Furniture
(1997). Asked repeatedly whether there were any more out there, the Blumkins, Bill Child and
Melvyn Wolff invariably named the Tatelman brothers. Barry and Eliot Tatelman's family has run the
business since 1927, when their grandfather started in a Boston suburb; it is now the largest
furniture retailer in both Massachusetts and New Hampshire, presents customers with an
entertainment experience the brothers call "shoppertainment", and has the highest sales per square
foot of any major furniture operation in the country. On selling, the Tatelmans gave each employee
at least 50¢ for every hour ever worked for Jordan's — $9 million, from their own pockets, not
Berkshire's.

Berkshire's furniture group is now number one in Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Texas, Nebraska,
Utah and Idaho. Star's Melvyn Wolff and Shirley Toomim moved into San Antonio and significantly
enlarged the Austin store.

**MidAmerican Energy** came through Walter Scott, Jr., chairman of Level 3 Communications and a
Berkshire director, who asked casually at a California conference whether Berkshire might invest.
Two short meetings with Scott and CEO David Sokol followed. Because the Public Utility Holding
Company Act of 1935 complicates utility acquisitions, the transaction was structured to avoid
Berkshire gaining voting control: an 11% fixed-income security plus common stock and exchangeable
preferred giving just under 10% of the voting power but about 76% of the equity interest, roughly
$2 billion in total. Scott and his family will bring their own investment to about $280 million
and be the controlling shareholder. Buffett notes further utility commitments are possible and
could be large — the seed of [[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### Acquisition accounting: the proposal that later became the rule

With the FASB proposing to end pooling, Buffett stakes out a position that agrees with both sides
and rejects both remedies.

He agrees with managements that goodwill amortization charges are usually spurious. Most
accounting charges describe something real even when they cannot measure it precisely —
depreciation tracks assets that invariably deteriorate, and obsolescence, bad-debt and warranty
charges reflect true costs. Economic goodwill often does the opposite:

> "In character, economic goodwill is much like land: The value of both assets is sure to
> fluctuate, but the direction in which value is going to go is in no way ordained."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

At See's, economic goodwill has grown irregularly but very substantially for 78 years.

But he rejects pooling as an escape, calling it the poetic notion that when two rivers merge their
streams become indistinguishable:

> "If you think otherwise, just ask employees severed from their jobs which company was the
> conqueror and which was the conquered." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

In most mergers a purchase has been made, so the FASB is right. His proposal: record the purchase
price at fair value whatever the currency, leave the resulting goodwill asset on the books without
amortization, and write it down only if it becomes impaired — applied retroactively for
consistency. His prediction if adopted is that managements would then choose cash or stock on the
real consequences for shareholders rather than the unreal consequences for reported earnings.

A practical corollary: Jordan's was bought as *assets* for cash, which permits amortizing the
goodwill over 15 years on the tax return and materially increases the cash the business delivers.
Buying stock for cash gets no such deduction, and stock-for-stock is the worst structure of all
from the acquirer's standpoint — a high price paid with no step-up in tax basis, so a later sale
can trigger a large capital gains tax at 35% or more even on a real economic loss. Berkshire has
accepted far-from-optimal structures when sellers insisted and the deal still made sense, but
"We have never done an inefficiently-structured deal, however, in order to make our figures look
better."

### Reported earnings, and Huggins' Law

Pre-tax operating earnings fell to $1,085 million from $1,899 million; after taxes and minority
interests, $671 million against $1,277 million. Reinsurance underwriting alone was $(1,440)
million pre-tax. Capital gains of $1,365 million brought total earnings to $2,450 million pre-tax
and $1,557 million after — down from $4,314 million and $2,830 million.

Almost all manufacturing, retailing and service businesses had excellent results. The exception was
Dexter Shoe, and Buffett is explicit that it was not a managerial failure: about 93% of the 1.3
billion pairs of shoes bought in the U.S. in 1999 came from abroad where extremely low-cost labour
is the rule. Berkshire, with H. H. Brown, is the leading domestic manufacturer and wants to keep
every U.S. job it can, while sourcing more output internationally to stay viable — incurring
significant severance and relocation costs in the reported figures. Helzberg's 200 stores have
recovered margins under Jeff Comment.

See's earned a record 24% operating margin. Bought for $25 million in 1972, it has since earned
$857 million pre-tax while requiring very little additional capital — the compact case for
[[Cases/SeeCandies]] and [[Concepts/EconomicGoodwill]]. Chuck Huggins took charge on the day of
purchase at 46, when See's pre-tax profit expressed in millions was about 10% of his age; at 74 the
ratio has reached 100%. Buffett names this Huggins' Law and says he and Munger now become giddy at
the mere thought of Chuck's birthday.

Few changes were made. The tech abstention is argued not as disdain but as a limit — Buffett shares
the general view that society will be transformed by tech products and services:

> "Our problem — which we can't solve by studying up — is that we have no insights into which
> participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

He puts it alongside patents, manufacturing processes and geological prospects, fields where
Berkshire brings nothing to the table and therefore makes no judgments.

> "If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of
> competence and when we are approaching the perimeter." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

Predicting the long-term economics of fast-changing industries is far beyond that perimeter, and
where others claim predictive skill and are apparently validated by the market, he neither envies
nor emulates them:

> "Instead, we just stick with what we understand." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

Written at the top of the dot-com market, this is the corpus' strongest statement of
[[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]].

On prices, he separates the businesses from their stocks:

> "In other words, we feel much better about the businesses than their stocks."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

> "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable
> business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

What really gets their attention is a comfortable business at a comfortable price. The market
arithmetic he offers is explicit rather than a forecast: corporate profits track GDP, GDP grows at
a real rate of about 3%, plus a hypothesised 2% inflation — a figure he takes from the market
itself, since TIPS yield about two percentage points less than standard Treasuries. Profits growing
about 5% mean the valuation placed on American business is unlikely to climb much more, so returns
will be dramatically less than investors have experienced or expect.

> "If investor expectations become more realistic — and they almost certainly will — the market
> adjustment is apt to be severe, particularly in sectors in which speculation has been
> concentrated." — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

He closes with two song lines: on when Berkshire will get to deploy major cash, "Who knows where
or when?"; and on anyone explaining the manic portions of the market, "Fools give you reasons,
wise men never try."

### Share repurchases: the definitive statement

Prompted by shareholder requests, some rationally based and a few leaning on spurious logic. The
rule has exactly two conditions, plus a caveat:

1. The company has funds — cash plus sensible borrowing capacity — beyond the near-term needs of the
   business.
2. Its stock sells below intrinsic value, conservatively calculated.
3. *Caveat:* shareholders must have been supplied everything they need to estimate that value,
   or insiders are buying out uninformed partners at a fraction of true worth.

Business needs are of two kinds: expenditures required to maintain competitive position, such as
remodeling stores at Helzberg's, and optional growth outlays expected to produce more than a dollar
of value per dollar spent, such as R. C. Willey's expansion into Idaho.

In the mid-1970s the wisdom of buying back was virtually screaming at managements and few
responded; Berkshire actively searched for large repurchasers, since it was a tipoff to both
undervaluation and shareholder-oriented management. That day is past:

> "Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around."
> — [[Sources/1999ltr]]

Repurchases are now all the rage and too often made for the ignoble unstated reason of pumping or
supporting the price. A seller today benefits from any buyer whatever his motive; the *continuing*
shareholder is penalised by repurchases above intrinsic value. He also dismantles the
offset-the-options rationale: buying back to cover shares issued on option exercise is a buy high,
sell low strategy that investors only ever employ unintentionally, and the two decisions should
each stand on their own feet.

He admits his own error — at times he did not repurchase when he should have, being too
conservative on value or too enthused about alternative uses — while noting the gain would have
been minimal given Berkshire's light trading volume, and giving the arithmetic: buying 2% of a
company's shares at a 25% discount to intrinsic value produces at most a ½% gain in that value.

The operational detail is unusually concrete: with A shares below $45,000 repurchases were
considered but deferred until shareholders could read the report; any buying would rarely be bid on
the NYSE but done in response to offers made directly, with B shares favoured at more than a 2%
discount to the A, and no transactions below 10 A shares or 50 B. And the promise: purchases will
never be made with the intention of stemming a decline in the price. See
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

**Acquired**

- **Jordan's Furniture** — the fourth furniture business, sourced from the referral chain running
  back to the Blumkins in 1983. Bought as *assets* for cash, deliberately: it permits amortizing
  goodwill over 15 years on the tax return and materially raises the cash the business delivers.
  Largest furniture retailer in Massachusetts and New Hampshire; highest sales per square foot of
  any major furniture operation in the country.
- **MidAmerican Energy** — about $2 billion, contracted in 1999. Structured around the Public
  Utility Holding Company Act of 1935: an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable
  preferred giving just under 10% of voting power but about 76% of the equity interest. Walter
  Scott, Jr. remains controlling shareholder with about $280 million of his family's money in.

Both were paid for in cash, with no Berkshire shares issued — the method Buffett says he and
Munger vastly prefer, and the follow-through on 1998's confession.

**Portfolio**

- Few changes. No additions to existing holdings, because the prices of the fine businesses
  already owned are simply not attractive.
- No scaling back either: a comfortable business at a questionable price is still preferred to a
  questionable business at a comfortable price.
- **No technology holdings**, stated as a competence limit rather than a market view.

**Considered and declined**

- **Share repurchases.** With A shares below $45,000 Berkshire considered buying, then chose to
  wait until shareholders had read the report. The conditions, mechanics and price discipline are
  all set out in advance rather than announced after the fact.

**Insurance**

- A $1.4 billion underwriting loss took the cost of float to 5.8% — the first materially positive
  cost in the series. $400 million of it was accepted willingly for business expected to deliver
  exceptional float over the next decade.
- Rates being raised at General Re; another unsatisfactory underwriting year expected while
  increases work through.
- General Re and Cologne incentive compensation restructured onto float growth and cost of float.
- GEICO marketing to rise to $300-$350 million in 2000, an explicitly optional outlay.
