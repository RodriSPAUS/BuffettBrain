# 1992 Annual Letter Summary

*Covering 1992: book value up 20.3%. The letter's centrepiece is the demolition
of the value-versus-growth distinction and the clearest statement in the
collection of how Buffett actually decides what a business is worth.*

## 🔑 Key Themes

### Value and growth are not two approaches

The passage most often cited from this letter, and the one that resolves an
argument the industry still has.

- "In answering this question, most analysts feel they must choose between two
  approaches customarily thought to be in opposition: "value" and "growth." Indeed,
  many investment professionals see any mixing of the two terms as a form of
  intellectual cross-dressing."
- The verdict: "We view that as fuzzy thinking (in which, it must be confessed, I
  myself engaged some years ago). In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at
  the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a
  variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact
  can be negative as well as positive."
- And the term itself: "we think the very term "value investing" is redundant. What
  is "investing" if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to
  justify the amount paid?"
- With the alternative named: "Consciously paying more for a stock than its
  calculated value - in the hope that it can soon be sold for a still-higher price -
  should be labeled speculation (which is neither illegal, immoral nor - in our view
  - financially fattening)."

### Why the usual value screens do not work

- "Typically, it connotes the purchase of stocks having attributes such as a low
  ratio of price to book value, a low price-earnings ratio, or a high dividend
  yield."
- Why that fails: "Unfortunately, such characteristics, even if they appear in
  combination, are far from determinative as to whether an investor is indeed buying
  something for what it is worth"
- And the inverse: "opposite characteristics - a high ratio of price to book value,
  a high price-earnings ratio, and a low dividend yield - are in no way inconsistent
  with a "value" purchase."

See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]] — this is a warning against screening
by ratio rather than by economics.

### Growth is not automatically good

- "Similarly, business growth, per se, tells us little about value. It's true that
  growth often has a positive impact on value, sometimes one of spectacular
  proportions. But such an effect is far from certain."
- The example: "investors have regularly poured money into the domestic airline
  business to finance profitless (or worse) growth. For these investors, it would
  have been far better if Orville had failed to get off the ground at Kitty Hawk:
  The more the industry has grown, the worse the disaster for owners."
- The condition that decides it: "Growth benefits investors only when the business
  in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing - in other words,
  only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of
  long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental
  funds, growth hurts the investor."

See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]].

### The valuation equation, stated plainly

- Attributed to its source: "In The Theory of Investment Value, written over 50
  years ago, John Burr Williams set forth the equation for value, which we condense
  here: The value of any stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash
  inflows and outflows - discounted at an appropriate interest rate - that can be
  expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset."
- The one formula covers everything: "Note that the formula is the same for stocks
  as for bonds."
- Where the difficulty actually lies: "A bond has a coupon and maturity date that
  define future cash flows; but in the case of equities, the investment analyst must
  himself estimate the future "coupons.""
- And why management enters the calculation at all: "the ability of management can
  dramatically affect the equity "coupons.""
- The decision rule, indifferent to style: "The investment shown by the
  discounted-flows-of-cash calculation to be the cheapest is the one that the
  investor should purchase - irrespective of whether the business grows or doesn't,
  displays volatility or smoothness in its earnings, or carries a high price or low
  in relation to its current earnings and book value."
- Including across asset classes: "When bonds are calculated to be the more
  attractive investment, they should be bought."

See [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] — this is the definitive statement.

### Best business, worst business

- "Leaving the question of price aside, the best business to own is one that over an
  extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates
  of return. The worst business to own is one that must, or will, do the opposite -
  that is, consistently employ ever-greater amounts of capital at very low rates of
  return."
- And the catch that shapes all of Berkshire: "Unfortunately, the first type of
  business is very hard to find: Most high-return businesses need relatively little
  capital."
- With the consequence for those businesses: "Shareholders of such a business
  usually will benefit if it pays out most of its earnings in dividends or makes
  significant stock repurchases."

This is the See's-versus-BHE tension stated as a general rule, fifteen years before
either page of this wiki existed. See [[Cases/SeeCandies]] and
[[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].

### The two defences against a wrong estimate

- First: "we try to stick to businesses we believe we understand. That means they
  must be relatively simple and stable in character. If a business is complex or
  subject to constant change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows."
- The framing of competence that follows: "What counts for most people in investing
  is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they
  don't know."
- "An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big
  mistakes."
- Second: "we insist on a margin of safety in our purchase price. If we calculate
  the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not
  interested in buying."
- With attribution: "We believe this margin-of-safety principle, so strongly
  emphasized by Ben Graham, to be the cornerstone of investment success."

See [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]].

### The 1977 criteria, restated with one change

- "Our equity-investing strategy remains little changed from what it was fifteen
  years ago, when we said in the 1977 annual report: "We select our marketable
  equity securities in much the way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in
  its entirety. We want the business to be one (a) that we can understand; (b) with
  favorable long-term prospects; (c) operated by honest and competent people; and
  (d) available at a very attractive price.""
- The single amendment, and its cause: "We have seen cause to make only one change
  in this creed: Because of both market conditions and our size, we now substitute
  "an attractive price" for "a very attractive price.""

Fifteen years, one word changed. See [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]].

## 📊 Investment Decisions

### General Dynamics — an arbitrage that became an investment

The clearest example in the letters of changing your mind mid-position.

- The original intent was short-term: "We've made the same sort of commitment
  perhaps a half-dozen times in the last few years, reaping decent rates of return
  for the short periods our money has been tied up."
- What changed it: "But then I began studying the company and the accomplishments of
  Bill Anders in the brief time he'd been CEO. And what I saw made my eyes pop: Bill
  had a clearly articulated and rational strategy; he had been focused and imbued
  with a sense of urgency in carrying it out; and the results were truly
  remarkable."
- The switch: "In short order, I dumped my arbitrage thoughts and decided that
  Berkshire should become a long-term investor with Bill."
- And the mechanical advantage of the situation: "We were helped in gaining a large
  position by the fact that a tender greatly swells the volume of trading in a
  stock. In a one-month period, we were able to purchase 14% of the General Dynamics
  shares that remained outstanding after the tender was completed."

Note what triggered the change: a capital-allocation record, not a cheaper price.
See [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

### The Salomon interlude closes

- "Last June, I stepped down as Interim Chairman of Salomon Inc after ten months in
  the job. You can tell from Berkshire's 1991-92 results that the company didn't
  miss me while I was gone."
- The outcome: "In Fortune's annual survey of America's Most Admired Corporations,
  conducted last September, Salomon ranked second among 311 companies in the degree
  to which it improved its reputation."
- With credit assigned by name: "It is no exaggeration to say that without the
  combined efforts of Salomon executives Deryck Maughan, Bob Denham, Don Howard, and
  John Macfarlane, the firm very probably would not" have survived.

### Convertible debentures redeemed

- "$25 million were converted into shares before yearend; $46 million were converted
  in January; and $405 million were redeemed for cash."
- Share count, and the point of quoting it: "Berkshire now has 1,152,547 shares
  outstanding. That compares, you will be interested to know, to 1,137,778 shares
  outstanding on October 1, 1964"

Twenty-eight years, a 1.3% increase in shares. The denominator matters as much as
the numerator — see [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]].
