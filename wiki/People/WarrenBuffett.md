## The scoreboard he insisted on from letter one

Buffett's first letter as Chairman opens not with a boast but with an attack on
the metric every other annual report leads with:

> "Most companies define "record" earnings as a new high in earnings per share.
> Since businesses customarily add from year to year to their equity base, we find
> nothing particularly noteworthy in a management performance combining, say, a 10%
> increase in equity capital and a 5% increase in earnings per share." [[Sources/1977ltr]]

> "We believe a more appropriate measure of managerial economic performance to be
> return on equity capital." [[Sources/1977ltr]]

He then applies the harsher yardstick to his own year: operating earnings per
share rose 37%, but beginning capital rose 24% — "making the gain in earnings
per share considerably less impressive than it might appear at first glance."
The same letter states the four-part test still used to evaluate a purchase
decades later:

> "We want the business to be (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable
> long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4)
> available at a very attractive price." [[Sources/1977ltr]] [[Applications/BusinessQualityChecklist]]

## As he appears in his own letters: an inventory of error

The letters' most distinctive feature as a corporate document is how much space
is given to admitted mistakes, in specific dollar terms, decade after decade.

**1980** — on shutting down Waumbec Mills years later than he should have:

> "Your Chairman made a costly mistake in not facing the realities of this
> situation sooner." [[Sources/1980ltr]]

**1985** — on the eventual closure of the entire textile operation:

> "But in the end nothing worked and I should be faulted for not quitting
> sooner." [[Sources/1985ltr]]

**1989** — the cigar-butt confession, the hinge admission of the whole
collection:

> "Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "For Berkshire's shareholders, myself included, the cost of this thumb-sucking
> has been huge." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

**1991** — the first "Mistake Du Jour," a $1.4 billion mistake of omission on
Fannie Mae:

> "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation for my amateurish
> behavior vis-a-vis Fannie Mae. But there isn't one. What I can give you is an
> estimate as of yearend 1991 of the approximate gain that Berkshire didn't make
> because of your Chairman's mistake: about $1.4 billion." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

**1994** — two mistakes in one letter, a needless sale and a leveraged one:

> "Late in 1993 I sold 10 million shares of Cap Cities at $63; at year-end 1994,
> the price was $85.25... Now I've become a repeat offender." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

> "Whatever the outcome, we will heed a prime rule of investing: You don't have to
> make it back the way that you lost it." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

**2007** — the practice generalised into a standing promise:

> "But I'll make more mistakes in the future – you can bet on that."
> [[Sources/2007ltr]]

**May 2020** — the same candour, live and unedited, at the first virtual
meeting. Having bought roughly 10% of the four major U.S. airlines and then sold
the entire stake at a loss during the COVID shutdown, Buffett refuses to spread
the blame:

> "And I'm the one who made the decision." [[Sources/Conference_call_2020]] [[Concepts/CircleOfCompetence]]

He is careful to separate the error from the four CEOs, framing it as a
low-probability event that materialized rather than a management failure — the
same "mistake of assessing future economics" category the 2024 letter would
later formalize.

**2024** — formalised into a named section, with the two categories the rest of
this wiki is organised around:

> "Mistakes – Yes, We Make Them at Berkshire Sometimes I've made mistakes in
> assessing the future economics of a business I've purchased for Berkshire – each
> a case of capital allocation gone wrong." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

> "At other times, I've made mistakes when assessing the abilities or fidelity of
> the managers Berkshire is hiring." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

Those two categories are [[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] and
[[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

## The rule about criticism

> "When discussing problems at specific subsidiaries, we do, however, try to
> follow the advice Tom Murphy gave to me 60 years ago: "praise by name, criticize
> by category."" [[Sources/2024ltr]] [[People/TomMurphy]]

## Personal history, pulled forward into investment decisions

Buffett repeatedly traces a current holding back to a personal association
decades old, rather than to a screen or a model. His first stock, at 20, was
GEICO — bought on the strength of a Saturday afternoon spent being taught the
insurance business by Lorimer Davidson, then sold too early:

> "Checking back, I find that I purchased GEICO shares on four occasions during
> 1951... This pattern of persistence suggests to me that my tendency toward
> self-intoxication was developed early." [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[Cases/GEICO]]

He funded part of that first GEICO stake delivering the Washington Post — a
company Berkshire would later buy a large stake in for about $10 million — and
his first business, aged six, was reselling Coca-Cola six-packs bottle by
bottle around the neighborhood, decades before Berkshire's billion-dollar Coke
position. The pattern recurs deliberately: an old personal thread resurfacing
as a large, late investment decision, not a coincidence he downplays.

## The strategy he had to be argued out of

His own starting method, and the verdict on it:

> "I call this the "cigar butt" approach to investing." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

> "Unless you are a liquidator, that kind of approach to buying businesses is
> foolish." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

See [[People/CharlieMunger]] for who did the arguing, and
[[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]] for where it led.

## Hiring philosophy: age as a non-factor

> "An observer might conclude from our hiring practices that Charlie and I were
> traumatized early in life by an EEOC bulletin on age discrimination. The real
> explanation, however, is self-interest: It's difficult to teach a new dog old
> tricks." [[Sources/1996ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

The line recurs nearly verbatim five years later — "It's hard to teach a new dog
old tricks" [[Sources/2002ltr]] — a rare case of a joke repeated exactly because
the underlying policy never changed.

## Succession

> "At 94, it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me as CEO and will be writing
> the annual letters. Greg shares the Berkshire creed that a "report" is what a
> Berkshire CEO annually owes to owners. And he also understands that if you start
> fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling
> yourself as well." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

Abel enters the letters two decades earlier as a utility manager — see
[[Cases/BerkshireHathawayEnergy]].
