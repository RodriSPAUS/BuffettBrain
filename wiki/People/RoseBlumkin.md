# Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B")

Founder of the [[Cases/NebraskaFurnitureMart]] and Buffett's standing illustration
of the low-cost operator as an unassailable competitive advantage. Berkshire
bought 90% of her business in 1983; her page here is grounded almost entirely in
that year's letter, where Buffett tells her story at length.

## From a border crossing to $100 million out of one store

Buffett recounts a biography that reads like folklore because it happened:

> "About 67 years ago Mrs. Blumkin, then 23, talked her way past a border guard to leave Russia for America." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

> "In 1937, after many years of selling used clothing, Mrs. Blumkin had saved $500 with which to realize her dream of opening a furniture store." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

When creditors pressed early on, she paid them as promised by a method "not taught
at business schools":

> "she simply sold the furniture and appliances from her home in order to pay creditors precisely as promised." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

By 1983 that $500 store had become the largest home-furnishings operation in the
country: "over $100 million of sales annually out of one 200,000 square-foot
store," selling more furniture, carpets and appliances by itself than all Omaha
competitors combined. [[Sources/1983ltr]]

## The moat is the expense ratio

Mrs. B is the human face of the [[Concepts/Moat]] Buffett most trusts — a cost
advantage so wide that no amount of capital would let a competitor close it. His
test for a business is whether he would want to compete against it, and the answer
here is the letter's most-quoted line:

> "I'd rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny. They buy brilliantly, they operate at expense ratios competitors don't even dream about, and they then pass on to their customers much of the savings." [[Sources/1983ltr]] [[Concepts/Moat]]

He names the result "the ideal business — one built upon exceptional value to the
customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners."
[[Sources/1983ltr]] It is the same structure Buffett prizes at
[[Cases/GEICO]]: low costs permit low prices, and low prices compound market
share.

## The owner who would not leave the floor

Mrs. B sold for "far-sighted family reasons" but kept working — at 90 she remained
chairman and was "on the sales floor seven days a week," carpet sales her
specialty. [[Sources/1983ltr]] She is Buffett's archetype of the
[[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] manager who stays because the business is the
life, not the payout, and of the age-blind hiring he defends across the letters.
