# Dempster Mill Manufacturing Co.

The second signature control situation of the Buffett Partnership years, after
[[Cases/SanbornMap]] — and the one that shows what a control *becomes* once the
activist has control. Where Sanborn was a valuation gap engineered into a
distribution, Dempster was a business bought cheap, actively worked, and
converted into cash "at virtually 100 cents on the dollar" [[Sources/1962ltr]].
It is the largest single position of the early partnership era, and the high
point of 1962.

## The business, and why it was cheap

Dempster Mill of Beatrice, Nebraska made farm implements and water systems — a
tough industry with tired management:

> "Operations have produced only nominal profits in relation to invested capital during recent years." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

At yearend 1961 the numbers were: sales of about $9 million in 1961,
consolidated net worth (book value) of about $4.5 million or $75 per share, and
working capital of about $50 per share. Buffett valued his controlling interest
at $35. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

## From general to control — over five years

Dempster began as an ordinary undervalued security, and only slowly became
something more:

> "Our first stock was purchased as a generally undervalued security five years ago. A block later became available, and I went on the Board about four years ago. In August 1961, we obtained majority control, which is indicative of the fact that many of our operations are not exactly of the 'overnight' variety." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

The position: 70% owned, another 10% held by associates, and only some 150 other
stockholders — so with virtually no market for the stock, "our own actions in
such a market could drastically affect the quoted price." Our own actions in
such a market is precisely the point of a control situation: the price is no
longer set by Mr. Market but by the controller's own estimate.

## The valuation rule — fair to both sides of the same door

Because partners transact *through* this price — new partners buy in on it and
old partners sell out on it — the estimate carries a fiduciary burden:

> "The estimated value should not be what we hope it would be worth, or what it might be worth to an eager buyer, etc., but what I would estimate our interest would bring if sold under current conditions in a reasonably short period of time." [[Sources/1961ltr]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

> "This is of particular importance since, in effect, new partners are buying in based upon this price, and old partners are selling a portion of their interest based upon the same price." [[Sources/1961ltr]]

Buffett's own arithmetic for the estimate is a liquidation-style margin: 40% off
inventories, 15% off receivables, auction value of fixed assets — the discounts
built into the $35 figure carry the same conservatism he demands of any
purchase. [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

## The conversion — the high point of 1962

Between the two letters the balance sheet does the work that waiting could not:

> "The successful conversion of substantial portions of the assets of Dempster to cash, at virtually 100 cents on the dollar, has been the high point of 1962." [[Sources/1962ltr]]

Concretely, in under a year: inventory of $4.2 million at last yearend heading
to about $1.9 million, cutting the inventory discount by about $920,000; and the
liabilities-ledger turned upside down — $166,000 of cash against $2,315,000 of
liabilities at November 30, 1961 became about $1 million in cash and investments
against $250,000 of liabilities. Applying the same discounts, yearend value
rose from $35 to at least $50 a share. In the October 31 report the
partnership's 22.3-point advantage over the Dow was about 40% Dempster-driven.

## Why a control situation, and why it is rare

Dempster is the concrete answer to the question Buffett poses in the 1961
letter — size hurts passive investing but *helps* control situations, because
"the opportunities increase in this field as the funds increase."
[[Sources/1961ltr]] And control situations plus work-outs are what insulate the
portfolio in a bull market he distrusts. But he is equally explicit that such
results are the exception, not the business:

> "I want all partners and prospective partners to realize the results described above are distinctly abnormal and will recur infrequently, if at all." [[Sources/1962ltr]]

## Harry Bottle — the operator who made the conversion real

The 1962 numbers above describe *what* changed; they do not say who did it.
Buffett hired an outside operator within a week of first meeting him:

> "On April 17, 1962 I met Harry in Los Angeles, presented a deal which provided for rewards to him based upon our objectives being met, and on April 23rd he was sitting in the president's chair in Beatrice." [[Sources/1963ltr]] [[People/HarryBottle]]

The verdict, a year later, is unqualified:

> "Harry is unquestionably the man of the year." [[Sources/1963ltr]]

Nine specific fixes are itemized in the 1964 letter's appendix — inventory
cut from over $4 million to under $1 million, administration expense halved,
five unprofitable branches closed — the concrete mechanism behind the
discount-to-liquidation numbers turning real. [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

## How it ends — sold on a going-concern basis, 1963

> "A satisfactory sale on a going concern basis of Dempster Mill Manufacturing operating assets was made about a month ago." [[Sources/1963ltr]]

The company, renamed First Beatrice Corp., was left holding mostly cash and
the securities portfolio built up during the conversion — a realization of
about $80 per share against an average purchase cost of about $28. Buffett
draws two morals from the whole episode: that the business rewards patience
("it is to our advantage to have securities do nothing price wise for months,
or perhaps years"), and that current holdings are never discussed publicly,
even with partners, while a position is open. [[Sources/1964ltr]]

For the partnership-era arc, contrast Sanborn (the market pricing securities
below their own value) with Dempster (an operating business converted to cash
through ownership) — and see [[Cases/BerkshireHathaway]] for the control
situation that outlasted the partnership itself, where no outside operator
like Harry Bottle was ever needed.
