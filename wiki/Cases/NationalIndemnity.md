# National Indemnity Company

Acquired through [[Cases/BerkshireHathaway]] in 1967, alongside National Fire
& Marine Insurance Company — the acquisition that eventually turns Berkshire
from a struggling textile mill into an insurance-funded holding company,
though that transformation is a later chapter than this corpus reaches. In
the partnership era itself, National Indemnity is simply one of two
"gratifying" 1967 acquisitions, run by the man who sold it.

## The acquisition, named without ceremony

> "Through our two controlled companies (Diversified Retailing and Berkshire Hathaway), we acquired two new enterprises in 1967." [[Sources/1968ltr]]

The other was Associated Cotton Shops, run by Ben Rosner under Diversified
Retailing (see [[Cases/HochschildKohn]]'s sibling company). Both deals are
graded identically, and highly:

> "These acquisitions couldn't be more gratifying. Everything was as advertised or better." [[Sources/1968ltr]]

## Jack Ringwalt — the seller who stayed to run it

Buffett's pattern with earned businesses — let the person who built it keep
running it — holds here as it did nowhere in the pure asset-conversion cases
like [[Cases/DempsterMill]]:

> "The principal selling executives, Ben Rosner and Jack Ringwalt, have continued to do a superb job (the only kind they know), and in every respect have far more than lived up to their end of the bargain." [[Sources/1968ltr]] [[People/JackRingwalt]]

> "Those with primary responsibility for their operations, Ken Chace at Berkshire Hathaway, Louis Kohn at Hochschild Kohn, Jack Ringwalt at National Indemnity and Ben Rosner at Associated Cotton Shops, continue to meld effort and ability into results." [[Sources/1968ltr]]

## The numbers — beating Fortune 500 manufacturers at their own game

By the January 1969 letter, National Indemnity and Associated Cotton Shops
are singled out for a return on capital most large American industrials
could not match:

> "Both of these companies earned about 20% on capital employed in their businesses." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

> "Among Fortune's '500' (the largest manufacturing entities in the country, starting with General Motors), only 37 companies achieved this figure in 1967, and our boys outshone such mildly better-known (but not better appreciated) companies as IBM, General Electric, General Motors, Procter & Gamble, DuPont, Control Data, Hewlett-Packard, etc..." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

## What it becomes inside Berkshire

By the partnership's dissolution, National Indemnity (with National Fire &
Marine) is described as one of the two genuinely strong legs of Berkshire
Hathaway's balance sheet, alongside the Rockford, Illinois bank — outshining
the original textile business the whole position had started as:

> "The insurance operation (of which B-H owns virtually 100%) and the bank (where B-H owns 97.7%) present a much happier picture." [[Sources/1969ltr]] [[Cases/BerkshireHathaway]]
