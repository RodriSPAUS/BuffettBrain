# NetJets (Executive Jet Aviation)

Berkshire's private-aviation business, and the case most often mistaken for an
aircraft-leasing investment — it is not one. Acquired in 1998 for $725 million,
"paying equal amounts of cash and stock". [[Sources/1998ltr]]

## The business is fractional ownership, not leasing

Executive Jet Aviation's model is "selling fractional shares of jets and operating
the fleet for its many owners" [[Sources/1998ltr]] — a customer buys, say, 1/8 of a
jet (100 flying hours a year), pays a monthly management fee plus an hourly rate,
and can summon a plane at a few hours' notice. Rich Santulli, EJA's CEO, "created
the fractional ownership industry in 1986". [[Sources/1998ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]

The only element of leasing is operational and internal: of its 163 aircraft, EJA
kept "23 'core' aircraft that are owned or leased by EJA itself, so that it can make
sure that service is first-class even during the times when demand is heaviest".
[[Sources/1998ltr]] Berkshire arrienda esos aviones *para su propia flota* — it is
not, and never was, in the business of leasing aircraft to third parties.

## How Buffett came to it

He was a customer before he was the owner: on a recommendation from Frank Rooney
(his manager at H.H. Brown), Santulli sold him a quarter-share of a Hawker 1000, and
Buffett's family flew 900 hours before he ever thought of buying the company. Its
pilots are trained by [[Cases/FlightSafety]], another Berkshire aviation subsidiary,
which is what let Buffett sell the corporate plane and fly NetJets instead.
