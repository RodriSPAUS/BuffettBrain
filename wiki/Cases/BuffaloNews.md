# Buffalo News

Berkshire's Buffalo newspaper. The corporate name was Buffalo Evening News, Inc.;
the paper became the Buffalo News when a morning edition launched in 1982
[[Sources/1983ltr]]. Read across the full corpus, the News is the single
clearest case study in these letters of a real franchise — measured, defended,
watched erode, and finally conceded to be beyond what even excellent management
can save.

## Penetration as the measure of a franchise

The 1983 letter is where Buffett states how a newspaper's moat is measured, rather
than asserting one exists:

> "We believe a paper's penetration ratio to be the best measure of the strength of
> its franchise. Papers with unusually high penetration in the geographical area
> that is of prime interest to major local retailers, and with relatively little
> circulation elsewhere, are exceptionally efficient buys for those retailers.
> Low-penetration papers have a far less compelling message to present to
> advertisers." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

For the six months to September 1983 the News ranked first in weekday penetration
among the 100 largest US papers, and third on Sundays. Sunday circulation had risen
from 270,000 in 1976, when the Courier-Express was the city's Sunday paper, to
376,000 [[Sources/1983ltr]].

The Sunday edition itself nearly didn't survive: a 1977 competitor lawsuit almost
killed it before a 3-0 appellate reversal 17 months later cleared the way — the
origin of Buffett's decades-long partnership with publisher Stan Lipsey, who
retired at the end of 2012:

> "It's no exaggeration for me to say that the News might now be extinct
> were it not for Stan." [[Sources/2012ltr]]

## Three causes, one of them not to Berkshire's credit

Buffett attributes the acceptance to Buffalo's low population turnover — "A stable
population is more interested and involved in the activities of its community than
is a shifting population" — to editorial reputation, and to a product decision that
costs money on purpose.

The news hole ran at 50% of content against an industry average in the high 30s,
giving readers over 25% more news page for page: "This news-rich mixture is by
intent. Some publishers, pushing for higher profit margins, have cut their news
holes during the past decade. We have maintained ours and will continue to do so."
[[Sources/1983ltr]]

That is the clearest instance in the early letters of spending margin to widen a
moat. See [[Concepts/Moat]].

## The economics, reported honestly

1983 profit margin somewhat exceeded the 10% after-tax target, but Buffett
attributes it to two non-recurring items — a subnormal state income tax charge from
a now-exhausted loss carry-forward, and an unanticipated drop in newsprint cost he
says will reverse [[Sources/1983ltr]].

The structural worry is the shift from run-of-press linage to preprinted inserts,
which rose from 9.3 million to 16.4 million lines in 1983. Preprints are less
profitable, more exposed to alternative delivery, and reduce the absolute space
given to news [[Sources/1983ltr]].

## 1990-91: the first crack in the franchise

By 1990, the News held earnings roughly flat (down 5%) against a newspaper
industry showing far more cyclical vulnerability than Buffett had expected — a
trend he attributes to eroding industry-wide pricing power rather than to a
one-year blip. [[Sources/1990ltr]]

The following year's letter turns that observation into the collection's central
essay on what a franchise actually is, using newspapers as the worked example of
one dying in real time:

> "The fact is that newspaper, television, and magazine properties have begun to
> resemble businesses more than franchises in their economic behavior."
> [[Sources/1991ltr]] [[Synthesis/MoatEvolution]]

> "The economic strength of once-mighty media enterprises continues to erode as
> retailing patterns change and advertising and entertainment choices
> proliferate." [[Sources/1991ltr]]

## 1995-96: the decline named, then briefly reversed

In 1995, Buffalo News earnings decline again, grouped with the shoe group and
World Book as one of three units reporting bad news honestly rather than
papering over it:

> Munger's rule — "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of
> itself" — is cited to explain the Buffalo News losing "another notch" of the
> franchise strength first flagged in 1991. [[Sources/1995ltr]] [[People/CharlieMunger]]

The following year, earnings recovered along with the shoe group and World Book —
a reminder that the decline was not monotonic even as the underlying trend held.
[[Sources/1996ltr]]

## 2006: the verdict, once the trend could no longer be denied

Fifteen years after the franchise-erosion essay, and with Stan Lipsey and Murray
Light still running the paper, Buffett delivers the case's final judgment — the
letters' clearest statement that management quality has a ceiling when the
underlying business model is the thing failing:

> "When an industry's underlying economics are crumbling, talented management
> may slow the rate of decline. Eventually, though, eroding fundamentals will
> overwhelm managerial brilliance." [[Sources/2006ltr]] [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]]
