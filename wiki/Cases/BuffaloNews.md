Berkshire's Buffalo newspaper. The corporate name was Buffalo Evening News, Inc.;
the paper became the Buffalo News when a morning edition launched in 1982
[[Sources/1983ltr]].

## Penetration as the measure of a franchise

The 1983 letter is where Buffett states how a newspaper's moat is measured, rather
than asserting one exists:

> "We believe a paper's penetration ratio to be the best measure of the strength of
> its franchise. Papers with unusually high penetration in the geographical area
> that is of prime interest to major local retailers, and with relatively little
> circulation elsewhere, are exceptionally efficient buys for those retailers.
> Low-penetration papers have a far less compelling message to present to
> advertisers." [[Sources/1983ltr]]

For the six months to September 1983 the News ranked first in weekday penetration
among the 100 largest US papers, and third on Sundays. Sunday circulation had risen
from 270,000 in 1976, when the Courier-Express was the city's Sunday paper, to
376,000 [[Sources/1983ltr]].

## Three causes, one of them not to Berkshire's credit

Buffett attributes the acceptance to Buffalo's low population turnover — "A stable
population is more interested and involved in the activities of its community than
is a shifting population" — to editorial reputation, and to a product decision that
costs money on purpose.

The news hole ran at 50% of content against an industry average in the high 30s,
giving readers over 25% more news page for page: "This news-rich mixture is by
intent. Some publishers, pushing for higher profit margins, have cut their news
holes during the past decade. We have maintained ours and will continue to do so."
[[Sources/1983ltr]]

That is the clearest instance in the early letters of spending margin to widen a
moat. See [[Concepts/Moat]].

## The economics, reported honestly

1983 profit margin somewhat exceeded the 10% after-tax target, but Buffett
attributes it to two non-recurring items — a subnormal state income tax charge from
a now-exhausted loss carry-forward, and an unanticipated drop in newsprint cost he
says will reverse [[Sources/1983ltr]].

The structural worry is the shift from run-of-press linage to preprinted inserts,
which rose from 9.3 million to 16.4 million lines in 1983. Preprints are less
profitable, more exposed to alternative delivery, and reduce the absolute space
given to news [[Sources/1983ltr]].
