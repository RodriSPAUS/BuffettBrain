## Why this case matters

BHE is the counter-example to almost everything else in this wiki. Berkshire's
best-known businesses are the ones that need little capital and throw off cash —
see [[Cases/SeeCandies]]. BHE is the opposite: enormous, capital-hungry, and
regulated, and it has never returned a cent to the parent. It exists in the
letters to show that a *low* return earned with certainty on a very large and
growing capital base is worth owning, which is a distinct branch of
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]].

## A category of its own

Buffett separates the regulated businesses from everything else in the letter and
in the accounts:

> "Regulated, Capital-Intensive Businesses We have two major operations, BNSF and
> Berkshire Hathaway Energy ("BHE"), that share important characteristics
> distinguishing them from our other businesses." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

> "A key characteristic of both companies is their huge investment in very
> long-lived, regulated assets, with these partially funded by large amounts of
> long-term debt that is not guaranteed by Berkshire." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

> "A century hence, BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy will still be playing vital
> roles in our economy." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

## Acquired, then compounded

Berkshire entered in 1999–2000, when the business was MidAmerican Energy:

> "We made important progress in this respect during 1999 by acquiring Jordan's
> Furniture and contracting to buy a major portion of MidAmerican Energy."
> [[Sources/1999ltr]]

> "When Berkshire entered the utility business in 2000, purchasing 76% of BHE, the
> company's residential customers in Iowa paid an average of 8.8 cents per ki low
> att-hour (kWh)." [[Sources/2019ltr]]

By 2014 it was one of the five businesses driving Berkshire's non-insurance
earnings:

> "Of the five, only Berkshire Hathaway Energy, then earning $393 million, was
> owned by us a decade ago." [[Sources/2014ltr]]

> "Our BNSF railroad and Berkshire Hathaway Energy ("BHE") – the two lead dogs of
> Berkshire's non-insurance group – earned a combined $8.3 billion in 2019
> (including only our 91% share of BHE), an increase of 6% from2018."
> [[Sources/2019ltr]]

## The reinvestment machine

The defining fact of the case, and the reason it belongs next to
[[Concepts/CapitalAllocation]] rather than [[Concepts/Moat]]:

> "(BHE has never paid a dividend to Berkshire since we assumed ownership. No
> investor-owned utility in America comes close to matching BHE's enthusiasm for
> reinvestment.)" [[Sources/2015ltr]]

The regulatory bargain that makes this work runs in both directions:

> "Those outstanding performances explain why BHE is welcomed by regulators when
> it proposes to buy a utility in their jurisdiction. The regulators know the
> company will run an efficient, safe and reliable operation and also arrive with
> unlimited capital to fund whatever projects make sense." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

Low prices are what buys the goodwill:

> "In Iowa, BHE's average retail rate is 6.8¢ per KWH." [[Sources/2015ltr]]

> "In contrast, here's what is happeningat the other large investor-owned Iowa
> utility: Last year, the rates it charged its residential customers were 61%
> higher than BHE's." [[Sources/2019ltr]]

> "New wind projects that are underway will take that figure to 89% by 2020."
> [[Sources/2016ltr]]

## Management

BHE is also where Greg Abel enters the letters, two decades before his
designation as successor:

> "Dave Sokol, MEHC's CEO, and Greg Abel, his key associate, are huge assets for
> Berkshire." [[Sources/2002ltr]]

> "Berkshire has three partners, who own the remaining 19.5%: Dave Sokol and Greg
> Abel, the brilliant managers of the business, and Walter Scott, a long-time
> friend of mine who introduced me to the company." [[Sources/2003ltr]]

See [[People/WarrenBuffett]] on succession and [[Concepts/ManagementQuality]].

## What the case establishes

- A regulated return is a *low* return accepted in exchange for the right to
  deploy very large amounts of capital for a very long time. That is a different
  proposition from a moat, and Buffett files it separately.
- Retaining every dollar of earnings is the correct policy when the business can
  reinvest at an acceptable regulated rate — the mirror image of the See's logic.
- Being the low-cost provider buys regulatory permission to keep growing, which
  is the closest thing to a competitive advantage a utility has.
