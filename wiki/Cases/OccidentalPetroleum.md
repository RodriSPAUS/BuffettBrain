# Occidental Petroleum

## Why this case matters

Occidental is Buffett's central real-world illustration, at the 2022 annual
meeting, of how speculative the stock market had become — not through a
metaphor but through the mechanics of how Berkshire was able to buy roughly
14% of a decades-old company in about two weeks. See [[Concepts/MrMarket]]
for the fuller argument this case is used to support.

## Built to 14% in Two Weeks

Buffett explains that four large passive holders — index funds tracking
BlackRock, State Street and Vanguard products, plus Dodge & Cox — together
held about 40% of Occidental's shares and simply did not trade:

> "So they own 40% of the company roughly, those 4 firms, and they didn't do
> anything during this period." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

With that block effectively locked away, the remaining float churned rapidly
enough among short-term traders that Berkshire's manager could accumulate a
large stake almost immediately once Buffett decided, after reading an analyst
presentation over a weekend, that Occidental was attractively priced:

> "And in 2 weeks, he buys 14% out of 60%." [[Sources/Conference_call_2022]]

Buffett calls the speed itself the real evidence of a "casino" market — a
company that had existed for decades changing hands at that pace, he argues,
"is not investment."

## Prior History: The Preferred Stock

The 2022 conversation also revisits Berkshire's earlier $10 billion Occidental
preferred stock and warrants, acquired to help fund the 2019 Anadarko
acquisition; by the end of the first quarter of 2020 that stake had been
marked down to about $5.5 billion, a $4.5 billion unrealized loss, before oil
prices recovered.

## What the case establishes

- A wide gap between a company's real long-run economics and its short-term
  trading behavior can persist even in a large, well-known stock — the same
  point [[Concepts/MrMarket]] makes about price versus value generally.
- Index-fund and institutional lock-up of a large share of a company's float
  can make the remaining shares behave more speculatively, not less, because
  a smaller pool absorbs the same trading volume.
