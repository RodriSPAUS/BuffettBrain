# Hochschild, Kohn & Co.

The partnership's first whole-business purchase by direct negotiation — and,
in hindsight, the case Buffett himself cites two decades later as the lesson
that changed how he bought companies. Bought in 1966 through Diversified
Retailing Company (BPL's other controlled holding company, alongside
[[Cases/BerkshireHathaway]]), sold in 1969 for close to what was paid for it.

## The purchase — the method's first negotiated deal

> "During the first half we, and two 10% partners, purchased all of the stock of Hochschild, Kohn & Co., a privately owned Baltimore department store." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Buffett is explicit that no new investment principle was involved — the same
quantitative-and-qualitative weighing applied to any marketable security —
and downplays its news value relative to the partnership's other, larger,
undisclosed holdings:

> "I still prefer the iceberg approach toward investment disclosure." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

Louis Kohn continued running the business day to day, in the same pattern
followed at [[Cases/NationalIndemnity]] and Associated Cotton Shops.

## The sale — close to book, three years later

By the December 5, 1969 letter, winding down the partnership, Diversified
Retailing sells Hochschild, Kohn entirely:

> "On December 1st, DRC sold its entire interest in H-K to Supermarkets General Corp. for $5,045,205 of cash plus non-interest bearing SGC notes for $2 million due 2-1-70, and $4,540,000 due 2-1-71." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

DRC's remaining operating business, Associated Retail Stores (formerly
Associated Cotton Shops), had "a net worth of about $7.5 million" and
continued growing sales and earnings — the healthier half of what had been a
two-store holding company.

## What Buffett says about it twenty years later

This is the case Berkshire's 1989 letter reaches for as the concrete example
of a lesson learned the hard way — not a fraud, not a disaster, just a
mediocre business bought cheap that stayed mediocre:

> "So-o-o - three years later I was lucky to sell the business for about what I had paid. After ending our corporate marriage to Hochschild Kohn, I had memories like those of the husband in the country song, 'My Wife Ran Away With My Best Friend and I Still Miss Him a Lot.'" [[Raw/1989ltr.md]]

> "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner." [[Raw/1989ltr.md]]

Hochschild, Kohn is the concrete anchor for the abstract lesson —
[[Cases/CommonwealthTrust]] traces the same "cigar-butt to wonderful
business" thread from the other end, the partnership-era bargain-buying
method this deal exemplifies.
