# Commonwealth Trust Co.

The partnership's first fully-worked "typical situation" — the position
described unnamed in 1957 as "between 10% and 20% of the portfolio" [[Sources/1957ltr]],
which [[Sources/1958ltr]] names and closes out a year later. It is the case Buffett chooses, in only his
second annual letter, to teach the whole method: illiquidity as an ally, patience
as a source of edge, and a sale made not because the thesis broke but because a
better one appeared. It is also, in this wiki, an honest dead end: **Commonwealth
is never mentioned again in any later partnership letter** (1959–1970 checked) —
no report of the eventual merger, no verdict on selling early.

## The setup: cheap, protected, and going nowhere

Commonwealth Trust of Union City, New Jersey had about $50 million in assets —
"about half the size of the First National in Omaha" — and was a well-run bank
earning about $10 per share while paying no dividend at all "for good reasons,"
which held the price down to about $50 against an intrinsic value Buffett put at
$125:

> "So here we had a very well managed bank with substantia1 earnings power selling at a large discount from intrinsic value. Management was friendly to us as new stockholders and risk of any ultimate loss seemed minimal." [[Sources/1958ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]
>
> (the "substantia1" is `Raw/1958ltr.md`'s own extraction artifact, quoted verbatim)

The catalyst waiting in the wings was ownership structure, not operations: a
larger bank already owned 25.5% of Commonwealth and "had desired a merger for
many years," blocked only "for personal reasons" — evidence, in Buffett's
reading, that the value would eventually be unlocked, "although it might be one
year or ten years."

## Illiquidity as a feature, not a risk

Commonwealth had only about 300 stockholders and traded perhaps twice a month.
Buffett accumulated roughly 12% of the bank over about a year at an average
price near $51 — becoming the second-largest stockholder — precisely because a
market that thin barely moves on his own buying:

> "Commonwealth only had about 300 stockholders and probably averaged two trades or so per month, so you can understand why I say that the activity of the stock market generally had very little effect on the price movement of some of our holdings." [[Sources/1958ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

Even so, a competing buyer briefly moved the price to about $65 — "very small
buying orders can create price changes of this magnitude in an inactive stock" —
which is the reason he gives for guarding against "leakage" about the
portfolio's holdings.

## The sale, and the reasoning behind it

Late in 1958 Buffett sold the entire block at $80 per share — "although the
quoted market was about 20% lower at the time" — to fund a new, larger position
([[Cases/SanbornMap]], though unnamed at this point in the text) where he could
become the largest holder. He is explicit that the sale was not a verdict on
Commonwealth's quality:

> "I might mention that the buyer of the stock at $80 can expect to do quite well over the years. However, the relative undervaluation at $80 with an intrinsic value $135 is quite different from a price $50 with an intrinsic value of $125, and it seemed to me that our capital could better be employed in the situation which replaced it." [[Sources/1958ltr]]

That is a pure opportunity-cost decision, not a change of mind about the
business: capital moved toward the higher-conviction idea, not away from a
failing one.

## What the letters do not say

Three things a reader might reasonably want are simply absent from the corpus
as ingested:

- Whether the merger with the 25.5%-owner bank ever happened, at what price, or
  in what year.
- Whether the eventual buyer at $80 "did quite well," as predicted.
- Any later reference to Commonwealth at all, favorable or not — no
  self-grading, positive or negative, of the decision to sell in 1958.

This is worth stating plainly rather than filling the gap with plausible
narrative: Commonwealth Trust is a closed case in this wiki, resolved entirely
within a single letter.
