# Sanborn Map Co.

The signature investment of Buffett's partnership years and one of the canonical
value-investing case studies — introduced as an unnamed 25% position in
[[Sources/1958ltr]], grown to 35% of assets in [[Sources/1959ltr]], and told in full
in [[Sources/1960ltr]]. It is the [[People/BenGraham]] method at its purest: a stock
priced so far below the value of the cash and securities *inside* the company that
the operating business came free.

## The business

Sanborn published extremely detailed maps of U.S. cities — water-main diameters,
hydrant locations, roof materials — used chiefly by fire-insurance underwriters. For
seventy-five years it ran as a near-monopoly with recession-proof profits and no need
for a sales force. In the early 1950s a competing method called "carding" cut into
it, and after-tax map profits fell from over $500,000 in the late 1930s to under
$100,000 by 1958–59.

## The valuation gap

Since the 1930s Sanborn had accumulated an investment portfolio (about $2.5 million,
roughly half bonds and half stocks) that came to dwarf the shrinking map business.
The market saw only the decline:

> "In the tremendously more vigorous climate of 1958 the same map business was evaluated at a minus $20 with the buyer of the stock unwilling to pay more than 70 cents on the dollar for the investment portfolio with the map business thrown in for nothing." [[Sources/1960ltr]] [[Concepts/MarginOfSafety]]

The stock had fallen from $110 a share in 1938 to $45 in 1958, even as the investment
portfolio alone rose from about $20 to $65 a share — so a buyer paid a *negative* $20
for a business still holding a wealth of irreplaceable data. [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

## The control play, and the governance lesson

Sanborn is also Buffett's earliest documented governance fight. Of fourteen
directors, nine were insurance men who together held just 46 of 105,000 shares, and
the dividend had been cut five times in eight years while directors' fees were left
alone — the misalignment that [[Principles/OwnershipMindset]] and
[[Concepts/ManagementQuality]] later make central. He bought a resigning family's
block from the founder's widow, built the position (with associates) to about 24,000
shares, and — rather than a proxy fight he expected to win — engineered an
SEC-approved plan under which about 72% of the stock was exchanged at fair value for
the portfolio securities, separating the two businesses and eliminating a large
corporate capital-gains tax.

## Why it mattered, and why it was rare

Buffett is explicit that control situations like this are the exception:

> "Our bread-and-butter business is buying undervalued securities and selling when the undervaluation is corrected along with investment in special situations where the profit is dependent on corporate rather than market action." [[Sources/1960ltr]]

The 1961 letter does not forget it either: Sanborn is named in the argument
that bigger funds help control situations precisely *because* such a play needs
capital — "A 'Sanborn Map' cannot be accomplished without the wherewithal."
[[Sources/1961ltr]] The same logic, pursued by buying and working a whole
company, is the subject of [[Cases/DempsterMill]].

Sanborn is the concrete root of ideas the later Berkshire letters state in the
abstract — margin of safety as downside protection, and the willingness to act as an
owner rather than a spectator. See [[Synthesis/MindsetEvolution]] for how far this
starting point is from the "wonderful businesses" era.
