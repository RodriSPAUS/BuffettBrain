# Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (the partnership-era origin)

Not the Berkshire this wiki is otherwise about — this page is the entity's
first eight years as a Buffett Partnership, Ltd. holding: a controlling
position bought quietly in a dying textile business, valued for years as a
liquidation-style bargain, and left, at the partnership's dissolution, as one
of two companies (with [[Cases/HochschildKohn]]'s sibling Diversified
Retailing) that partners could keep instead of taking cash. The Berkshire
that becomes a $1 trillion holding company is a later chapter this page does
not tell; see [[Synthesis/MindsetEvolution]] for that arc.

## Bought as a "general," at $7.60

Purchases began in 1962, inside the "Generals-Private Owner" category, on the
same logic as any other statistically cheap position:

> "Our purchases of Berkshire started at a price of $7.60 per share in 1962." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

The price reflected real decline, not mispricing alone. Postwar, the company
had slid a long way from its own peak:

> "In the postwar period the company had slid downhill a considerable distance, having hit a peak in 1948 when about $29 1/2 million was earned before tax and about 11,000 workers were employed." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

By the time control was assumed in spring 1965 — average cost $14.86,
reflecting heavy buying that year — the business had shrunk to two mills:

> "At the time we acquired control in spring of 1965, Berkshire was down to two mills and about 2,300 employees." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

## Held secret, then revealed in two steps

The mid-year 1965 letter reports a controlling interest acquired but declines
to name it:

> "During the first half, a series of purchases resulted in the acquisition of a controlling interest in one of the situations described in the 'General Private Owner' section of the last annual letter." [[Sources/1965ltr]]

The November 1, 1965 letter to partners names it directly, and fixes the
valuation rule in the same sentence — a rule, not a hope, matching the
fiduciary standard already set at [[Cases/DempsterMill]]:

> "The partnership owns a controlling interest in Berkshire Hathaway Inc., a publicly-traded security." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

> "We will value our position in Berkshire Hathaway at yearend at a price halfway between net current asset value and book value." [[Sources/1965ltr]] [[Principles/Fidelity]]

## A pleasant surprise: the people were already good

Unlike Dempster, no outside operator had to be brought in. Buffett credits
the remaining management directly:

> "It was a very pleasant surprise to find that the remaining units had excellent management personnel, and we have not had to bring a single man from the outside into the operation." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

> "There is no question that the state of the textile industry is the dominant factor in determining the earning power of the business, but we are most fortunate to have Ken Chace running the business in a first-class manner, and we also have several of the best sales people in the business heading up this end of their respective divisions." [[Sources/1966ltr]] [[People/KenChace]]

> "Berkshire is a delight to own." [[Sources/1966ltr]]

## Valued as a business, priced as a stock, and never the two confused

The gap between carried value and the quoted market price is treated as
irrelevant by design:

> "We valued B-H at 25 at yearend 1967 when the market was about 20 and 31 at yearend 1968 when the market was about 37." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

> "We would have done the same thing if the markets had been 15 and 50 respectively. ('Price is what you pay. value is what you get')." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

## From a textile company to a holding company, before the partnership even closes

By the December 5, 1969 letter — written to inform departing partners what
they would actually own if they kept their B-H stock — Berkshire has already
become something structurally different from the position bought in 1962. It
now holds three main businesses: the original textile operation, National
Indemnity Company and National Fire & Marine Insurance Company (acquired
1967, see [[Cases/NationalIndemnity]]), and a Rockford, Illinois bank:

> "B-H has three main operating businesses, the textile operation, the insurance operation (conducted by National Indemnity Company and National Fire & Marine Insurance Company, which will be collectively called the insurance company) and the Illinois National Bank and Trust Company of Rockford, Illinois." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

The textile business itself, the reason the whole position existed, is by
now graded honestly as the weak link:

> "The textile operation presently employs about $16 per share in capital and, while I think it has made some progress relative to the textile industry generally, cannot be judged a satisfactory business." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

While the newer, unrelated businesses carry the balance sheet:

> "The insurance operation (of which B-H owns virtually 100%) and the bank (where B-H owns 97.7%) present a much happier picture." [[Sources/1969ltr]]

> "Adding in the textile business and miscellaneous assets, and subtracting parent company bank debt of about $7 million, gives a tangible net asset value of about $43 per share for B-H, or about $45 stated book value, allowing for the premium over tangible assets paid for the bank." [[Sources/1969ltr]] [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]]

Buffett's own plan, stated to departing partners choosing between cash and
stock, is to keep buying:

> "All I can say is that I'm going to do so and I plan to buy more." [[Sources/1969ltr]]
