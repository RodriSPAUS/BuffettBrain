## Definition

Reported earnings are an accounting output; owner earnings are what an owner can
actually take out of a business without impairing it. The gap between the two is
the reason Buffett distrusts a single year's return-on-equity figure.

Two distinct corrections make up the idea in the early letters.

## Correction 1 — earnings the owner never sees reported

A shareholder owning under 20% of a company records only the dividends received,
not the share of earnings retained on their behalf. Buffett argues the retained
portion is no less real: "The value to Berkshire Hathaway of retained earnings is
not determined by whether we own 100%, 50%, 20% or 1% of the businesses in which
they reside. Rather, the value of those retained earnings is determined by the use
to which they are put and the subsequent level of earnings produced by that
usage." [[Sources/1980ltr]]

By 1981 this unrecorded amount had passed reported operating earnings — GEICO,
General Foods, R. J. Reynolds and The Washington Post alone were expected to
contribute over $35 million of it in 1982. Buffett's conclusion about the metric
is explicit: the accounting rules that ignore these earnings "diminish the utility
of our annual return on equity calculation, or any other single year measure of
economic performance." [[Sources/1981ltr]]

The translation is real but not punctual: "While market values track business
values quite well over long periods, in any given year the relationship can gyrate
capriciously." [[Sources/1981ltr]]

## Correction 2 — capital expenditure the income statement omits

The complementary error runs the other way: reported earnings can overstate what
an owner may withdraw, because some capital spending is mandatory just to stand
still. The 1981 letter states the mechanism before the term existed — "inflation
acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its
requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host
organism. Whatever the level of reported profits (even if nil), more dollars for
receivables, inventory and fixed assets are continuously required by the business
in order to merely match the unit volume of the previous year." [[Sources/1981ltr]]

Under those conditions a business earning 8% or 10% on equity "often has no
leftovers for expansion, debt reduction or "real" dividends" [[Sources/1981ltr]].

The Scott Fetzer purchase in [[Sources/1985ltr]] and its accounting treatment in
[[Sources/1986ltr]] are where this becomes a worked calculation.

## Contrasts & nuances

- Owner earnings is a *judgment*, not a line item: the maintenance-capex component
  cannot be read off the accounts.
- The two corrections can offset. A holding company with high-return investees may
  understate earnings; a capital-hungry manufacturer overstates them.
- Related but distinct from [[Concepts/IntrinsicValue]], which discounts owner
  earnings rather than measuring them.
