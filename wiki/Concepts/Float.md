# Float: Insurance-Based Capital for Investment

## Definition

Float is money that Berkshire holds but doesn't own. It originates in insurance operations because most policies require that premiums be prepaid and, more importantly, because it usually takes time for an insurer to hear about and resolve loss claims.

> "Float is money we hold but don't own." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

> "In an insurance operation, float arises because most policies require that premiums be prepaid and, more importantly, because it usually takes time for an insurer to hear about and resolve loss claims." [[Sources/1994ltr]]

## The Cost of Float

Float is not free money. It is borrowed money whose interest rate is the underwriting result, and Buffett insists on measuring it that way:

> "In those years when we have had an underwriting profit, such as the last five, our cost of float has been negative." [[Sources/1997ltr]]

> "In 2004 our float cost us less than nothing, and I told you that we had a chance – absent a mega-catastrophe – of no-cost float in 2005." [[Sources/2005ltr]]

Negative cost is the goal, not a given. The 1990 letter is candid about what can go wrong:

> "In addition, these companies, like all others in the industry, are vulnerable to catastrophe losses that could exceed their reinsurance protection and take their cost of float right off the chart." [[Sources/1990ltr]]

## Underwriting Discipline

Maintaining a low cost of float requires discipline in underwriting - the willingness to write less business when prices are inadequate. This discipline is part of Berkshire's competitive advantage in the insurance business.

## Float as Funding Layer

Float that costs nothing and grows behaves like permanent equity that pays no dividend. That is what makes the insurance operation the funding layer beneath everything else Berkshire owns.
