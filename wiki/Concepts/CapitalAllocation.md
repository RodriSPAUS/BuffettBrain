# Capital Allocation: The CEO's Primary Job

## The Central Challenge of Management

Capital allocation is considered the CEO's primary responsibility, yet many executives lack experience in this critical area. Most leaders rise to the top through expertise in other areas like marketing, production, or engineering, but then must make capital allocation decisions without adequate preparation.

> "This point can be important because the heads of many companies are not skilled in capital allocation. Their inadequacy is not surprising. Most bosses rise to the top because they have excelled in an area such as marketing, production, engineering, administration or, sometimes, institutional politics." [[Sources/1987ltr]]

> "They now must make capital allocation decisions, a critical job that they may have never tackled and that is not easily mastered." [[Sources/1987ltr]]

## Berkshire's Advantage Through Control

Owning businesses outright provides Berkshire with the ability to make capital allocation decisions directly, unlike passive stock investments where allocation is out of their control.

> "First, when we control a company we get to allocate capital, whereas we are likely to have little or nothing to say about this process with marketable holdings." [[Sources/1987ltr]]

## Evaluating Marginal Returns vs. Average Returns

A company's overall return on equity can mask poor allocation decisions. The key is to evaluate how the marginal dollar is deployed, not just the average return.

> "Many corporations that consistently show good returns both on equity and on overall incremental capital have, indeed, employed a large portion of their retained earnings on an economically unattractive, even disastrous, basis." [[Sources/1984ltr]]

## The Principle of Flexible Capital Deployment

Earnings need not be reinvested where they were generated. Great businesses earning high returns on tangible assets often cannot reinvest large portions of their earnings internally at attractive rates.

> "There's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it. Indeed, it's often a mistake to do so: Truly great businesses, earning huge returns on tangible assets, can't for any extended period reinvest a large portion of their earnings internally at high rates of return." [[Sources/2007ltr]]

## Two Models of Capital Utilization

Berkshire's approach varies depending on the business characteristics:

- **See's Candies Model**: High returns with limited reinvestment opportunities, generating cash that's deployed elsewhere
- **Berkshire Hathaway Energy Model**: Large capital appetite with modest but acceptable returns, retaining all earnings internally

> "(BHE has never paid a dividend to Berkshire since we assumed ownership. No investor-owned utility in America comes close to matching BHE's enthusiasm for reinvestment.)" [[Sources/2015ltr]]

## Dividend Policy Philosophy

Berkshire's dividend policy reflects their capital allocation philosophy - retaining earnings when they can be deployed at attractive rates of return.

> "On January 3, 1967, we disbursed our sole payment – $101,755 or 10¢ per A share." [[Sources/2024ltr]]

## Share Repurchases as Capital Allocation

When shares trade below intrinsic value, repurchases can be the optimal use of capital, creating more value per share for continuing shareholders.

## Long-term Perspective in Allocation

Successful capital allocation requires a long-term perspective, focusing on the business's ability to generate returns over time rather than short-term market movements.

> "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." [[Sources/1989ltr]]

## Inflation Considerations

During inflationary periods, capital allocation becomes even more critical as businesses must determine whether additional capital will generate adequate returns in real terms.
