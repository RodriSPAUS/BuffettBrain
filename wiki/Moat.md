# Moat (Economic Moat)

*A sustainable competitive advantage that protects a business from competitors and allows it to earn above-average returns over time.*

## 📚 Definition

Buffett defines a moat as the "durable competitive advantage" that enables a company to maintain pricing power, defend market share, and generate high returns on capital over decades — not just years.

> "The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to grow, but rather selecting the future winners in that industry." — [[2024ltr.md#p3]]

## ⚙️ Types of Moats

| Type | Description | Example from Letters |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Brand Moat** | Customer loyalty and pricing power derived from strong brand recognition | Coca-Cola — [[1988.md]] |
| **Cost Advantage** | Ability to produce at lower cost than competitors, enabling superior margins or pricing flexibility | GEICO — [[2024ltr.md#p4]] |
| **Network Effects** | Value increases as more users join; creates self-reinforcing adoption | American Express — [[1993.md]] |
| **Climate-Resilient Moat** | Moat reinforced by ability to withstand environmental stressors (e.g., wildfires, storms) while maintaining underwriting discipline | Berkshire P/C Insurance — [[2024ltr.md#p8-9]] |
| **Float Moat** | Competitive advantage derived from insurance float — low-cost, long-term capital for investment | Berkshire's $171B float — [[2024ltr.md#p9]] |

## 🌍 Evolution Over Time

- **1980s–1990s**: Focus on static moat durability (e.g., Coca-Cola's brand)
- **2000s–2010s**: Emphasis on management's role in *maintaining* moats amid disruption
- **2020s+**: Moats must now demonstrate *resilience* to systemic risks (climate, regulation, geopolitics) — as highlighted in [[2024ltr.md#p8-9]]
